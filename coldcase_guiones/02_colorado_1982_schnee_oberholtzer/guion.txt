After July 1982, the case of Annette Schnee and Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer settled into a pattern that every family of a cold case victim knows by heart. A burst of attention. A few leads. Then silence. Then an anniversary, a newspaper story, a hopeful phone call, and silence again. The years began to stack up. 1983. 1984. 1985. The children who had been in school when the two women disappeared grew up and had children of their own. The ski town of Breckenridge kept growing. The roads over the passes were plowed every winter, and every spring the snow melted into Sacramento Creek, and every January 6 came and went. Somewhere in those same years, a quiet revolution was taking place in laboratories far from the Colorado mountains. In the middle of the 1980s, scientists discovered that the genetic code inside human cells could be used to tell one person from another, with a precision that blood typing could never approach. At first it was experimental. Then it entered the courtroom. Then it became the most powerful tool in forensic science. And in the evidence that had been collected on Hoosier Pass in January 1982, there was exactly the kind of material this new science needed. The stain on Bobbie Jo's glove. The blood on the tissues in her backpack. The blood found near her body. In the early 1990s, that evidence was finally tested in a way that had been impossible in 1982. And the result changed the case in two ways at once. The first change was that the blood belonged to a man. Not to Bobbie Jo. To an unidentified male. The second change was that this man was not Jeff Oberholtzer. The DNA excluded him. After roughly a decade of living under suspicion, Jeff was, in the language of the lab, ruled out. Investigators moved on from him as a suspect. Prosecutors would later state it plainly: he had been a suspect in the early stages, and DNA testing had excluded him as the possible killer. Think about what that moment must have meant, and also what it could not give back. A laboratory result can clear a name in a file. It cannot erase years of looks, whispers, and doubt. Jeff Oberholtzer had lost his wife, and then lost a decade of his reputation, and the only thing that could restore it was a scientific technique that had not existed when the suspicion began. Decades later, when the cameras of 48 Hours came to tell this story, Jeff Oberholtzer was still there, still willing to talk about Bobbie Jo, still describing the last phone call word for word. He had outlived the suspicion. He had never outlived the loss. And he still did not know who had stopped for his wife on the road that night. But the same result that cleared him also created a new and stubborn problem. Investigators now had the genetic signature of the man who had almost certainly killed both women. They had his profile. What they did not have was his name. A DNA profile, on its own, is like a fingerprint with no one to compare it to. It can tell you yes or no when you hold it up against a specific person. It cannot tell you where to look. For decades, the man who left his blood on Bobbie Jo's glove remained a profile without a name. Meanwhile, the families refused to let go. And at the center of that refusal stood a man who had already spent a career chasing killers. His name was Charlie McCormick. He was a former homicide detective with the Denver Police Department. After he retired from the city, he did not retire from this case. He signed on as a private investigator for Annette Schnee's family. His fee was one dollar a year. One dollar a year. It was not a business arrangement. It was a promise. One report says he pursued the case privately for thirty-two years. While McCormick worked for the Schnee family, an investigator named Eaton stayed on the case for the Summit County Sheriff's Office. Between them, and the Park County Sheriff's Office on the other side of the pass, the file never fully closed. Every few years, the case surfaced in the news. Local papers in the mountains ran stories about the investigators still looking for clues. In 2015, a Colorado newspaper reported that the public was being invited to help solve what was then a thirty-three-year-old double murder. Thirty-three years. Annette would have been in her mid-fifties. Bobbie Jo would have been in her early sixties. The man who killed them was still out there, getting older too. One headline, years into the investigation, described McCormick as an eighty-one-year-old still hot on the case's trail. Another, from a Denver television station, framed the story as a chase for closure before time ran out. That phrase captures the hidden enemy of every cold case. Not just silence. Time itself. Witnesses die. Memories fade. Investigators retire. Parents grow old. Evidence can degrade or be lost. And a killer who was thirty in 1982 would be in his sixties, then his seventies, with every passing year bringing him closer to dying without ever facing a court. For the Schnee family, the waiting was long and personal. Annette's mother had given her daughter warm socks for Christmas in 1981. Year after year, she lived without knowing who had taken her daughter. Annette's brother Russell, seven years older, stayed in Siouxland. Her sister Cindy, the little girl Annette used to babysit, grew into an adult who would one day write the words that a prosecutor would read aloud in court. Here is the ledger of those cold years, in plain numbers. Six months between Annette's disappearance and the discovery of her body. Around ten years before DNA testing cleared Jeff Oberholtzer. Thirty-two years, by one count, that a retired Denver detective spent working the case for a dollar a year. Thirty-three years by the time the public was asked, once again, to help. And all that time, one DNA profile, preserved from a glove, that matched no name anyone could find. It is easy to call these the lost years. But they were not entirely lost. In those years, the evidence was kept. The glove was not thrown away. The tissues were not destroyed. The profile was not forgotten. Investigators and families kept the case alive long enough for science to catch up. Because while the case sat still in Colorado, something was changing in the wider world. Ordinary people, millions of them, were spitting into tubes and mailing their DNA to genealogy companies, hoping to learn where their great-grandparents came from or to find long-lost cousins. They were not thinking about crime. They were thinking about family. And without knowing it, some of them were building the map that would one day lead investigators to one man living quietly in the Colorado mountains. Somewhere in those vast new family trees, there were people who shared a small piece of their DNA with a killer. They had never met him, or if they had, they had no idea what he had done. The next part of this story is about how a stain on a glove and a stranger's family tree finally met.

To understand how this case was finally solved, you need to understand one idea. It is simple, and it changed everything. Every person carries DNA inherited from their parents, who inherited it from theirs, and so on, back through generations. That means that every person shares pieces of their DNA with relatives they have never met. Brothers and sisters share a lot. First cousins share less. Second and third cousins share smaller pieces still. But even a distant cousin carries a recognizable trace of the same family. For most of the history of forensic DNA, police did not use that idea. The standard forensic DNA test, the kind used in criminal databases, looks at a small set of locations in the genetic code. It is excellent for one job: comparing a crime scene sample to a specific person and saying match or no match. Think of it like a key and a lock. If you have the right key, the lock opens. If you do not have the key, the lock tells you nothing about where to find it. For almost forty years, the blood on Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer's glove was a lock without a key. The new approach works differently. It is called investigative genetic genealogy. Instead of looking at a small set of markers, scientists build a much larger genetic profile from the crime scene sample, reading many thousands of points across the genetic code. That larger profile can be compared not against criminals, but against ordinary people who have chosen to share their DNA on certain genealogy websites. Those people are not suspects. They are relatives. A third cousin here. A second cousin there. People who uploaded their DNA to find family, and whose matches can now help investigators find a family too. Here is how it works, step by step, in plain words. First, the lab extracts DNA from the old evidence and builds the detailed profile. Second, that profile is compared with the profiles in the genealogy databases that allow law enforcement searches. The database returns a list of people who share some DNA with the unknown person, along with how much they share. Third, genealogists take those distant relatives and build their family trees backward, sometimes for generations, using public records, birth and marriage certificates, obituaries, census records. They look for the place where the trees of different relatives meet, the common ancestors. Fourth, they build the trees forward again, down through the children and grandchildren of those ancestors, looking for a person who fits. Born at the right time. A man. Living in the right place at the right time. Slowly, the tree narrows from thousands of people, to dozens, to a handful, sometimes to a single family. And then the last and most important step. Genealogy alone never proves anything. It is only a lead. To be sure, investigators need a direct comparison between the suspect's own DNA and the DNA from the crime scene. That is the confirmation. Without it, there is no case. The method burst into public view in April 2018, when investigators in California announced an arrest in the case of the so-called Golden State Killer, a series of murders and rapes from the 1970s and 1980s that had gone unsolved for decades. The man arrested had been found through a genealogy database and confirmed with DNA he had left behind. Almost overnight, police departments and prosecutors across the country realized that old evidence sitting on their shelves might now be able to speak. In Colorado, one name connected to this new kind of work was a former prosecutor named Mitch Morrissey. He had served as the District Attorney in Denver. When he left office after reaching his term limit, he turned his experience into a company that specialized in genetic genealogy and cold cases. It was called United Data Connect, and it was based in Denver. Around the same time, Metro Denver Crime Stoppers, an organization best known for anonymous tip lines, was working with law enforcement to bring this new science to old cases. Now the story returns to the mountains. In 2020, the Park County Sheriff's Office began a new phase of the investigation into the murders of Annette Schnee and Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer. According to the sheriff's office, that work was built on new information developed through genetic genealogy, in partnership with Metro Crime Stoppers and United Data Connect. DNA was extracted from evidence in the case, the same evidence that had been collected and preserved since 1982. And through genealogy, that DNA was traced, not to a single name at first, but to a single family. Two brothers. That is the moment where all the years of waiting suddenly matter. If the glove had been thrown away, there would have been nothing to test. If the blood had been used up in earlier testing, there would have been nothing left for the new method. If the evidence had been stored badly and degraded beyond use, the science would have had nothing to read. Every person who handled that evidence correctly over thirty-eight years, people whose names most of us will never know, had a hand in what happened next. There is also something quietly remarkable about how the answer was found. It did not come from a confession. It did not come from a witness who finally broke their silence. It came from strangers. Somewhere, people related to the killer by blood had chosen to share their DNA in the hope of learning about their own families. They had nothing to do with the crime. They may never have met him. And yet, because of their curiosity about their own ancestors, two women who died on a snowy day in 1982 were about to get their names back into the headlines, this time next to the name of the man accused of killing them. Two brothers. That was what the genealogy delivered. It was a huge step forward. But it was not an answer yet. Two brothers means two men. And only one of them could have left his blood on that glove. This is the limit of the method, and it is worth understanding. A family tree can point to a family. It can even point to one branch of a family. But brothers come from the same parents, and to the distant cousins in a genealogy database, two brothers look almost exactly alike. The science had done everything it could do from a distance. The last mile would have to be walked by a detective. The investigators needed to know which one. They needed to know where each brother had lived, what each had been doing in January 1982, and whether either of them had ever had any connection to the mountains of Summit and Park County. And they needed to do it without tipping anyone off. Because if the wrong person found out what investigators were looking at, the right person might disappear, or destroy whatever could connect him to the crime. The case now landed on the desk of a Park County detective who would carry it the rest of the way. Her name was Wendy Kipple. And when she first looked at the two names the genealogy had produced, one of them was about to set off what she would later describe as red flags.

Detective Sergeant Wendy Kipple of the Park County Sheriff's Office is the detective who carried this case through its final stretch. By the time it reached her, it was a story that had been told so many times it had almost hardened into legend, a case of boxes, files, and families who would not give up. And then, in 2020, she was handed something no one on the case had ever held before. Two names. The genealogy had narrowed the family to two brothers. One was named Bruce Phillips. The other was named Alan Phillips. The first question was simple. Which brothers had ever been in Colorado? The answer came back quickly. Only one of them had lived in the state. That brother was Alan. Kipple pulled his criminal history. What she found did not prove anything about 1982, but it changed the way she looked at the name. She later told 48 Hours that his record showed he had been arrested in 1973 for assault and burglary. An arrest is not a conviction, and the public record does not tell us how that case ended. But to a detective looking at a man whose family's DNA pointed at a double murder, it was enough, in her words, to set off red flags. Then something even stranger happened. Kipple went back through the old records connected to the case, the paper archive that had been building since the winter of 1982. She described the moment to 48 Hours in a single, almost casual sentence. "Halfway back I see this tab, says, 'Alan Phillips.'" His name was already there. Somewhere in the old files, long before anyone had heard of genetic genealogy, there was a tab with the name of the man the DNA was now pointing toward. The public reporting does not fully explain why that file existed or what was inside it. But we do know one thing about Alan Phillips and the night of January 6, 1982. And it is time to tell it. Remember the pickup truck stuck in a snowdrift on top of Guanella Pass. Remember the headlights flashing SOS into the storm. Remember the sheriff on the airliner who saw the signal, the crew who radioed for help, and the fire chief, Dave Montoya, who drove up the mountain and brought the driver down alive. That driver was Alan Lee Phillips. He was thirty years old. He was a mechanic. And on the night that Annette Schnee and Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer were killed, he had been pulled out of the snow by the very people whose job was to keep the public safe. When he was rescued, there was something about his appearance that people remembered. His face was bruised. He had explanations. According to CBS, he said he had fallen while wandering in the snow. Another account says he claimed he had hit his face on his truck after getting out of it. And he told Montoya he had gotten drunk and decided to drive home. In January 1982, those explanations sounded reasonable enough. A man alone on a mountain in a storm, lost, drunk, slipping in the snow. Who would doubt him? But decades later, investigators came to a very different conclusion about that bruised face. They concluded that Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer had likely struck him with a brass key ring as she fought for her life. Her glove carried his blood. The injury that rescuers saw that night may have been the mark she left on him. Kipple was blunt about what had put him on that mountain in the first place. As reported by the New York Times, she said, "It was his own stupidity that got him up there, because the pass is not passable in the wintertime." Let that settle for a moment. A man who, according to the conviction that would follow, had just abducted and shot two young women, drove onto a mountain road that no sensible person drives in winter, got stuck, and signaled for help. And help came. A sheriff in the sky noticed. A fire chief on the ground went up through the storm. They saved his life. They had no way of knowing what he had done hours before. There is no suggestion in the public record that anyone who helped him that night did anything wrong. They did exactly what rescuers are supposed to do. They saw a signal and they answered it. The tragedy is not that they saved a life. The tragedy is that the life they saved belonged to the only man who knew what had happened to Annette and Bobbie Jo, and he would keep that knowledge to himself. Nobody connected the rescue on Guanella Pass with the murder on Hoosier Pass. The two events lived in separate files, separate memories, separate parts of the mountains, for almost four decades. Now, in 2020, Wendy Kipple had a name, a family, a criminal record, an old file tab, and a story about a rescue on the night of the murders. What she did not have yet was proof. Genealogy is a lead, not evidence. To make an arrest, she needed Alan Phillips's own DNA, and she needed it to match the blood on the glove. She could not simply knock on his door and ask him for a sample. If he was the killer, that would warn him. So investigators did what investigators in genealogy cases do. They watched. In the early weeks of 2021, Kipple and other investigators kept Alan Phillips under surveillance. One report says the surveillance lasted four to five weeks. By then, Phillips was seventy years old, a semi-retired mechanic living in Dumont, a small community in Clear Creek County. He was, on the surface, an ordinary older man in a mountain town. The plan was simple in theory. Wait for him to throw something away. A cup, a napkin, a bag, anything that might carry traces of his saliva. Once a person throws something into the trash, it is generally considered abandoned, and investigators can collect it. But Alan Phillips did not cooperate with the plan, because he did not seem to throw anything away. Kipple later told 48 Hours, "He wouldn't throw anything away." And then, with what sounds like a mix of frustration and disbelief, "He didn't even throw out his garbage." Imagine the patience it takes. Days of watching. Weeks. Waiting for a seventy-year-old man to discard a single item. All while knowing that if he was the man you believed he was, he had already gotten away with it for thirty-nine years. Eventually, he did discard something. Reports describe the item in slightly different ways. One account says it was a napkin. Another describes it as a bag from the fast-food chain Sonic. What matters is what was on it. Investigators collected it. The DNA was tested. And it was compared with the DNA from the blood on Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer's glove. It matched. Thirty-nine years after a twenty-nine-year-old wife fought back on a mountain pass, the stain she left on her own glove had a name. Alan Lee Phillips. Born in 1951. Thirty years old in January 1982. Seventy years old in February 2021. Annette Schnee had been twenty-one. Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer had been twenty-nine. Since that night he had lived thirty-nine more years, years that neither of them was ever given. Investigators obtained warrants. And on February 24, 2021, Wendy Kipple got the chance to do something she had been working toward for months.

February 24, 2021. Clear Creek County, Colorado. Thirty-nine years, one month, and eighteen days after Annette Schnee and Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer set out to get a ride home, Alan Lee Phillips was stopped by law enforcement in a traffic stop near his home. There was no chase. There was no standoff. According to the sheriff's office, he was taken into custody without incident. The person who put the handcuffs on him was the detective who had spent months building the case. Wendy Kipple later described that moment to 48 Hours in two short sentences. "I got to put the handcuffs on him and he was shocked." And then, "The look on his face was just priceless." It is worth thinking about what that shock meant. For thirty-nine years, Alan Phillips had lived as a free man. He had worked. He had grown old. He had lived in a mountain community not far from the passes where it all happened. And then, on an ordinary day in February 2021, a detective was standing in front of him with handcuffs. After the arrest, investigators did something that matters a great deal in a case like this. They did not rely only on the discarded item. They took a DNA sample directly from Phillips and tested it again. The result confirmed the original match. The blood on Bobbie Jo's glove was his. One week later, on March 3, 2021, the public learned what had happened. Park County Sheriff Tom McGraw stood at a news conference held at the headquarters of the Colorado Bureau of Investigation and announced an arrest in two cold case homicides dating back to 1982. The sheriff's office explained that the investigation had begun in 2020 with new information developed through genetic genealogy, in partnership with Metro Crime Stoppers and United Data Connect. The initial charges were serious and specific. Two counts of first-degree kidnapping. Two counts of first-degree assault. Two counts of first-degree homicide. One count of each for each woman. At the time of his arrest, Alan Lee Phillips was seventy years old. Annette Schnee, had she lived, would have been about sixty. Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer would have been in her late sixties. For the families, the news was overwhelming. A Denver television station summed up their reaction in a headline that quoted the families' own description of what they had lived through. Thirty-nine years of hell. That phrase is more than an expression. It is a measure of time. Thirty-nine Christmases without Annette. Thirty-nine anniversaries of a phone call that ended with the words, she had a ride. Thirty-nine years in which Jeff Oberholtzer had to wonder who had stopped for his wife. At this point in the story, it is important to use careful words. In March 2021, Alan Phillips was a man who had been arrested and charged. He had not been convicted of anything. He was presumed innocent, as every defendant is, and the case would have to be proven in court. The case moved forward. At a preliminary hearing, prosecutors presented evidence that they said linked the two murders, and a judge ordered Phillips to stand trial. The trial took place in Fairplay, the county seat of Park County, the same county where Bobbie Jo had lived, where Annette had been found in Sacramento Creek, and where the pass that separated their two stories marks the county line. In September 2022, a jury heard the case. Phillips maintained his innocence. He would later say that the DNA evidence against him had been contaminated and mishandled. When CBS's 48 Hours later asked the Phillips family and his legal team for interviews, they all declined. His attorney insisted that his client had been wrongfully convicted. By then, the outline of the evidence was publicly known. The orange socks that linked the women. The blood on Bobbie Jo's glove. The genealogy that led to the Phillips family. The discarded item and the confirmation sample. And the story of the rescue on Guanella Pass, a man with a bruised face, out alone in the storm on the same night the two women disappeared. On September 15, 2022, the jury returned its verdict. It did not take long. Reports describe roughly four to five hours of deliberation. Alan Lee Phillips was found guilty on all eight counts. For each of the two women, the jury convicted him of first-degree murder after deliberation, of two counts of first-degree felony murder, one connected to kidnapping and one connected to robbery, and of second-degree kidnapping. Four counts for Annette. Four counts for Bobbie Jo. Eight in total. For Jeff Oberholtzer, the verdict carried a meaning of its own. DNA had excluded him three decades earlier, but being excluded is not the same as having an answer. For years, the suspicion had fallen on him. Now a jury had attached another name to it, beyond a reasonable doubt. For Charlie McCormick, the retired Denver detective who had taken the Schnee family's case for one dollar a year, it was the end of a road he had walked for decades. And for the investigators of Park County, Summit County, and the Colorado Bureau of Investigation, it was the moment when a case file opened in the snow of 1982 could finally be marked with a conviction. Some of the people who had waited forty years for this day lived to see it. Annette's mother lived to see it. So did her brother, Russell, and two of her sisters. Annette's brother would afterward share with reporters what it meant to see a verdict after so long. Forty years after a mother gave her daughter orange socks for Christmas, those socks had helped a jury understand that two murders were the work of one man. On November 7, 2022, the case returned to court for sentencing. Judge Stephen Broome presided. Phillips was now seventy-one years old. Before the sentence was pronounced, Deputy District Attorney Mark Hurlbert read a statement from Annette's sister, Cindy French. In it, she described what those first six months had done to their family. She described their mother breaking down in tears during the time Annette was missing. It is a simple image, and it carries the whole weight of this story. A mother in Iowa, crying for a daughter who had not come home, for half a year, until a boy fishing in a creek gave the family an answer no family wants. Then came the sentence. Judge Broome sentenced Alan Lee Phillips to two life sentences without parole, to be served one after the other. Two consecutive life terms. One for Annette. One for Bobbie Jo. Phillips did not accept the outcome. He said he would appeal. He claimed that the DNA evidence used against him had been contaminated and mishandled. That claim would not go away when he left the courtroom. In fact, it would outlive him. Because the story of Alan Lee Phillips did not end with a sentence. Less than four months after he was sent to prison for the rest of his life, he would be dead. And a few years after that, his own family would turn the spotlight back onto the very science that had convicted him.

February 27, 2023. Arkansas Valley Correctional Facility, a state prison in the town of Ordway, on the plains of southeastern Colorado, far from the mountains. Alan Lee Phillips, seventy-one years old, died there that day. He had been in prison serving his two life sentences for less than four months, and the verdict against him was only about five and a half months old. The Crowley County coroner, Gary Gibson, confirmed the death and said that foul play was not suspected. An autopsy was performed on March 1. Later reports, citing official records, stated that he had taken his own life. So the man who had lived thirty-nine years in freedom after January 6, 1982, spent only a few months behind bars. No appeals court, as far as public reporting shows, ever ruled on his claim that the DNA had been contaminated and mishandled. The families had their verdict. They had their sentence. And then, very quickly, the man at the center of it all was gone. For many cold cases, that would be the end of the story. This one has another chapter, and it is still being written. To understand it, you need to know about a woman named Yvonne Woods, known as Missy. For twenty-nine years, she analyzed DNA evidence for the Colorado Bureau of Investigation, and she testified in some of the state's most important criminal trials. In recent years, it came out that she had manipulated DNA data in her work. The scandal grew to involve more than a thousand cases. The state of Colorado would spend about eleven million dollars revisiting her cases and retesting evidence. In January 2025, she was charged with one hundred and two felonies. In June 2026, she pleaded guilty to four of them: cybercrime, first-degree perjury, forgery, and attempting to influence a public servant. And on September 8, 2026, at the age of sixty-six, she was sentenced to ten years in prison. In court, she said, "I know an apology cannot undo the harm." The CBI has said that its review found she deviated from standard testing protocols and cut corners, but that it found no evidence that she manufactured DNA profiles or planted DNA. Here is where the Phillips case enters that scandal. Alan Phillips's daughter, Andrea Shelton, believes her father was wrongfully convicted. Through a Denver law firm, Mehr Jolly, she gave the Colorado Bureau of Investigation formal notice that she intended to sue over Missy Woods's role in her father's case. Her attorney, Kevin Mehr, said that in this forty-year-old cold case, "the only evidence against our client was DNA and DNA specifically processed and analyzed by 'Missy' Woods." In June 2025, her lawyers filed a notice of claim seeking one hundred million dollars from the CBI. The CBI said it could not comment on pending litigation. It is essential to be precise about what this means and what it does not. These are allegations made by the family of a man who maintained his innocence. As of the time of this video, no court has found, in any public reporting we could locate, that the DNA in this case was mishandled, and the conviction of Alan Lee Phillips has not been overturned. We could not confirm from public sources exactly what role Missy Woods played in the testing in this case, or whether the claim has since been resolved. What we can say is that the evidence against Phillips came from more than one step: a genealogy lead pointing to his family, a discarded item that matched the glove, and a second sample taken directly from him after his arrest that matched again. Whether that is enough to answer his family's doubts is a question the courts, not this channel, would have to decide. And there is a hard lesson in it either way. Every conviction built on DNA rests on trust. Trust that the lab is honest. Trust that the analyst followed the rules. When that trust is broken anywhere, it shakes every case it touches, even the ones that may have been done right. That is why the honesty of forensic science matters not only to defendants, but to victims and their families too. The Schnee and Oberholtzer families waited forty years for a verdict. They deserve a verdict that no one can question. Now, let us go back to where it all began. Hoosier Pass. January 1982. A high mountain road covered in snow. Along the roadside, a few scattered belongings. A driver's license. A backpack. A glove with a stain on it. Today we know what that sock was. It was a Christmas present from a mother in Iowa to her daughter in the mountains. It was the last trace of Annette Schnee's final ride, carried in a stranger's vehicle to the place where Bobbie Jo Oberholtzer was found. It was the thread that tied two murders together and told investigators to look for one man. And we know what the stain on the glove was. It was the mark of a woman who fought back. Annette Kay Schnee was twenty-one years old. She was a big sister who babysat the little one. She was a daughter from Sioux City who moved to the mountains and wore her mother's Christmas socks in the snow. She was trying to get home to Blue River.
