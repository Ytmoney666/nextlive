In November 2000, Angela would have turned thirty. In November 2010, she would have turned forty. Melanie would have turned thirty in the middle of that decade. Each birthday that passed without them was another reminder of a life that stopped in the middle of a sentence. Angela's mother, Linda, lived with a fact that would later sit at the center of her testimony in court. The holidays were never the same. At every family gathering, every Thanksgiving, every Christmas, there was an empty place. Angie won't be there. Those four words, spoken decades later, compress thirty years of holidays into a single sentence. Melanie's sister, Jill, grew from a young woman into an adult and then into middle age. She lived through all the milestones of an ordinary life, the ones that Melanie never had, and she lived through them with a sister missing. Many years later, she would describe her grief to a judge, and it was clear that it had not faded. It had simply become part of who she was. And Marlene Bernas, the mother who had kissed her daughter goodbye and said see you in a little while, waited for the knock on the door that would tell her someone had finally been caught. That knock did not come in 1993. It did not come in 1995, or 2000, or 2010. Meanwhile, inside the Phoenix Police Department, the case was passing from hand to hand. This is one of the hidden costs of a cold case. The detectives who stood at the canal in 1992 and 1993 did not stay on the job forever. They were promoted, transferred, retired. Some of them, like Mike Meislish, would only return to the case decades later, as witnesses. New detectives inherited the files, the boxes, the photographs, the list of tips. They had to learn the case from paper, without having seen the scenes themselves. They had to read the reports written by people they had never met, look at photographs taken before some of them had joined the force, and decide which of the hundreds of old tips deserved a second look. A cold case is, in a very real sense, a conversation between generations of detectives. And yet the case was never forgotten. That is something worth saying clearly. The canal murders were among the most notorious unsolved killings in Phoenix. Through the 1990s and 2000s, investigators kept returning to them. Tips kept coming in. Men were looked at and excluded. The list of names checked over the years reportedly grew to around two thousand. Every exclusion was a small piece of work, a sample, a comparison, a report. And every exclusion ended the same way. Not him. In 1998, something changed nationally. The FBI's Combined DNA Index System, known as CODIS, began operating as a national database, allowing DNA profiles from crime scenes to be searched against profiles from convicted offenders across the country. Over the following years, states expanded the list of crimes for which offenders had to give a DNA sample. Think of it as a giant library of fingerprints, except that every entry is a DNA profile, and every search asks the same question. Is this person already in here? For many cold cases, CODIS was the miracle that everyone had been waiting for. A profile that had sat silent for years would suddenly match an offender who had been convicted of something else, somewhere else. In the canal murders, there was no such miracle. The killer's profile did not match anyone in the criminal databases. Year after year, the system searched, and year after year, it came back empty. As you will learn later in this story, that was not because the man responsible had lived a life without any contact with the justice system. It was because of when, where and how those contacts happened. That is part of the ledger of this case. Let us lay it out plainly, in numbers. Two young women killed, ten months apart. One DNA profile linking both crimes. About two thousand names checked, according to one account. Six years between the first murder and the launch of the national DNA index. Then about sixteen years of searches in that index with no match. Twenty-two years in total between Angela's last bike ride and the day an arrest was finally made. Each of those numbers represents not just a statistic, but nights of waiting for families who did not know if they would ever have an answer. And in those years, something else was happening, far away from the Phoenix Police Department, that no one in law enforcement was paying much attention to. Ordinary people were beginning to buy DNA tests to learn about their family history. In 2000, a company called Family Tree DNA began selling genetic tests to genealogists. Early tests focused on the Y chromosome, the piece of DNA that passes from father to son. Hobbyists who wanted to know whether two men with the same surname shared a common ancestor would compare their results. Many of them posted those results in public online databases, alongside their surnames, so that distant relatives could find them. This was not built for police. It was built for family reunions, for people trying to trace an ancestor who came over on a ship two centuries ago, for people who wanted to know whether the Millers in Ohio were related to the Millers in Virginia. But inside those databases, almost by accident, a new kind of map was being drawn, a map that connected genes to family names. By the early 2010s, thousands of men had posted their Y chromosome results in public databases. Nobody in the canal investigation was looking at them. Nobody in most police departments in America was looking at them either. There was no established method for police to use them, and no precedent showing it could work in a murder case. And all the while, somewhere, the man whose DNA was sitting in the Phoenix evidence room was living his life. He was not in hiding. He was not using a false name. He had moved away for a time, and then come back. He was raising a child. He was going to public events. People in certain circles in Phoenix knew him by sight. He did not know that the evidence he had left behind on the canal in 1992 and 1993 had been preserved, carefully, for two decades. He did not know that a scientist in California was developing a method that could read his family name out of that evidence. And he certainly did not know that one day, a group of strangers would invite him to lunch.

To understand how the canal murders were finally solved, we have to step away from Phoenix for a moment and talk about something that sounds, at first, like it has nothing to do with murder. We have to talk about surnames. In most of the English-speaking world, and in many other cultures, a child traditionally takes the family name of the father. A boy named Miller has a father named Miller, who had a father named Miller, and so on back through the generations, sometimes for hundreds of years. Of course, there are exceptions. People change their names. Children are adopted. Families have secrets. But as a general rule, the surname follows the male line of a family. Now here is the remarkable thing. There is a piece of human DNA that follows exactly the same path. It is called the Y chromosome. Only males have it, and a father passes it to his son almost unchanged. That son passes it to his son. And so on, down the generations. The Y chromosome does not get shuffled and mixed like most of our DNA. It travels down the male line like a family heirloom, with only tiny changes appearing over many generations. So, in many families, the Y chromosome and the surname travel together. They are like two passengers on the same train, getting on in the same station centuries ago and riding side by side ever since. If you can read a man's Y chromosome, you can sometimes make an educated guess about his family name. Scientists measure the Y chromosome in a particular way. They look at places on it where a short piece of genetic code repeats itself several times in a row, like a word stuttered over and over. The number of repeats at each of those places varies between families. Count them at a dozen or more places, and you get a list of numbers. That list is called a Y STR profile. STR stands for short tandem repeat, which is just the scientific name for that stutter. Two men who share the same list of numbers, or nearly the same list, are very likely to share a common ancestor on their father's side. Remember what we said in the last part of this story. By the early 2010s, many genealogy hobbyists had tested their Y chromosomes and posted those lists of numbers in public online databases, along with their surnames, to find distant relatives. That means that, without anyone planning it, a giant reference book had been created. On one side, lists of Y chromosome numbers. On the other side, surnames. And that leads to the idea at the heart of this story. If police had a Y chromosome profile from a crime scene, they could, in theory, compare it to those public databases. If the profile closely matched men with a particular surname, it would suggest that the unknown killer might carry that surname too. It would not identify him. It would not prove anything. But it might turn a pool of thousands of men into a much smaller group. The person who turned that theory into practice in the canal murders was a forensic genealogist named Colleen Fitzpatrick. She ran a company called Identifinders International, and she had spent years working on ways to use genetic genealogy in investigations and in identifying the unknown. She had developed a research strategy to cross-reference a suspect's DNA profile with publicly available DNA databases. What she needed was a case, and a police department willing to try. In 2014, at a conference, she approached members of the Phoenix Police Department. They had a case that fit. A double murder, more than twenty years old, with a male DNA profile from the crime scenes and no match in the criminal databases. Here it is important to be precise about what Fitzpatrick did and did not do. She did not look at the killer's private medical information. She did not search for his face, his height or his eye color. She took the list of Y chromosome numbers from the crime scene profile and entered them into her software, which compared them to the public genealogy databases. The search returned matches. Six of them, according to her account. And the six matches shared a surname. That surname was Miller. Now, Miller is one of the most common surnames in the United States. According to the 2010 census, more than a million Americans carry it. On its own, the word Miller would be close to useless. But that is not how it was used. Fitzpatrick herself was careful about what the result meant. It did not mean that one of the men in the database was the killer. It meant that the killer was likely to be related to those men through the male line, and that he might carry the Miller name. As she put it, any kind of lead generated from genetic genealogy is just a lead. That sentence is worth repeating, because it is the most important thing to understand about this technology. It is not a fingerprint. It is not a confession. It is a direction to look in. The real proof still has to come the old-fashioned way, by finding a specific person and comparing his DNA directly with the evidence. If you have followed true crime over the last few years, you will know the name of the case that made this kind of work famous. In April 2018, investigators in California arrested a former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo and identified him as the Golden State Killer, responsible for a long series of murders and rapes in the 1970s and 1980s. He later pleaded guilty and was sentenced to life in prison. That case used a different and more powerful kind of genealogy, based on the DNA we inherit from both parents, uploaded to a public website called GEDmatch, and it made headlines around the world. After 2018, genetic genealogy became one of the most talked about tools in modern policing. But the canal murders in Phoenix came first. More than three years before the Golden State Killer arrest, a Phoenix investigation had already used public genealogy data to generate a lead in a double murder. Local reporting would later describe it as the first arrest using a technique that matches crime scene DNA to DNA home test kits. Whether or not it was the very first, it was certainly among the earliest. It was done before there were guidelines, before there were dedicated teams, before most people even knew it was possible. There was a reason it worked here, and it was not luck alone. It worked because the Phoenix Police Department had never stopped investigating. Over two decades, detectives had built up a long list of men who had come to their attention for one reason or another. When the surname Miller came back from the genealogy search, it was not dropped into a void. It was laid on top of an existing list of names. The question detectives now had to ask was simple. Of all the men we have ever looked at in this case, which ones are named Miller? The answer to that question would take them, step by step, to a house in the Sunnyslope neighborhood of north Phoenix.

Every cold case that is finally solved has two kinds of heroes. There are the scientists who make something new possible. And there are the detectives who turn that possibility into a name. In the canal murders, the scientist was Colleen Fitzpatrick. One of the detectives was a Phoenix police cold case investigator named Clark Schwartzkopf. Cold case work is not glamorous. It is reading files that are older than some of your colleagues. It is tracking down witnesses who have moved, changed names or died. It is re-checking tips that were checked twenty years ago, in case something was missed. It is making phone calls to families who have been disappointed many times before, and trying not to raise their hopes too high. When the genealogy search came back with the surname Miller, Detective Schwartzkopf had something to compare it with. Over the years, the canal investigation had produced a long list of men who had come to the attention of police. According to accounts of the case, that list contained people with the last name Miller. Six of them, by one account. And one of those names had come up recently. In 2014, according to reporting on the case, a tip had already pointed investigators toward a man named Bryan Patrick Miller. Now there were two separate signals pointing in the same direction. A surname read out of the killer's own DNA. And a man with that surname already on the list. Accounts differ slightly on the exact numbers. One says the surname lead narrowed the list down to about five suspects. Another describes it as turning a list of around two thousand possibilities into a handful. Either way, the effect was the same. For the first time in twenty-two years, investigators were not facing an endless sea of possible men. They were facing a few names. But a surname is not proof. A tip is not proof. To arrest someone for murder, detectives needed what Colleen Fitzpatrick had said they would always need. They needed that man's DNA, compared directly to the evidence from the canal. There are several ways police can obtain a person's DNA. They can ask for it voluntarily. They can obtain a court order, if they have enough evidence to justify one. Or they can collect what is sometimes called abandoned or discarded DNA. When a person throws away a cigarette, a coffee cup or a napkin, or leaves a glass behind on a restaurant table, courts in the United States have generally held that the person has given up any expectation of privacy in that object. Police can pick it up and send it to the laboratory, without the person ever knowing. Asking voluntarily carries a risk. If the man is the killer, he now knows that police are looking at him. He might run. He might destroy evidence. He might refuse, and the investigation would be exposed. So the Phoenix detectives chose the quiet route. Undercover officers arranged to have lunch with Bryan Patrick Miller at a restaurant. He did not know who they were. He accepted. The meal went ahead like any other lunch. And when the meal was over and he left, the officers kept what he had left behind. According to reporting on the case, it was the glass he had been drinking water from. That glass went to the laboratory. Scientists extracted the DNA from it, developed a profile, and compared it to the profile from the canal murders, the profile that had been waiting in evidence storage since 1992 and 1993. On January 13, 2015, the answer came back. It was a match. After twenty-two years, two months and five days, the man who killed Angela Brosso and Melanie Bernas had a name. So who was he? Bryan Patrick Miller was forty-two years old in January 2015. He lived in Sunnyslope, an older neighborhood in north Phoenix, not far from the canal paths where the murders had happened. He lived there with his daughter, in a home that had once belonged to a grandmother. When police later went inside, they described the house as a hoarding situation, crowded with possessions. Do the arithmetic, and the age is striking. In November 1992, when Angela Brosso went out for her birthday-eve ride, Bryan Patrick Miller was a young man of about twenty. In September 1993, when Melanie Bernas went out for her evening ride, he was about twenty-one. Barely older than the seventeen-year-old high school junior he would be convicted of killing. By the time the DNA caught up with him, he was in his forties, a father, a man with a public life. And part of that public life is the detail that made this case famous far beyond Arizona. In certain circles in Phoenix, Bryan Patrick Miller was a recognizable figure. At comic and science fiction conventions and other public events, he appeared in costume as a character he called the Zombie Hunter. One documentary summary describes him as someone who dressed up like a comic book superhero and cruised the valley. In those circles, people knew the costume. Before most of Phoenix ever heard his name, many people in those fan communities knew him simply as the Zombie Hunter. Think about the contrast. For more than two decades, the families of Angela and Melanie had waited for the name of a monster. The man the DNA pointed to was someone who had been walking around the same city in a costume, playing a hero, posing for photographs. He was not a stranger in a distant state. He was not a drifter who had vanished. For years, he was there, in plain sight. It is also worth pausing on the people who made that lunch possible. Undercover work like this looks simple when it is described in one sentence. In reality it requires planning, patience and nerve. The officers had to approach a man who, if the evidence was right, had killed two people. They had to give him a believable reason to sit down with them. They had to behave naturally for the length of a meal. And they had to make sure that the glass they collected could be traced, without any doubt, from the restaurant table to the laboratory, because every step would later be examined by defense lawyers in court. The chain of custody that began on the canal in 1992 now had a new link, a glass on a restaurant table in 2015. Now, it is important to be precise about what the DNA match meant in January 2015. It meant that the man whose glass was collected at that lunch had the same DNA profile as the man who left evidence at both canal scenes. In legal terms, it gave police probable cause to arrest him. It did not, by itself, convict him. That would be the job of a court, and as you will see, it would take a very long time. But on the night of January 13, 2015, that was not what mattered to the detectives who had carried this case. What mattered was that, after twenty-two years, there was finally someone to arrest. They went to the house in Sunnyslope. And Bryan Patrick Miller, the man known as the Zombie Hunter, was taken into custody.

The arrest of Bryan Patrick Miller was announced by Phoenix police in January 2015, and the news spread fast, from Phoenix television to national outlets. After twenty-two years, the canal murders had a suspect in custody. For a city that had lived with the case for two decades, it felt almost unreal. The man arrested was forty-two years old. The crimes had happened when he was about twenty. On the day of his arrest, detectives questioned him. That interrogation was recorded on video, and years later, in November 2022, the recording was shown at his trial and released to the public. By then, much more was known about the man behind the costume. On February 2, 2015, Bryan Patrick Miller appeared in court and pleaded not guilty. He was charged with the murders of Angela Brosso and Melanie Bernas. From that day on, the legal wording matters. In 2015 he was a defendant. He was presumed innocent. He was charged, not convicted. And it would be more than eight years before a court decided his fate. As the case moved forward, his past began to come into view. And it explained a great deal about why the DNA had stayed silent for so long. In 1989, when Bryan Patrick Miller was about sixteen years old, he attacked a woman in the parking lot of the Paradise Valley Mall in Phoenix. He stabbed her in the back. She survived. Because he was a juvenile, the case was handled in the juvenile justice system. According to reporting on the case, he served about one year in a juvenile detention facility. Then he was released. In 1989, there was no national DNA database. Juvenile records were sealed. A teenager who stabbed a stranger could serve his time and return to the community with no DNA profile anywhere that could later be compared to a crime scene. During the canal murder prosecution, a judge ordered his juvenile court records unsealed. And at his trial, in October 2022, the woman he had stabbed in 1989 took the witness stand. Thirty-three years after the attack, she described it for the court. Then there was Washington State. A few years after the canal murders, Miller moved to Everett, Washington, north of Seattle. In 2002, according to reports at the time of his arrest, he was charged with assault there in an attack on a woman. She survived. He was reportedly acquitted. An acquittal is not a conviction, and offender DNA databases were built from convictions. Later, he moved back to Phoenix with his daughter. Lay those facts side by side and you have the second half of the system-failure ledger. A violent attack on a stranger at sixteen, handled quietly in juvenile court, before any DNA database existed. A second accusation of violence against a woman, in another state, ending without a conviction. Two contacts with the justice system. Neither one placed his DNA where it could be matched to the canal. For more than twenty years, the most important evidence in Phoenix and the most important suspect in Phoenix never met. Why did it take so long from arrest to trial? For the families, those years were their own kind of waiting. The man was in custody, and that mattered enormously. But a name in a jail record is not the same as a verdict. Hearings were scheduled and postponed. Experts were hired and reports were written. Anniversaries continued to pass. The answer lies mostly in the defense. Miller's lawyers pursued an insanity defense. They argued that he suffered from autism and from dissociative disorders, and that he had been so severely abused by his mother as a child that he was not legally responsible for what he did. Questions about his mental health also had to be resolved before any trial could begin. A judge eventually ruled that he was mentally fit to stand trial. Then Miller made a significant choice. He waived his right to a jury. That meant a single judge, Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Suzanne Cohen, would hear the evidence, decide whether he was guilty, and, if he was, decide his sentence. A trial without a jury is a different kind of courtroom. There are no jurors to persuade with emotion, no jury selection, no deliberation room. Every argument is made to one person, a professional judge who must explain her reasoning in writing and whose decisions will be examined by higher courts. For a case that would almost certainly end in a request for the death penalty, it placed an enormous responsibility on a single judge. The trial finally began in early October 2022, almost thirty years after Angela's death. It was long. It ran for months. And it brought back, one by one, the people whose lives had been touched by that canal. Angela's boyfriend testified about her last day, the birthday cake, the three searches in the dark. Marlene Bernas testified by telephone about the kiss goodbye and the words see you in a little while. Retired detective Mike Meislish described the scene he had seen in September 1993. The woman from the Paradise Valley Mall testified about 1989. The interrogation video was played. The scientists explained the DNA, and the detectives explained the genealogy lead and the lunch. The core of the defense was the claim that Miller was insane at the time. The prosecution argued that he knew exactly what he was doing. On April 11, 2023, Judge Cohen delivered her verdict. She rejected the insanity defense. She found that Miller knew what he was doing. And she found him guilty on all counts. According to the Associated Press, those were two counts each of first-degree murder, kidnapping and attempted sexual assault. For the first time, in the eyes of the law, Bryan Patrick Miller was no longer a suspect or a defendant. He was a convicted murderer. The case then moved into the penalty phase, because the State of Arizona was seeking the death penalty. On April 19, 2023, the families spoke. Angela's mother told the court that she no longer has the holidays other mothers have, because Angie won't be there. Melanie's older sister, Jill Canetta, described the grief she had carried for three decades. The defense asked for life in prison, pointing again to the childhood abuse and to his autism. The prosecution argued that the crimes deserved the ultimate punishment. On May 22, 2023, Miller spoke in court. He said he was not seeking sympathy, and that he hoped the families could find peace. On Wednesday, June 7, 2023, in a courtroom in downtown Phoenix, Judge Suzanne Cohen announced her decision. She accepted that the abuse Miller had suffered as a child was real and had been proven. She found that it was not enough to outweigh the crimes. According to one account of the hearing, she described the crimes as beyond the pale. She sentenced Bryan Patrick Miller, now fifty years old, to death for the murder of Angela Brosso, and to death for the murder of Melanie Bernas. He also received prison terms on the other counts, reported as twenty-four years. Then and now. About twenty years old on the canal path in 1992. Fifty years old in a courtroom in 2023. Thirty years and seven months had passed since Angela's last bike ride. And yet, even then, the story was not quite over.

After the sentence, Bryan Patrick Miller was taken to Arizona's death row. A few months later, in October 2023, he spoke to journalists from death row for the first time since his conviction, in interviews that were broadcast in Arizona and on the national CBS program 48 Hours. He complained about the conditions. He said death row was cold. He described the food as not great. And he said, once again, that he did not commit the murders. Hold those two images side by side for a moment. On one side, a man complaining about cold cells and bad food. On the other side, two women who never reached twenty-two and eighteen. Angela Brosso never got to complain about anything again after November 8, 1992. Melanie Bernas never got to finish her junior year. A court of law, after a trial that lasted months and heard the evidence from both crime scenes, the DNA, the genealogy lead, the lunch, the testimony of the families and of a woman he stabbed in 1989, found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. His denial does not change the verdict. Under Arizona law, every death sentence is automatically appealed to the Arizona Supreme Court. Miller's case went there as State of Arizona versus Bryan Patrick Miller, case number CR 23 0157 AP. According to the court's public docket, his lawyers asked for more time to file their opening brief. The court granted an extension to June 2024, then another to September 2024, and warned that no further extensions would be granted without extraordinary circumstances. As of October 2026, our research did not find a published ruling from the Arizona Supreme Court in his appeal. He remains under sentence of death. Appeals in capital cases in Arizona typically take years, and after the direct appeal there can be further rounds of review in state and federal courts. So, legally, this story may not have reached its final page. But for the families of Angela and Melanie, the most important page has already been written. In April 2023, a judge looked at the evidence and said the word they had waited more than thirty years to hear. Guilty. What changed because of this case? A great deal. In 2015, using a public genealogy database to help identify a suspect in a murder was almost unheard of. Today it is one of the most powerful tools in cold case work in the United States. Since the Golden State Killer arrest in 2018, genetic genealogy has been used to help identify suspects in a long list of cold cases, many of them decades old, and to give names to unidentified victims who had been buried as John and Jane Does. It has also raised serious questions about privacy, about consent, and about what ordinary people agree to when they send their DNA to a genealogy company. Those debates are real, and they are not over. But in Phoenix, in the canal murders, the method did exactly what Colleen Fitzpatrick said it would do. It produced a lead. Just a lead. And then detectives did the rest. There is another lesson in this case, less dramatic but just as important. The DNA that convicted Bryan Patrick Miller was collected in 1992 and 1993. It was preserved for more than two decades. If anyone, at any point in those years, had lost it, contaminated it, or thrown it away to make room in an evidence warehouse, none of this would have happened. The quiet hero of this story is not only a genealogist or a detective. It is also a sealed evidence package on a shelf, and every person who signed for it, stored it and protected it, for twenty-two years, without knowing whether it would ever matter. And there is a hard lesson in the ledger. A teenager who stabbed a stranger in 1989 served about a year in juvenile detention and left no DNA behind in any database. A man charged with assault in Washington in 2002 was acquitted. Two contacts with the justice system did not connect him to the canal. Nobody can say for certain what might have changed if they had. But it is impossible to look at those dates and not wonder. The case has since been told on national television, on 48 Hours, on Dateline, and in a forensic documentary series, and almost every telling returns to the same contrast, the costume and the canal. But for the people of north Phoenix, it is not a television story. The Arizona Canal still runs across the northern part of the city. People still walk and run and ride their bikes along its banks in the cool of the evening. Most of them were not born, or were small children, when Angela and Melanie went out for their last rides. Many of them have never heard their names. That is one reason stories like this one need to be told. And now, let's go back to where this story began. Sunday, November 8, 1992. North Phoenix. A twenty-one-year-old woman from Camp Hill, Pennsylvania, gets on her bicycle on the eve of her twenty-second birthday. She has been in Phoenix for five months. She has a job working on new technology. She has a home. Behind her, in the apartment, the man she lives with is baking her a birthday cake. He expects her back within the hour. And ten months later, a seventeen-year-old girl is kissed goodbye by her mother, who tells her she loves her and that she will see her in a little while. She gets on her bicycle too. They both ride near the same canal. Neither of them comes home. Let us remember them not for how their lives ended, but for how they were lived. Angela Brosso. A girl from a small Pennsylvania town who crossed the country to study technology. A young woman her boss said would always make you laugh or smile. Bold. Fearless. A cyclist. Someone's daughter. Someone's Angie. Twenty-one years old, and one night away from twenty-two. Melanie Bernas. A high school junior who signed up for the harder classes. A friend her friends called adventurous and spontaneous. A little sister. A daughter whose mother called the emergency rooms because the worst thing she could imagine was a bicycle accident. Seventeen years old. For twenty-two years, the canal kept flowing past the places where it happened. For twenty-two years, two families waited. And in the end, the answer came from a laboratory, a list of surnames built by people searching for their ancestors, a detective with a long list of names, and a glass left on a restaurant table. If there is one lesson in this story, it is this. Evidence does not forget. People move away. People retire. People put on costumes and become someone else for a while. But a small piece of evidence, kept safe and waiting, can outlast all of it. The cake was baked for a birthday that never came. The words see you in a little while were never fulfilled. But Angela's and Melanie's names were finally spoken in a courtroom next to the word guilty, and that is something their families had waited three decades to hear.
