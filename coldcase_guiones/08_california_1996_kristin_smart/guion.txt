And the physical evidence, wherever it was, was getting older, harder to find and easier to dismiss. On May 25, 2002, exactly six years after Kristin was last seen, she was declared legally dead. It is one of the strangest moments a family can live through. A court document says that your daughter is no longer alive. And yet there is no body, no funeral in the usual sense, no grave to visit, no answer to the question of where she is or what happened in her last minutes. Kristin was legally dead. Her case was still officially a disappearance. And no one had been charged with anything. For a family, that legal step is not an ending. It is a document that has to be filed, a box that has to be checked, while the question at the center of their lives stays exactly where it was on that first silent Sunday. Six years of Sundays had passed. There would be many more. Here it is worth explaining why a case like this is so hard for the people who investigate it, because it is easy, from the outside, to assume that years without an arrest mean years without work. In a homicide with a body, investigators have something to analyze. There is a cause of death. There may be DNA, fibers, a weapon, a timeline from the medical examiner. In a case with no body, almost every question has to be answered indirectly. Prosecutors must first prove that the person is dead and did not simply leave. Then they must prove that someone killed her. Then they must prove who. And they must do all of it without the single most important piece of evidence in any murder case. Cases without a body are rare and difficult to bring to trial. Prosecutors who attempt them usually wait until they have a mountain of circumstantial evidence, because they know they may get only one chance. If a jury acquits, the suspect can never be tried again for the same crime. That rule, part of the protection against double jeopardy, hung over the Kristin Smart case for decades. Charge too early, lose, and Paul Flores would be free forever. So the case waited. And the waiting itself became part of the story. In 2010, voters in San Luis Obispo County elected a new sheriff, Ian Parkinson. He took office in 2011 and said he would review the Kristin Smart case. It was not a dramatic announcement. It was the kind of quiet promise many new officials make about old cases. What made it different was that, over the following decade, it turned out to be real. Years later, on the day of the arrests, Parkinson would acknowledge that mistakes were made early in the investigation, and he would explain why it had taken so long to bring charges. Investigators, he said, needed to build a case that would stick. In January 2016, dogs trained to detect old human remains were brought to the hillside above the Cal Poly campus, the slope beneath the big letter P that students see every day. The dogs alerted at several spots. Months later, those alerts turned into the largest physical search in the history of the case. In September 2016, the FBI and the San Luis Obispo County Sheriff's Office set up a command post on the hillside. Reports at the time described twenty-five FBI agents and fifteen members of the sheriff's office, along with investigators from the California Department of Fish and Wildlife. The digging was planned to last four days. Crews expected to move about twenty thousand cubic feet of earth. Sheriff Parkinson said the excavation came from a lead developed through a comprehensive review of the entire case. Three sites were marked. The first, known as site one, was the closest of the three to the dorm where Kristin had been living. For the Smart family, and for everyone who had followed the case for twenty years, the sight of federal agents digging on the same campus where Kristin had last been seen was overwhelming. On September 8, 2016, officials announced that they had found items of interest. They did not say what those items were. Items that needed a closer look were to be sent to the FBI's forensic anthropologist in Quantico, Virginia, or to a state laboratory. For a few days, it seemed possible that the answer was finally in the ground. Then the dig ended. No remains of Kristin Smart were recovered. Whatever the items of interest had been, they did not bring her home. It is hard to describe how that kind of ending feels to a family. You allow yourself to hope, because the dogs alerted, because the FBI came, because the cameras are there. And then the holes are filled in, the agents leave, and you are back where you started, except now you are twenty years older. But something did change in 2016, even if the public could not see it. When Sheriff Parkinson later laid out the history of the investigation, one report quoted him as saying that investigators had discovered evidence in late 2016 that confirmed Paul Flores as the suspect. He did not make that evidence public at the time, and it is reported in only one account, so we treat it with care. If it is accurate, it means that from late 2016 on, the sheriff's office was no longer asking who. It was asking how to prove it. Let us stop and put the system-failure ledger on the table, the way this channel does in every case. In 1996, the first call to campus police produced no report. Campus interviews began four days after the disappearance. According to the family's lawsuit, the dorm room of the last person seen with Kristin was not searched for sixteen days. The cadaver dogs did not arrive for more than a month, after the room had been cleaned. The man last seen with her refused to answer questions in a civil deposition in 1997. Kristin was declared legally dead in 2002. A sheriff who took office in 2011 reviewed the case and launched a dig in 2016 that recovered no remains. By 2019, twenty-three years had passed. Not one person had been charged. Twenty-three years. About twelve hundred Sundays. And during all of those years, the witnesses who could change everything were still out there, living ordinary lives, carrying what they knew. Some had never spoken to police. Some had never been asked at all. The case did not need new technology to move. It needed someone to go and find those people, sit with them, listen, and convince them that their memories mattered. The person who did that did not carry a badge. He carried a microphone.

Many of the cold cases on this channel are solved by one dramatic piece of science. A DNA profile uploaded to a genealogy database. A family tree that narrows to one surname. A discarded cup that produces a match. The Kristin Smart case was not solved that way. There was no DNA match that named her killer, and there was no family tree. What brought this case to a courtroom was something slower and, in some ways, more human. A set of older, sometimes controversial tools, used again and again over twenty-five years, combined with the oldest investigative method of all: finding people who know something and getting them to talk. To understand the breakthrough, you need to understand each of those tools, what it can do, and what it cannot. Start with the dogs. A cadaver dog is trained to find the scent of human decomposition. When a body breaks down, it releases chemical compounds into the air, into soil, into fabric and into wood. A trained dog can detect those compounds at levels no human nose could notice, and it is taught to give a specific signal, an alert, when it finds them. In 1996, in Santa Lucia Hall, the dogs were handled by volunteers. Adela Morris testified that the search was blind, meaning she was not told which room investigators suspected. Her two border collies each searched all three floors of the building, and both reacted only to Room 128. A boxer named Torrey, brought in separately, did the same. A blind search with multiple dogs is about as strong as dog evidence gets. But dog evidence has limits, and the defense in this case pressed every one of them. A dog cannot tell you whose remains it smells, or when they were there. Its alerts can be affected by its training, by its handler and possibly by chemicals in the environment. When Morris was asked about her dog's rate of false alerts, she said they had not tracked it at the time. And she had not known the room had been cleaned. The same pattern appeared in 2021, under a deck in Arroyo Grande. There, dog handlers searched the area beneath the back porch of Ruben Flores's home. The dogs did not give a formal alert under the deck, although both handlers said they noticed changes in the dogs' behavior. Dogs, in other words, could point. They could not prove. The second tool is ground-penetrating radar. Imagine pushing something that looks a bit like a lawn mower slowly across a yard. Inside it, an antenna sends short radio pulses into the ground and listens for the echoes that bounce back. Undisturbed soil, built up layer by layer over many years, sends back a fairly even pattern. A hole that was dug and refilled, or an object buried in the ground, sends back something different, an anomaly on the screen. Radar does not show a picture of what is underground. It shows where the ground has been disturbed or where something unusual may be hidden. In March 2021, investigators moved a ground-penetrating radar unit back and forth across sections of Ruben Flores's backyard in Arroyo Grande and across ground underneath the porch. A radar specialist named Phillip Hanes worked alongside an archaeologist hired by the sheriff's office. The third tool is forensic archaeology, which is exactly what it sounds like. Instead of digging quickly with a shovel, an archaeologist excavates the way she would excavate an ancient site, one thin layer at a time, recording the color, texture and shape of the soil as she goes. That patience matters, because soil remembers. If something has decomposed in the ground, it can leave a stain, a patch of soil that looks different from everything around it. The archaeologist in this case, identified in court reports as Cindy Arrington and in some reports as Christine Arrington, had nearly thirty years of experience. She testified that under the deck, investigators found several anomalies, and that one of them was a dark stain that did not match the surrounding soil, consistent with human decomposition. On cross-examination, she acknowledged that some anomalies could have been caused by roots, human or animal activity. When photographs of that stain were shown at trial, jurors cried, according to reporters in the courtroom. The fourth tool is the laboratory. Soil from the excavation was collected and sent for testing. A forensic DNA analyst named Angela Butler testified that a test called HemDirect found human blood in several soil samples from the Arroyo Grande property. In simple terms, HemDirect reacts to hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells, and it reacts to the hemoglobin of humans, other primates and ferrets. Some samples tested positive. Others tested negative. The defense pointed out that there were substantially more negatives than positives, and Butler replied that weighing the samples overall as negative would be improper forensic analysis. A sheriff's forensic specialist, Shelby Liddell, testified that signs of possible blood were also found inside a trailer on the property after it was sprayed with a forensic chemical. The defense called its own experts. Dr. Elizabeth Johnson told the jury that the positive soil results were likely false positives, that the test was not designed for soil, that its results can be affected by the soil's acidity, which was never measured, and that studies show blood in soil is too degraded to be detected after about four weeks. A forensic taphonomy expert, Dr. David Carter, a specialist in what happens to a body after death, was asked whether there was evidence that a body had ever been buried there. And as you already heard, an older stain on Paul Flores's dorm mattress produced DNA results that a forensic analyst called uninformative. So the science in this case never delivered a single clean answer. Every piece came with a counterargument. Then there is the fifth tool, the newest one, which arrived only in 2026. It is called soil vapor sampling. Instead of digging up soil, scientists collect gas from underground and test it for volatile organic compounds, the airborne chemicals associated with human decomposition. It is, in the words of the reporting on it, still considered an evolving science. You will hear about it again at the end of this story. Here is the key to understanding how this case was finally built. None of these tools, on its own, could convict anyone. The dogs could be questioned. The radar showed only disturbed ground. The stain could be roots. The blood tests could be false positives. But a jury is allowed to look at all the evidence together, the way you would look at a mosaic rather than at a single tile. Kristin was last seen with Paul Flores. Witnesses said he was holding her up. Dogs alerted on his room. He had a black eye he explained two different ways. Years later, radar, an archaeologist's stain and soil tests pointed to his father's backyard. And then, crucially, there were the people. The witnesses who had never spoken to investigators, and whose memories would change how every other piece of evidence looked. The tools could only point. People had to finish the sentence. And in 2019, someone set out to find them.

His name is Chris Lambert. When Kristin Smart disappeared in May 1996, he was eight years old, living in the small town of Orcutt, a short drive down the coast from Cal Poly. He did not know her. He was not a detective, not a journalist, not a lawyer. He was a singer-songwriter and a recording engineer, a man who made music for a living. National reporting would later describe him as shy. And for years, like everyone else on the Central Coast, he drove past the same roadside billboard in Arroyo Grande. Kristin's grinning face. A reward of seventy-five thousand dollars. A question no one had answered for more than two decades. At some point the billboard stopped being part of the scenery for him. According to reporting at the time of the arrests, the billboard led him, about three years earlier, on a detour from his music career, a detour that took over his life. He began to research the case. And then he did something that the official investigation, for all its years of effort, had never fully done. He went looking for the people who had never talked to police. The result was a podcast called Your Own Backyard. Published timelines date its release to 2019. Lambert produced it himself. He took no advertising, relying instead on donations from listeners. Episode by episode, the podcast walked listeners through the night of May 24 and 25, 1996, the people at the party, the walk home, the early investigation, the years of silence. He did not have subpoena power or a badge. What he had was patience, a microphone and a growing audience. And something unexpected happened. People started to talk to him. Some of them were witnesses who had never been interviewed. Some had information they had carried for years without knowing what to do with it. Lambert's approach, as described in reporting, was to find overlooked or reluctant witnesses and to encourage them to take what they knew to investigators. The podcast did not replace the sheriff's work. It fed it. By April 2021, Your Own Backyard had reached about seven and a half million downloads and had climbed to number two on the iTunes podcast chart. A story that had faded into a local cold case was suddenly being followed by listeners across the country. Sheriff Ian Parkinson would later say publicly that Lambert's work helped bring witnesses forward and was a positive for the investigation. It is rare for a sheriff to credit a podcaster. It tells you how much those new voices mattered. Think about the difference between the two kinds of approach. A detective knocks on a door with a badge, and some people close up, afraid of getting involved, afraid of being wrong, afraid of the person they might be talking about. Someone who has spent years on the story, who knows every name and every date, and who simply asks to listen, can sometimes reach the same people in a different way. That is not a criticism of the police. It is a reminder that witnesses are human beings, and that sometimes the right question needs the right messenger. One of the witnesses whose story became central to the case was Jennifer Hudson, the woman who said that, as a seventeen-year-old in the summer of 1996, she had heard Paul Flores admit to killing Kristin at a backyard skate ramp. Her account would later be tested hard in court. The defense questioned her timeline and her past, and it questioned her connection to the podcast. Hudson testified that Lambert asked her not to tell authorities that the two of them had been to a property once she contacted investigators, and she said she told authorities everything during her meetings with them. That exchange shows both the power and the risk of a podcast investigation. It can find witnesses no one else found. It can also give a defense lawyer something to attack. Meanwhile, the sheriff's office was moving. In 2020, investigators served search warrants at properties connected to the Flores family, and by this time Paul Flores had been publicly named the prime suspect in Kristin's disappearance. Later, the Smart family's civil attorney would allege in a lawsuit that after those 2020 searches, Ruben Flores, Paul's father, moved Kristin's remains. That is an allegation in a civil case. It has never been proven, and as you will hear, a jury later acquitted Ruben Flores of the criminal charge against him. Then, in March 2021, investigators came to Ruben Flores's home in Arroyo Grande with a search warrant, cadaver dogs and ground-penetrating radar. The search was expected to take about two days. The radar was pushed back and forth across the backyard and across the ground beneath the porch. Archaeologists excavated the sloped area under the deck. Afterward, the sheriff's office made a short announcement. Additional evidence related to the murder of Kristin Smart, it said, had been discovered at the site. Notice the words. Not the disappearance. The murder. After a quarter of a century, the sheriff's office was now describing the case, in public, as a homicide. Four weeks later, on the morning of Tuesday, April 13, 2021, Paul Flores was arrested in San Pedro, in the Los Angeles area. That afternoon he was booked into the San Luis Obispo County Jail on a charge of murder. His father, Ruben Flores, who would be eighty-one years old by the time of the verdict, was arrested in Arroyo Grande and charged as an accessory after the fact, accused of helping to hide what his son had done. His bail was set at two hundred fifty thousand dollars. Let the then-and-now sink in. When Kristin disappeared, Paul Flores was a college freshman. When he was arrested, he was a man in his forties. Kristin was still, and would always be, nineteen. The billboard that had pulled Chris Lambert into the case had stood for more than two decades. The phone in Stockton had been silent for nearly twenty-five years of Sundays. At a news conference that day, Sheriff Parkinson explained why the arrest had taken so long. Investigators, he said, needed to build a case that would stick. And he offered a line that could serve as the motto of every no-body homicide case in America. It's taken many years to get to this point, he said, but as I've always said, it's not what you believe, it's what you can prove. He also acknowledged that mistakes had been made early in the investigation. He credited the podcast. He declined to discuss the details of the search warrants. Paul Flores's lawyer declined to comment. Ruben Flores's lawyer said his client was innocent. And the sheriff said the words that hung over the entire day. We have not recovered Kristin. The search for her remains, he said, would continue regardless of anything that happened in court. For the Smart family, the news was the closest thing to an answer they had received in a quarter of a century. Their daughter's case was no longer a disappearance. It was a murder prosecution with a defendant in custody. But it was also only the beginning of a new and painful chapter, because an arrest is not a conviction, and in a case without a body, every single piece of evidence would now be fought over in public. The question was no longer who. It was whether a jury, after twenty-six years, would agree.

The legal road began in a San Luis Obispo courtroom in the summer of 2021. On August 2, 2021, a preliminary hearing opened to decide whether there was enough evidence to send Paul and Ruben Flores to trial. Four witnesses testified on the first day. Paul's mother, Susan Flores, declined to testify. Over the following weeks, the public heard, many for the first time, from the dog handlers who had searched Room 128 in 1996, from investigators, from forensic specialists and from Jennifer Hudson. In September 2021, Superior Court Judge Craig van Rooyen ruled that both men would stand trial. Along the way he made the remark about the black eye, that Paul Flores's changing explanations showed consciousness of guilt. Court records that had been sealed for years began to come into public view, including the prosecution filing that described the nicknames some women said Flores had been called. Then came a question of fairness. After twenty-five years of headlines, billboards, searches and a national podcast, could anyone in San Luis Obispo County serve on an impartial jury? Defense attorneys for both men argued that they could not, and in the spring of 2022 Judge van Rooyen agreed. Citing years of publicity and the size of the county, he moved the trial north to Monterey County. The case would be heard in Salinas. The trial itself had an unusual design. Paul and Ruben Flores would be tried together, in the same courtroom, but each would have his own jury. Paul's jury would decide whether he was guilty of murder. Ruben's jury would decide whether he was guilty of being an accessory after the fact. Some evidence would be heard by both juries, and some by only one. Monterey County Superior Court Judge Jennifer O'Keefe presided. On Monday, July 18, 2022, opening statements began. Deputy District Attorney Chris Peuvrelle stood up and began not with a piece of evidence but with a number. It had been, he told the jurors, 1,359 Sundays since Kristin Smart went missing. He explained what that number meant. Kristin had called her parents every Sunday. On Sunday, May 26, 1996, she did not call. Prosecutors told the jury that Paul Flores murdered Kristin while attempting to rape her in his dorm room on the night of May 25, 1996, and that Kristin had been incapacitated, unable to walk or stand on her own. The defense told a different story. The last time Paul Flores saw Kristin Smart, his lawyers said, she was alive and capable of walking on her own back to Muir Hall. Soon after the trial began, it paused because of what court officials described only as a health issue. Then the witnesses came, week after week. The very first was Kristin's mother. Denise Smart described her daughter, the summer in Hawaii, and the days after the phone stopped ringing. I did whatever I could, she told the court. Students from 1996, now middle-aged, described the party on Crandall Way, the loud thud, the young man who seemed fixated on Kristin, the walk toward campus. The dog handlers described Cholla, Cirque and Torrey and the alerts on Room 128. Defense attorneys called them volunteers and questioned their methods. In September 2022 the jury heard the science of the backyard. The archaeologist described the dark stain beneath the deck. The DNA analyst described positive HemDirect results in several soil samples, and admitted there were more negatives than positives. When photographs of the possible burial site were shown, jurors wept. Jennifer Hudson took the stand and repeated her account of the skate ramp in 1996. Under a long cross-examination by Paul Flores's attorney, Robert Sanger, about her timeline, her past and her connection to the podcast, she broke down in tears at times. Two other women also testified. Each said that Paul Flores had drugged and raped her, in incidents years after Kristin disappeared. Those allegations were not charges in this trial, and the jury was allowed to hear them as part of the prosecution's case about his conduct. Later, in his appeal, Flores would argue that those women should never have been allowed to testify. Near the end of September, the prosecution rested. The defense called its own witnesses, including experts who told the jury that the soil results were likely false positives and that blood would not survive in soil long enough to be detected. Then, in early October, came the closing arguments. Peuvrelle returned to his number. It's been 1,370 Sundays, he said, since Stan and Denise Smart have waited for a call from Kristin Smart. Paul Flores, he told the jury, was guilty as sin. The defense urged the jurors to see a case built on volunteers' dogs, faded memories, a podcast and soil that proved nothing. And then the waiting began again. Ruben Flores's jury finished first. On Monday, October 17, it delivered a sealed verdict to Judge O'Keefe. Paul's jury kept deliberating, for nearly two weeks in all. On Tuesday, October 18, 2022, both verdicts were read in the same courtroom, moments apart. Paul Flores was found guilty of first-degree murder. Ruben Flores, eighty-one years old, was found not guilty of being an accessory after the fact. Two juries. One courtroom. Two opposite outcomes. Ruben Flores was acquitted. In the eyes of the law, he did not help hide Kristin's body, and he has not been convicted of any crime in this case. Paul Flores, the freshman from Room 128, was now, after twenty-six years, a convicted murderer. Sentencing came on Friday, March 10, 2023. Before it began, Judge O'Keefe denied defense motions asking for a new trial and for an acquittal. The Smart family filled the hearing with Kristin's life. A video of home movies and photographs, starting from when she was a baby. A letter from her father. Words from her brother, her sister, a cousin, a childhood friend. Her brother Matt asked the court to make sure Flores spent his life in prison. Then Denise Smart spoke last. She looked at the man who had been at the center of her family's nightmare for twenty-six years and described what she saw. Watching Paul Flores sit stone-faced and remorseless behind his mask, she said, was emblematic of the hiding he has done for the last 26-plus years. She spoke about the body that was still missing. Torturing a family by continuing to withhold the location of their sister and daughter, she said, is a cruel and visceral pain that no one should ever have to bear. And she asked a question that no one in the room could answer. Where is the humanity in this human being? Judge O'Keefe sentenced Paul Flores to twenty-five years to life in state prison. Under that sentence he must serve at least twenty-five years before he can be considered for parole. She also ordered him to register as a sex offender for the rest of his life, and, according to reports from the courtroom, she called him a cancer to society. Paul Flores has always maintained his innocence. He did not tell the court where Kristin is. Outside the courthouse, Denise Smart told reporters what the day meant to her. Today is not really a day of joy, she said. It's a day of relief that Kristin's voice was heard. The case had a verdict. It had a sentence. It still did not have Kristin.

On the day of the verdicts, October 18, 2022, Kristin's father, Stan Smart, stood before reporters and read a statement on behalf of the family. It was not a celebration. After 26 years, he said, with today's split verdicts, we learned that our quest for justice for Kristin will continue. And then the line that captured everything the family felt. Without Kristin, there is no joy or victory with this verdict. The family thanked both juries. They said their faith in the justice system had been renewed. They thanked Chris Lambert, the musician whose podcast had helped bring the case back to life. And they made clear that one verdict, the acquittal of Ruben Flores, was not the end of their fight. Their civil attorney, James Murphy, had already filed a separate lawsuit in April 2021 that accused Ruben Flores of moving Kristin's remains. After the verdicts, Murphy said he expected the civil case to proceed, and he put it bluntly. I have a very strong feeling, he said, that Ruben is going to be held civilly accountable. It is important to be precise here. A civil lawsuit is not a criminal conviction. Ruben Flores was acquitted by a jury of the only criminal charge against him, and he has not been convicted of any crime connected to Kristin's death. Susan Flores, Paul's mother, has never been charged with any crime in this case. Whatever the civil courts decide, those facts stand. The story did not end with the sentence, either, because the man in prison did not stop fighting it. In 2023, Paul Flores appealed his conviction and brought in a new attorney. In 2024, a judge ruled that he must pay just over three hundred fifty thousand dollars to the Smart family for costs they had incurred. By 2025, his appeal was before the California Court of Appeal. His lawyers raised an issue involving a juror identified as Juror 273, argued that the two women who testified he had drugged and raped them should never have been heard by the jury, and that the prosecutor had committed misconduct during his closing argument. They also challenged the testimony that Kristin had seemed drugged that night, calling it hearsay without a proper foundation. And they continued to argue that there was no evidence he had sexually assaulted Kristin. On October 24, 2025, the Court of Appeal upheld the conviction. On November 21, 2025, it refused to rehear the case. Flores took one more step, to the California Supreme Court, asking it to review his conviction. In January 2026, the state's highest court said no. With that decision, his direct appeals were exhausted. The conviction was final. Paul Flores, forty-nine years old, remained in prison under his sentence of twenty-five years to life. The San Luis Obispo County District Attorney, Dan Dow, issued a statement. While we are very pleased that this decision brings a stronger sense of finality for all involved, he said, we will always remember Kristin Smart. The institution that had failed in the first days faced its own reckoning. In May 2023, the president of Cal Poly, Jeffrey Armstrong, publicly apologized to the Smart family for the way the university had handled the investigation into Kristin's disappearance. In January 2024, Kristin's parents and her brother and sister sued the university, claiming negligence, emotional distress and wrongful death. Their lawsuit argued that campus police had not taken the first reports seriously, had allowed key evidence to be destroyed, had waited four days to interview witnesses and sixteen days to search the dorm room of the last man seen with her, and had not fully investigated earlier reports that Flores had harassed other students and staff. The family said it did not begin to understand the full extent of those failures until the university's apology. As of the latest reporting available to this channel, in the summer of 2025, that lawsuit was still being fought. On Wednesday, May 6, 2026, the San Luis Obispo County Sheriff's Office served a search warrant at the home of Susan Flores on East Branch Street in Arroyo Grande. Chris Lambert was the first to report it. Investigators brought ground-penetrating radar.
