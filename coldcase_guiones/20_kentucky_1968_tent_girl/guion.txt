On Friday, May 17, 1968, a man named Wilbur Riddle was walking the edge of U.S. Route 25, just north of Georgetown, Kentucky, looking for something most people never notice. He was looking for old glass insulators, the small glass caps that once held telephone and telegraph wires to the tops of wooden poles. People collected them. Riddle was one of those people. That morning, near the place where the old highway met the newer Interstate 75, he found something else. She was small, about five feet one inch tall. She weighed somewhere around 110 pounds. She had reddish-brown hair. Investigators guessed that she was a teenager, somewhere between 16 and 19 years old. Nobody knew her name. Nobody came forward to say that a daughter, a sister or a wife had not come home. Friday, May 17, 1968. Wilbur Riddle was not a policeman, not a reporter, not anyone whose job involved looking for trouble. Accounts of the case describe him as a working man, a well driller by trade, and like a lot of people in that era he had a hobby that took him out along the old roads. He collected glass insulators. If you have ever driven past an old line of telephone poles in the countryside, you may have seen them, or the places where they used to be. They are thick glass knobs, often tinted blue or green, that were screwed onto wooden pegs on the crossarms of the poles to hold the wires in place and keep the electrical current from running down into the wood. As telephone and telegraph lines were replaced and rebuilt in the twentieth century, many of those old insulators were left behind, dropped in the weeds beneath the poles, or taken down and discarded. To collectors, they were small treasures. To everyone else, they were invisible. And so, on that spring day, Riddle was searching the roadside along U.S. Route 25, north of Georgetown, near the place where the old highway met Interstate 75. He was looking down, scanning the ground for the glint of glass. That is the only reason he found her. It was the thick, durable kind of material used for tents and tarpaulins, the kind of canvas that can sit out in the weather for a long time and still hold together. It had been wrapped and tied around something. Riddle found that inside the canvas was the body of a young woman. She had been there long enough that her remains had begun to decompose. He reported what he had found, and the machinery of a death investigation in rural Kentucky in 1968 began to turn. In most murder investigations, the first question detectives ask is who the victim was. Everything else flows from that answer. Who she knew. Who she lived with. Who she argued with. Where she worked. Who saw her last. The identity of the victim is the doorway into the case. In the spring of 1968, investigators in Scott County were standing in front of a locked door with no key. They could not ask who her husband was, because they did not know she had one. They could not ask who her family was, because no family had come forward. They could not trace her last movements, because they did not know where she had lived. Every ordinary step of a homicide investigation began with a name, and they did not have one. It is worth being honest about how little of that first investigation survives in public accounts. The detailed police files from 1968 have not been published. What we have are the outlines, preserved in later reporting and reference accounts. We know that the remains were examined and the death was treated as a homicide. We know a description was released. We know no one claimed her. We know she was buried as an unknown. And we know that, in the years that followed, the case did not lead to an identification or an arrest. Beyond that, we will not pretend to know the names of tipsters, the details of interviews or the theories that detectives discussed among themselves, because those details are not in the public record we can verify. What we can do is look at the walls those investigators ran into, because the walls are documented, and they explain almost everything that happened next. The first wall was the missing-person report that did not exist. In 1968, the basic way to identify an unknown body was to compare it against people who had been reported missing. If a family in Lexington had gone to the police in December 1967 and said that a 24-year-old woman with reddish-brown hair, about five feet one, had not come home, a detective might have been able to make the connection. That report was never filed. The person living with her told her family she had left him for another man. Her family, hearing that story from far away, had no reason to file a report in a city where they did not even know her address. So when Scott County investigators went looking for a missing young woman who matched, there was nothing to find. The second wall was the age estimate. The description that went out described a girl of 16 to 19. In the late 1960s, teenagers ran away from home in large numbers, and a description like that would naturally point attention toward runaway girls, toward high schools, toward parents searching for daughters who had left home. It did not point toward a young married mother in her mid-twenties. A 24-year-old wife living with her husband would not have appeared on any list of missing teenagers. Even if someone had been carefully comparing the description against every report in the region, the age alone might have ruled her out. The third wall was time. The investigators believed the young woman had died relatively recently before she was found. In reality, she had last been known alive more than five months earlier. That meant that anyone looking for a match would naturally search among women who had gone missing in the spring of 1968. Bobbie had vanished in the winter of 1967. Once again, the clue and the truth were looking in different directions. The fourth wall was geography and bureaucracy. Lexington and Georgetown are only about a dozen miles apart, but they sit in different counties, with different sheriffs, different coroners and different filing cabinets. In 1968, there was no shared computer system that could take the description of an unknown woman in Scott County and automatically compare it with records in Fayette County, let alone in Illinois, where her family lived. The FBI's new National Crime Information Center, launched the year before, was designed for wanted persons and stolen property. A file for unidentified bodies would not be added to it until the 1980s. Information moved by telephone, by teletype and by mail, and it moved only when someone thought to send it. The fifth wall was the lack of any science that could speak for her. In 1968, there was no DNA testing. Fingerprints only help if the person's prints are on file somewhere, and if the remains still allow prints to be taken. Dental records only help if you already have a name, so you know which dentist to ask. Facial sketches only help if the right person sees them. The police sketch of the Tent Girl ended up carved onto her headstone in Georgetown, but the people who would have recognized her face were in Illinois and in other states, far from Georgetown. Now put all five walls together. A woman not reported missing. An age estimate pointing at teenagers. A time-of-death estimate pointing at the wrong season. A county line between where she lived and where she was found. And no science to fill the gap. It is not hard to see how a case like this went cold. There is something else that made the walls even higher, and it is the most uncomfortable fact in this chapter. The one person who could most easily have connected the dots, the man who had been living with Bobbie in Lexington, was not in the investigators' field of vision at all. As far as any public account shows, no one in 1968 knew to ask him a single question about the woman in the canvas, because no one knew she had been his wife. His explanation to her family, that she had run away with another man, was never tested against the facts, because no one in authority knew there was anything to test it against. That is not an accusation. George Earl Taylor was never charged with any crime in this case, and he is presumed innocent. It is simply a description of how invisible this case made everyone connected to it. The victim had no name. Her family had no idea she was dead. And the people around her had no reason, in the eyes of the law, to be asked anything at all. And so, as the summer of 1968 arrived, the investigation slowed. In the same weeks, the country was absorbing another shock, when Senator Robert Kennedy was shot in Los Angeles in early June. The Tent Girl story faded from the newspapers. The headstone went up in the cemetery. The case file, whatever it contained, went into a drawer. In a small Kentucky community, the young woman in the green canvas became something between a tragedy and a legend, a story told and retold, with the facts slowly wearing smooth like an old coin. The cost of all this can be measured in time. Every month that passed was another month in which a baby grew up without her mother. Another month in which a family in another state believed a story that was not true. Another month in which whoever wrapped that canvas around her went on with life. The months became years. That kind of search does not begin in a single day. It is built out of years of wondering. In Lexington, or wherever life took them, Bobbie's daughter grew up. She had been about eight months old when her mother vanished. She would have been starting school in the early 1970s, entering her teens by the end of that decade, becoming an adult in the 1980s. We are not going to say her name, and we are not going to speculate about her childhood. She is a private person, and she was a baby when this happened. But it is worth noting what the arithmetic means. By the time anyone learned what had happened to her mother, the baby girl from 1967 was a woman of about 31. And in the family of the man who found the body, the story never went away. Wilbur Riddle told it. According to later accounts, the discovery stayed with him long after the official investigation had gone quiet. His daughter, Lori, grew up hearing about the young woman in the green canvas whom nobody had claimed. To her, it was a family story, the strange and terrible thing her father had found beside the road. Meanwhile, the world outside was changing in ways that would one day matter to this case, though no one in Georgetown could have known it. Let us keep a ledger of those years, because the numbers tell the story better than adjectives. In 1968, a young woman was found and could not be named. Zero missing-person reports matched her. The estimated age was 16 to 19. The real age was 24. The estimated time of death was recent. The real gap since she was last known alive was more than five months. The distance between where she lived and where she was found was about a dozen miles. Her family lived in other states entirely, and had been told she ran away. In 1983, fifteen years after she was found, the FBI added a file for unidentified persons to its national crime computer system, so that police agencies could, for the first time, enter descriptions of unknown bodies into a shared national system. It was an important step. But it depended entirely on agencies entering old cases, on families filing reports, and on someone running the comparisons. It was not open to the public. A sister in Arkansas could not log in and look. In 1984, sixteen years after she was found, a British geneticist named Alec Jeffreys, working at the University of Leicester, discovered that DNA could be used to tell one human being from another with extraordinary precision. Within a few years, DNA evidence was being used in criminal investigations, first in England and soon in the United States. It was a revolution. But in its early form, it needed good quality samples, and it needed something to compare against. A body that had been buried as an unknown for years, with no name and no family sample, was still beyond its reach. Scientists were also learning to read a different kind of DNA, a small separate set of genes that every person inherits only from their mother. That kind of DNA would become one of the most important tools for identifying old remains, especially bones and teeth. In the 1990s, forensic laboratories in the United States began using it in casework. We will come back to exactly how it works, because it is the science that would finally confirm who Tent Girl was. And then there is the year 1987, which is where two threads in this story cross in a way that is almost impossible to believe. In October 1987, George Earl Taylor, Bobbie's husband, the man who had told her family that she had left him for another man, died of cancer. He was never charged in connection with his wife's death. He was never tried. He was never convicted of anything related to her. He died without anyone in authority ever confronting him with the knowledge that the young woman buried as Tent Girl was his wife, because at that point no one in authority knew it. Whatever he knew, he took with him. Had Bobbie lived, she would have turned 44 that September, only weeks before he died. Nineteen years and five months had passed since the green canvas was found beside Route 25. In all that time, as far as any public account shows, no one had ever stood at the Tent Girl's grave knowing her real name. And in the very same month, October 1987, on Halloween night, the other thread began. Wilbur Riddle's daughter, Lori, was spending the evening with a group of friends. It was the kind of night for telling stories, and she told them one that was real. She told them about the dead woman her father had found almost twenty years earlier, wrapped in canvas by the side of a Kentucky highway. Among the friends listening that night was a teenager named Todd Matthews. He was 17 years old. Think about that timing. In the same month that the man many would later suspect died and took his secrets with him, the young man who would one day uncover the truth heard the story for the first time. One door closed. Another opened. Neither of them knew the other existed. Most teenagers who hear a ghost story on Halloween forget it by the next morning. Todd Matthews did not. Something about the young woman in the canvas, a girl with no name and no family and a sketch on a headstone, stayed with him. He wanted to know who she was. Not in a casual way. In the way that becomes a mission. The next year, according to one account of his life, he married Lori Riddle. The man who had found the Tent Girl became his father-in-law. The family story became his family story. And over the next decade, that 17-year-old would turn into one of the most determined amateur investigators in America. But in 1987, he had no badge, no training, no laboratory and no database. He had a question. And in the years ahead, he would discover that the most powerful tool for answering it had not yet been invented. It was about to be.

To understand how the Tent Girl got her name back, you have to understand two very different inventions. One of them lived inside a home computer. The other lived inside a laboratory. Neither of them existed when Wilbur Riddle found the green canvas in 1968. By 1998, both of them were ready, and the story of this case is the story of how one ordinary man brought them together. Let us start with the first one, because it is the one that made all the difference. In the early 1990s, most Americans had never heard of the internet. It had grown out of government and university computer networks, and for years it had been a tool for scientists and engineers. Then, in the middle of the decade, it opened up to everyone. Web browsers made it possible to click from page to page instead of typing commands. Companies began selling home internet service. Families bought computers, plugged them into the telephone line and listened to the modem screech and hiss as it dialed in. Suddenly a person sitting at a kitchen table in a small town could read pages written by people thousands of miles away. And people began to use this new space for something no one had planned. They used it to look for the missing. Families who had lost someone, a runaway son, a sister who stopped writing, a father who walked out one day and never came back, began posting descriptions of them online. Some posted on bulletin boards and message forums. Some posted in early online classified sections. Some built simple web pages of their own. At the same time, a few people began collecting descriptions of the unidentified dead, the John and Jane Does buried in county cemeteries across America, and posting those online too. For the first time in history, the two halves of the puzzle could exist in the same place. On one side, descriptions of people who had vanished. On the other side, descriptions of people who had been found but never named. Think about why this mattered so much in a case like the Tent Girl. Her identification had failed for 30 years because the information that could have matched her was trapped in separate places. Her description sat in a file in Scott County, and on a headstone in Georgetown. Any memory of her disappearance sat with a family scattered across several states. The official systems that existed did not connect them, and the public could not see into those systems at all. The internet did something those systems could not. It let anyone compare anything. It did not need a detective to authorize a search. It did not care about county lines. A worried sister in Arkansas and a curious young man in Tennessee could read the same words on the same night. The second invention lived in the laboratory, and it is a little more technical, so let us take it slowly. Every cell in your body contains DNA, the long chemical instruction manual that makes you who you are. Most of that DNA is stored in the nucleus, the control center of the cell. You inherit half of it from your mother and half from your father, and the particular combination is unique to you. That is the kind of DNA that most people think of when they hear the term, the kind that can point to one individual. But there is another, much smaller set of DNA in your cells, and it lives somewhere else. Inside each cell are tiny structures called mitochondria. You can think of them as the cell's power plants. They turn food into the energy that keeps the cell alive. And they carry their own small loop of DNA. Here is what makes mitochondrial DNA so special for identifying the dead. First, a single cell can contain hundreds or even thousands of copies of it, compared with just one set of nuclear DNA. So even when old remains have broken down and most of the DNA has been destroyed, there is often enough mitochondrial DNA left to read, especially in dense, protected tissues like bone and teeth. Second, it is inherited in a very simple way. You get your mitochondrial DNA only from your mother. She got hers from her mother, and so on, back through the generations. A mother passes the same mitochondrial DNA to every one of her children, sons and daughters alike. That means that two sisters born to the same mother carry, in almost every case, the same mitochondrial DNA. It is like a family signature passed down the maternal line, unchanged from one generation to the next. There is an important limit, and it is worth being honest about it. Mitochondrial DNA cannot single out one person the way nuclear DNA can. Everyone who shares the same maternal line, brothers, sisters, a mother, her sisters, their children, can carry the same sequence. So a mitochondrial match does not say this is definitely the person. It says this person is consistent with belonging to that family. Combined with other evidence, a missing-person report, a matching description, a matching timeline, it can turn a strong suspicion into a confident identification. And a mismatch is powerful too, because it can rule a family out. In the 1990s, this science had already proved itself in some extraordinary cases. In the early part of the decade, scientists used mitochondrial DNA to help confirm the identity of remains believed to belong to Russia's last royal family, the Romanovs, who had been executed in 1918. One of the reference samples came from Prince Philip, the husband of Queen Elizabeth the Second, who was related to the Russian empress through his maternal line. If a technique could reach back across most of a century to a family murdered in 1918, it could certainly reach back 30 years to a young woman buried in Kentucky. And in that same year, 1998, only a couple of months after the Tent Girl was identified, mitochondrial DNA testing would be used to identify the remains of the Vietnam War serviceman who had been buried in the Tomb of the Unknowns at Arlington National Cemetery. The technique was arriving everywhere at once. But here is the catch, and it is the reason science alone could not solve this case. DNA can only compare. It needs two things. It needs a sample from the unknown person, and it needs a reference sample from someone who might be related to her. The Tent Girl's remains were in the ground in Georgetown, and they could be reached. But who should the laboratory compare them to? Without a name, there was no family. Without a family, there was no reference sample. Without a reference sample, the most advanced DNA laboratory in the world was helpless. That is the lock at the center of this case. The laboratory had the key, but it had no idea which door to open. Someone had to find the family first. Someone had to look at a description of a missing woman and say, this could be her. That someone would not be a scientist. It would be a young man with a home computer, a decade of stubbornness and a question he had carried since a Halloween night in 1987.

Todd Matthews was not a detective. He was not a lawyer, not a scientist, not a journalist. By the late 1990s he was a young man living in Livingston, Tennessee, a small town in the hills of the Upper Cumberland, and the newspapers that later told his story described him as a factory worker. He had a job, a wife and a life that had nothing to do with police work. What he also had was the Tent Girl. Ever since that Halloween night in 1987, when he was 17 and Lori Riddle told the story of what her father had found beside a Kentucky highway, the young woman in the green canvas had stayed in his mind. He married Lori, and Wilbur Riddle, the man who had found the body, became his father-in-law. That meant the story was no longer something he had heard once. It was part of the family he had joined. He could hear it from the man who had lived it. For about ten years, Matthews tried to find out who she was. In the beginning, there was no internet to speak of, at least not for ordinary people in small Tennessee towns. According to later accounts, his early research was done mostly the old way, in person, by asking, reading and following up on whatever leads he could find. It was slow. It was frustrating. Most of it went nowhere. And there was no official reason for any of it. Nobody was paying him. Nobody had asked him to do it. The case was not his job, and to the official world, it was an old, cold mystery that had long since stopped producing news. Then, in the mid-1990s, Matthews heard about the internet. He understood something about it very quickly, something that many professionals took years to grasp. This was not just a new way to send letters or read the news. It was a place where people who were looking for each other could finally meet. A description posted in Arkansas could be read in Tennessee. A question asked in Kentucky could be answered in California. He saw it as a tool, and he started to use it. In 1997, he built a website dedicated to the Tent Girl. In those days, building a website was not something you did with a few clicks. It took effort and patience. On that site, he laid out what was known about her. Where she was found. When she was found. Her estimated age, her height, her weight, her reddish-brown hair. The sketch. The grave. The details that had been carved into stone in Georgetown three decades earlier were now available to anyone, anywhere, who happened to come across them. And at the same time, he searched. He went through the early online spaces where families were posting about people they had lost, reading description after description, comparing each one against what he knew about the woman in the canvas. Most did not fit. The age was wrong, or the date, or the height, or the place. No search engine of that era could do this for him. There was no program that could line up two descriptions and flag a match. It was a man, a screen and a list of strangers, each of them somebody's missing son or sister or mother. It is easy to tell this part of the story quickly. It was not quick. Each comparison was a small act of attention, repeated over and over, with no guarantee that the answer was out there at all. Then, in January 1998, according to CBS News, he found it. Some accounts place it a little later, in February, but they agree on what it was. It was a posting on an early online listing for missing persons, written by a woman named Rosemary Westbrook, who lived in Benton, Arkansas. She was trying to find her sister. Her sister was Barbara Ann Taylor. Bobbie. And according to the news coverage that followed, Bobbie had disappeared from Lexington, Kentucky, in late 1967. According to one account, Rosemary had posted about her sister before and had heard nothing back. Thirty years of searching, and the internet had been silent, too. We do not have the full text of that posting today, and we will not reconstruct it. But the outline was enough. A young woman. Last known in Lexington, about a dozen miles south of the place where Wilbur Riddle had found the green canvas. Last known in late 1967, months before the body was discovered in May 1968. According to news coverage of the case, the description Rosemary gave matched the appearance described on the Tent Girl's headstone. Stop for a moment and think about what that means. For 30 years, the description of the Tent Girl had been carved in stone in Georgetown. For 30 years, the description of Bobbie Taylor had lived in the memory of her sister. They described the same woman. But they had never been in the same place, in front of the same pair of eyes, until a factory worker in Tennessee sat down and read them both. Matthews did not call a press conference. He did not announce that he had solved the case. He wrote to Rosemary Westbrook. He emailed her, and told her about the young woman found in Scott County, Kentucky, in May 1968. Consider where Rosemary stood in that moment. For three decades, her family had lived with a story, that Bobbie had left her husband and run off with another man. A story like that leaves room for hope. If she left, she might still be alive somewhere. And then a stranger wrote to tell her that a young woman matching your sister's description was found dead beside a Kentucky highway only months after she disappeared, and that she had been lying in a grave marked Tent Girl ever since. We do not have Rosemary's own words from that moment, and we will not invent them. But we know what she did next. She did not dismiss it. She did not walk away. She was willing to give a sample of her own DNA so that the question could be answered for certain. According to CBS News, investigators would later compare samples of Rosemary's saliva with cells taken from the dead woman's teeth and bones. That is courage of a particular kind. It is the courage to risk learning that the hope you have carried for 30 years, the hope that your sister was alive somewhere, was false all along. The information went to the authorities in Scott County. A missing woman from Lexington. An unknown woman found near Georgetown. A sister willing to be tested. For the first time since 1968, investigators had what they had never had before. A name to test. A family to compare. A door, and a key that might fit it. But a matching description is not proof. Many young women in 1967 had reddish-brown hair and stood around five feet one. To be certain, the Tent Girl would have to be lifted from the grave where she had rested for three decades, and the message her mother had written into every cell of her body would finally have to be read.

On Monday, March 2, 1998, almost 30 years after she was buried as an unknown, the Tent Girl was lifted from her grave in the Georgetown Cemetery. An exhumation is never a small thing. It requires legal permission, careful planning and people who understand that they are handling not evidence, but a human being. The remains were taken to a laboratory in Frankfort, the Kentucky state capital, where they were examined by Dr. Emily Craig, a forensic anthropologist. A forensic anthropologist is a scientist who reads the story written in human bones. From the shape of the pelvis, the growth of the bones, the condition of the joints and the teeth, they can estimate a person's sex, age and height, and sometimes old injuries or illnesses. In 1968, the young woman in the canvas had been described as a girl of 16 to 19. When Dr. Craig looked at the remains in 1998, according to one detailed account of the case, she estimated an age at death of between 20 and 30. It was a small revision on paper. In reality, it changed everything. A woman of 20 to 30 was exactly the kind of woman who could be Bobbie Taylor, who had been 24 years old when she disappeared. For three decades, the official description had been pointing at a teenager. Now, the bones themselves were saying something different. But a better age estimate is still not a name. The next step was the one that science had not been able to take for 30 years. According to that same account, samples from the remains, part of an arm bone including the elbow joint, and the lower jaw with its teeth, were sent to a DNA laboratory in the Raleigh-Durham area of North Carolina. There, the DNA in them would be compared with the reference sample from Rosemary Westbrook. Remember the science we talked about earlier. Inside the bones and teeth of the Tent Girl, protected for decades, were mitochondrial DNA molecules, the small family signature passed down from mother to child. If the woman in the grave was Bobbie, then her mitochondrial DNA should match the mitochondrial DNA of her sister Rosemary, because they had the same mother. Some accounts describe the reference as a cheek swab. CBS News described it as a sample of Rosemary's saliva. One account says DNA was taken from the pulp inside a tooth. The details vary from source to source. The result does not. According to CBS News, investigators compared the sister's sample with cells taken from the dead woman's teeth and bones, and found a match. On its own, a mitochondrial match says that the woman in the grave belonged to the same maternal family as Rosemary Westbrook. But it did not stand alone. It sat alongside a sister's report of a woman missing from Lexington in late 1967, a body found about a dozen miles away in May 1968, a matching description and a revised age estimate that now fit. Together, they pointed in only one direction. On April 23, 1998, the Scott County Sheriff's Office announced that the young woman known for 30 years as Tent Girl had been identified. One account gives the date of the announcement as April 22. Her name was Barbara Ann Hackmann Taylor. Bobbie. She was 24 years old when she disappeared. She was a wife, a mother, a daughter and a sister. She had a name all along. It had simply been waiting. Thirty years, almost to the month, after Wilbur Riddle found her beside Route 25. The announcement made news far beyond Kentucky. A woman nobody could name for three decades had been identified, and the breakthrough had come not from a crime laboratory's database but from a factory worker in Tennessee and an online posting by her sister. Later writers would call it one of the first cold cases ever solved with the help of the internet, and some would call it the very first. Now we have to talk about the question that the identification could not answer. Who killed her? Once the Tent Girl had a name, she had a past. And her past led straight back to Lexington in late 1967, and to the man she had been living with there. Her husband, George Earl Taylor. Here is what the record shows. George Earl Taylor was a carnival worker. He and Bobbie had moved to Kentucky in 1967 without telling her family in Illinois. When she disappeared, he did not report her missing. Instead, according to her family and to later reporting, he told them that she had left him for another man. After she was identified, police named him as the prime suspect in her death, according to reference accounts of the case. CBS News reported that many people suspected he may have killed his wife during an argument. Todd Matthews himself came to believe that Taylor was responsible. And now, here is what the record also shows, and it matters just as much. George Earl Taylor was never charged in connection with Bobbie's death. He was never arrested for it. He was never tried. He was never convicted. He could not be, because he had died of cancer in October 1987, more than ten years before anyone knew that the Tent Girl was his wife. A man who was never charged and never convicted is, in the eyes of the law, innocent. Suspicion is not proof, and no court ever heard any evidence against him. That leaves this case in a painful place. There was no interview, because the man described as the prime suspect was already dead. There was no confession. There was no trial, no testimony, no verdict and no sentence. There was no moment in a courtroom where Bobbie's sisters could stand up and say what her loss had cost them. The legal system never reached the question of who wrapped her in that green canvas. It did not fail at the final step. It never got to take it. The family made their own statement in the only way left to them. When they commissioned a new marker for Bobbie's grave, they did not include the name Taylor. The stone would carry the name she had been born with, Hackmann, the name she had shared with Rosemary and her other sisters. It is a quiet decision, carved in stone, and it says more than any press release could. As of today, no one has ever been charged with the murder of Barbara Ann Hackmann Taylor. The case remains, officially, unsolved. The man described as the prime suspect died more than a decade before his wife was identified, and he is presumed innocent. And yet something did change in April 1998, something no court could have given her. For 30 years she had been a description and a nickname. Now she was a person again, and her family knew, at last, that she had not abandoned anyone. She had not run away. She had been taken. There was one more thing left to do. Bobbie had been buried once already, by strangers, under a name that was not hers. In a few days, she would be buried again, and this time, her family would be there.

On Saturday, April 25, 1998, two days after the announcement, Barbara Ann Hackmann Taylor had a funeral. For the first time since the winter of 1967, she was surrounded by people who knew who she was. The next day, a newspaper in Cookeville, Tennessee, the Herald-Citizen, ran a story about the young man from Livingston who had made it possible. Reporter Tracie LeFevre described Todd Matthews, then 27 years old, and the roughly ten years he had spent trying to put a name to the woman his father-in-law had found. And she wrote a single line that holds the whole story inside it. That search concluded only yesterday in Georgetown, Ky., at the funeral of Barbara Hackmann Taylor. Her family could have taken her home. They could have moved her to Illinois, or to Arkansas, or anywhere else. Instead, they chose to have her laid to rest again in the Georgetown Cemetery, in the town that had looked after her when no one else knew she was gone. And they made another choice, one that is still visible to anyone who visits. They did not remove the old headstone. The stone with the police sketch and the words Tent Girl was kept. A new base was placed beneath it, carrying the name she was born with, the name her family chose, without her husband's surname. Today the two stones stand together, one on top of the other. The upper one tells you what the world called her for 30 years. The lower one tells you who she really was. It may be one of the most honest grave markers in America. It does not hide the years she spent without a name. It answers them. In 1998, three of her sisters were named in coverage of her death: Rosemary Westbrook of Benton, Arkansas, Jan Daigle of Placerville, California, and Marie Copeland of Arundel, Maine. Rosemary was the one who had kept posting, kept asking and kept hoping, and in the end, she was the one whose own DNA carried her sister home. For Todd Matthews, the Tent Girl was not the end. It was the beginning. He had seen, with his own eyes, what could happen when the description of a missing person and the description of an unidentified body were placed where ordinary people could read them both. He had seen how many families were still posting into the silence, and how many John and Jane Does were still lying in graves marked with numbers, nicknames and question marks. In 1999, a website called the Doe Network went online, built around exactly that idea. It collected cases of missing and unidentified people and put them where anyone could compare them. By 2001, it had grown into an organization of volunteers, and in July 2011, it became an official nonprofit. Todd Matthews is widely described as one of its co-founders. Over the years, volunteers working with information like the cases on the Doe Network have helped bring names back to people who had been nameless for decades. Then the federal government came calling. Years later, Matthews told NBC how the story had carried him there. It took 30 years, and ultimately, I was called to the Department of Justice to develop the NamUs program in 2007. NamUs is the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System, a national database, funded by the federal government, that lets families, police, medical examiners and coroners search missing-person and unidentified-remains cases side by side. In other words, it is a national, official version of exactly what Todd Matthews had been doing by hand from a small town in Tennessee in the 1990s. In 2011, he joined the program's management team, and he later served as its director of case management and communications, before leaving that role in early 2020. NBC summed up his journey in a single headline: Solving Tent Girl Case, Factory Worker Turns Missing Persons Expert. Think about the chain of events that leads from a roadside in Kentucky to a federal database. A man looking for glass insulators. A Halloween story. A teenager who would not let it go. A website. A sister's post. An email. A grave opened. A name restored. And from that name, a network of volunteers, and a national system that is still used today. In 2018, fifty years after she was found, her story was told again on national television, in an episode of the series Who Killed Jane Doe, a title that is itself the question no court has ever answered. By then, Matthews had spent more than thirty years with this case, longer than Bobbie herself had been alive, and Wilbur Riddle's discovery had become one of the founding stories of a whole movement of volunteer investigators. Many questions remain. Where was Bobbie during the months between her disappearance in late 1967 and the day she was found in May 1968? Where did the green canvas come from? Who tied it, and who drove it to that stretch of road near the interstate? The man described as the prime suspect died in October 1987, never charged, never tried, never convicted, and presumed innocent under the law. Unless new evidence appears, those questions may never be answered in a courtroom. But one question has been answered, and it was the one that mattered most to the people who loved her. Who was she? She was Bobbie. Let us go back, one last time, to where this began. Friday, May 17, 1968. A man walking slowly along the shoulder of U.S. Route 25, north of Georgetown, Kentucky, his eyes on the ground, looking for the glint of old glass in the weeds. He was looking for something small and forgotten that everyone else had driven past. That is exactly what he found. Not a piece of glass, but a person. Someone everyone else had driven past. Barbara Ann Hackmann. Born September 12, 1943, in Illinois. One of seven children. A sister to Rosemary, to Jan, to Marie. A wife. A mother to a baby girl. A young woman of 24 who moved to Kentucky and never came home. Twenty-four years alive. Thirty years without a name. Mistaken for a teenager by people who were trying their best. Buried by strangers under a nickname. Given back her name by her sister's DNA and a stranger's persistence. Her story leaves one lesson, and it is a simple one. A name matters. A missing-person report matters. If someone you love stops answering, if a story about why they left does not sound right, report it, and keep their description where others can see it. Today, families can search NamUs and volunteer sites like the Doe Network themselves. Somewhere in those records, there may be someone who has been waiting as long as Bobbie waited. It took 30 years, a factory worker and a sister who never gave up, but the woman in the green canvas has her name back, and it is carved in stone where the whole world can read it. And remember, no case stays cold forever.
