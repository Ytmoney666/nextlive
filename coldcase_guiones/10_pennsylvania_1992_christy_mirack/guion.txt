On Monday morning, December 21, 1992, a twenty-five-year-old sixth-grade teacher named Christy Mirack put on her coat and her gloves inside a small townhouse in East Lampeter Township, Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. Christmas was four days away. Her roommate had already left for work at seven o'clock. She never left. A little after nine that morning, her own principal, worried because she had not arrived, drove to her door and found it standing ajar. Inside, Christy Mirack was dead on her bedroom floor. She had been beaten, sexually assaulted and strangled. Whoever did it had walked into her home in the narrow space of a weekday morning and walked out again into an ordinary Lancaster County Monday. And he left something behind. Not a fingerprint on a glass. Not a name. Something smaller and far more patient. Traces of his own DNA, on her and on the carpet beneath her. To understand why that evidence could not solve the case in 1992, you need to understand what DNA could and could not do at the time. Think of a DNA profile as a kind of barcode. Every person carries a unique one, except identical twins. In the early nineteen nineties, crime laboratories could read enough of that barcode to compare two samples side by side. If police had a suspect, they could take a sample of his blood or his hair, read his barcode, and compare it with the barcode from the crime scene. If the two did not match, he could not be the man who left the evidence. That was a huge step forward. It meant innocent people could be cleared with scientific certainty. But it had a terrible limitation. The barcode from the crime scene was only useful if detectives had someone to compare it with. If the man who left it was not on their list, the barcode just sat there. It was like holding a key and having no idea which of a million doors in the world it would open. So in the days after Christy's murder, detectives had something extraordinary and something useless at the same time. They had the killer's genetic signature. And they had no idea whose it was. Christy's friends, the young women who had been her peers when she died, went on with their lives in the way she never could. The Lancaster newspaper would later write about them in a story whose headline said it all. Friends married, had babies, but have not forgotten Christy Mirack. Each wedding, each birth, each new job was a reminder of the life that had been interrupted. They grew older. In their memories, she stayed twenty-five. Then came the hardest loss since the murder itself. On November 4, 2002, Christy's mother, Gerry Mirack, died at her home in Shamokin. She was fifty-nine years old. She died one day after what would have been Christy's thirty-fifth birthday. Read that again slowly. The day before her mother died, Christy would have turned thirty-five. A mother lived almost ten years after her daughter's murder without an arrest, and then she was gone too, the day after her daughter's birthday. When the Lancaster newspaper wrote about her, the headline carried a phrase that would echo through this case for years to come. A mother's dying wish. Justice for Christy Mirack. She did not live to see it. That is the part of cold cases that statistics never capture. Every year without an answer is not just a year of paperwork. It is a year in which parents age, in which grandparents die, in which brothers and sisters carry something heavier than they should have to carry. Gerry Mirack waited almost ten years. Vincent Mirack would wait much longer. Meanwhile, the investigation did not disappear. It changed shape. In October 2008, the FBI added Christy's case to its website, in a section devoted to cases where the Bureau was seeking information from the public. An FBI agent had been assisting with part of the investigation, and putting the case online was one way to reach people who might never read a Lancaster newspaper. Christy Mirack's photograph now sat among other unsolved cases on a federal website, available to anyone in the world with an internet connection. It did not bring a name. In June 2009, Christy's brother Vince did something that would have been impossible when his sister died. He created a Facebook page under Christy's name. The goal was simple. Keep her face in front of people. Keep her story alive. Ask anyone who knew anything to come forward. Social media was still new then, and families of the missing and murdered were only beginning to discover what it could do. Vince was one of them. He was no longer the little brother in the front row of Christy's pretend classroom. He was the man standing up for her in public, year after year, refusing to let the world forget. There was one more development in those years, and it was the one that should have solved the case. Over time, a national system had been built to store DNA profiles. It is called CODIS, the Combined DNA Index System, run by the FBI. Think of it as a giant library of genetic barcodes. When someone is convicted of certain crimes, their profile goes into the library. When evidence is collected from an unsolved crime, its profile goes in too. And the computer constantly compares them, looking for a match. The DNA from Christy's crime scene was entered into that national database. And it came back with nothing. No match. Not one. That silence was itself a clue, though it was a frustrating one. It meant the man who killed Christy Mirack had never been entered into the system. His genetic barcode was simply not in the library. He was, in the language of investigators, a man with no profile on file. The most powerful forensic tool in America was looking straight at his genetic signature and could not tell who he was. So let us take stock, the way an honest ledger would. By the middle of the 2010s, the case of Christy Mirack looked like this. More than five hundred people interviewed in the first months alone. One prime suspect cleared by science. Three men ruled out by DNA. An acquaintance excluded by state police testing. A reward of ten thousand dollars, unclaimed. Three agencies, the township police, the Pennsylvania State Police and the FBI, all of which had worked the case. A posting on the FBI website. A Facebook page run by her brother. A national DNA database search with no hit. One mother, dead at fifty-nine, without an answer. And more than twenty years without an arrest. None of those numbers is a sign that people did not care. Each of them is a sign of how much they did. But together they describe a wall, and by 2016 the case had been pushed up against that wall for nearly a quarter of a century. In 2016, the case changed hands one more time. The Lancaster County Detectives, who work under the district attorney, took over jurisdiction. At the time, the district attorney was Craig Stedman. A county detective named Christopher Erb became deeply involved in the case. A new set of eyes was looking at the old file. That alone did not guarantee anything. Fresh eyes on an old case often see the same thing the old eyes saw. But this time, something was different. It was not the evidence that had changed. It was the world around it. Outside Lancaster County, in laboratories and on websites that had barely existed a few years earlier, a new kind of science was taking shape. Millions of ordinary people were sending samples of their own DNA to companies to learn about their ancestry. Some of them were uploading the results to public websites to find long-lost relatives. They were doing it out of curiosity, out of love for family history, out of a desire to know where they came from. None of them were thinking about a murdered teacher in Lancaster County. But one of them, without knowing it, was about to become the most important witness Christy Mirack ever had.

In November 2016, Detective Christopher Erb reached out to a company called Parabon NanoLabs, a private DNA technology firm based in Virginia. Parabon was offering something that sounded, to many people at the time, almost like science fiction. It claimed it could take a DNA sample from a crime scene and use it to predict what the person who left it might look like. The service is called DNA phenotyping. Here is the simplest way to understand it. Your DNA contains instructions for many of your physical traits. Some of those instructions influence the color of your eyes, the color of your hair, the tone of your skin, even certain features of the shape of your face. DNA also carries clues about ancestry, about which parts of the world a person's ancestors came from. Phenotyping reads those instructions and makes predictions. It is like a police sketch, except the witness is not a frightened neighbor who caught a glimpse in a parking lot. The witness is the killer's own genetic code. On March 7, 2017, Detective Erb sent Parabon a DNA sample that had been collected from the carpet at the crime scene in 1992. Pause on that date. The carpet had been waiting for twenty-four years, two months and fourteen days. The evidence collected by investigators that December morning, preserved through three agencies and more than two decades, was now traveling to a laboratory that could read it in ways no one in 1992 could have imagined. That is the value of evidence that is kept and kept properly. It does not age the way memories do. It simply waits for the world to catch up. Parabon analyzed the sample and created composite images. On October 31, 2017, District Attorney Craig Stedman released them to the public. There were three images, each showing how the man who left the DNA might have looked at a different age. One at twenty-five. One at forty-five. One at fifty-five. Three versions of the same unknown face, aging across the decades that he had been free. The district attorney's office also launched a website dedicated to collecting tips, called WhoKilledChristyMirack.com. For Christy's family, the composites meant something enormous. For twenty-five years they had lived with a blank space where the killer's face should have been. Now there was something to look at. Christy's brother Vince put it in four words that the local newspaper used as a headline. "We have a face now." Think about what it means for a family to say that. For a quarter of a century, the man who took their sister and daughter had been a shadow, a theory, a sample in a lab. Now he had features. Now, maybe, someone would recognize him. In December 2017, as the twenty-fifth anniversary of Christy's murder arrived, police and the family told the newspaper that they remained hopeful the killer would be found. Hopeful. After twenty-five years, that was the word they chose. But a composite image, no matter how scientifically produced, is still only a prediction. It is not a photograph. It cannot be matched against anything. The images did not, on their own, bring a name. As the newspaper later summed it up, the service had produced images, but no leads. And by the spring of 2018, the investigators were facing the same wall that every investigator before them had faced. They had the killer's genetic signature. They now had a predicted face. They still did not know who he was. Then, in April 2018, something happened on the other side of the country that changed everything for cold-case investigators in America. In California, investigators announced an arrest in the Golden State Killer case, one of the most notorious unsolved series of crimes in American history. The way they had found their suspect stunned the country. They had not found him through a national police database. They had found him through a public genealogy website, a place where ordinary people uploaded their DNA results to find relatives. This was a different kind of science entirely, and it is worth slowing down to understand it, because it is the engine of everything that happens next. It is called genetic genealogy, and in police work, investigative genetic genealogy. Here is how it works. Every person shares DNA with their relatives. You share about half of your DNA with each of your parents and with your full brothers and sisters. You share less with cousins, and less still with second cousins, third cousins and beyond. The amount of shared DNA tells you roughly how closely two people are related. Genealogy websites use this to connect relatives who have never met. If two people upload their DNA and share a certain amount, the website tells them they might be cousins. Now imagine uploading not your own DNA, but the DNA from a crime scene. The killer himself is not on the website. But perhaps his cousin is. Or his aunt. Or a sister he has never met. The website shows which of its users share DNA with the crime-scene sample, and how much. From there, a genealogist does the patient, painstaking work of building family trees backward and forward, using birth records, marriage announcements, obituaries and public documents, until the branches of those trees meet at a small number of people who could be the source. It is like solving a puzzle where you are given the edges first and must work your way toward the center. The national police database had failed in Christy's case because her killer had never been entered into it. Genetic genealogy did not need him to be entered into anything. It only needed someone in his family to have been curious about their own roots. On May 1, 2018, Detective Erb's phone rang. On the line was Thom Shaw, a forensic artist at Parabon NanoLabs, the same company that had created the composites. Shaw was not calling about the composites. He was calling to pitch a new service. Parabon was launching a genetic genealogy unit. It would take crime-scene DNA, compare it with DNA that people had voluntarily uploaded to public genealogy databases, and combine the results with census, vital and historical records to build out family trees. It was, in effect, an offer to try the method that had just made national headlines in California. District Attorney Stedman later described how his office saw that moment. Parabon, he said, was really their last shot. They did not feel they had any more options. Everything else had been tried. Five hundred interviews. Suspects cleared. A national database search with no hit. Composite faces with no name. If this did not work, it was hard to imagine what would. They said yes. Stedman would later say that the decision to use Parabon, the last shot, "has turned out to be our best." With the district attorney's authorization, Parabon took the genetic data from the crime-scene sample and uploaded it to a public genealogy database. In Pennsylvania, no one had ever made an arrest using information from genealogical websites. Lancaster County was about to be the first. What happened in the next few weeks moved faster than anything in the previous twenty-five years combined. And at the center of it was a genetic genealogist who was about to read a family tree all the way down to a single name.

Her name is CeCe Moore. In 2018 she had joined Parabon NanoLabs to lead its new genetic genealogy unit, and the case of Christy Mirack came to her early in that work. She was not a detective. She did not carry a badge or a gun. Her tools were a computer, a database of people who had chosen to share their DNA, and an enormous archive of public records, birth notices, marriage announcements, obituaries, newspaper clippings. And her method was patience. The crime-scene profile from the carpet in East Lampeter Township was uploaded to GEDmatch, a public genealogy database where people post their DNA results to find relatives. At that time, the site allowed this kind of comparison by law enforcement. The results came back with a list of people who shared DNA with the unknown man. Not the man himself. His relatives. Some of them distant. People who had no idea that a sample from a 1992 murder had just been compared with their own genetic code. Moore began to build. As she later described it on national television, she took those distant matches and built out their family trees, branch by branch, generation by generation, looking for the place where they connected. Her analysis also offered a clue about ancestry. The DNA suggested a man of mixed European and Puerto Rican descent. That narrowed the search. She focused on a target family in Lancaster with northern European roots and some Puerto Rican heritage. And somewhere in those branches, there was one match that would matter more than any other. One of the people who had uploaded DNA to the database was a half-sister of the man Moore was looking for. She had sent in her DNA for the most innocent reason imaginable, to learn about her own ancestry. And according to reporting on the case, he did not even know her. A half-sister he had never known, curious about her roots, had unknowingly placed the most important piece of the puzzle on the table. Then came the detective work in the archives. Moore searched Lancaster newspapers. In them, she found an engagement announcement for a man who was the son of a woman from the target family, carrying her maiden name, with a father whose name was Latin American. The pieces were moving toward each other. She found one more thing. An online magazine had once interviewed a local DJ about his love of Puerto Rican food, and in that interview he had talked about his Puerto Rican heritage. At that point, as Moore described it, she knew she had him. The tree had narrowed to a single branch. And the branch had a name. Raymond Charles Rowe. In Lancaster County, many people knew him by another name. DJ Freez. He was a disc jockey who ran an entertainment business under that professional handle. He was a popular wedding DJ. And according to reporting after his arrest, he was a regular at events for children, including school events. He had built his life, his business and his reputation in the same county where Christy Mirack was murdered. In 1992, by the math of the ages given in court reporting, he had been twenty-three or twenty-four years old. Now he was forty-nine. For twenty-five years, he had been playing music at other people's happiest days. That is the twist that stunned Lancaster when the news finally broke. The killer was not a drifter who had moved on. He was not a monster in hiding. He was a familiar face, a man with a microphone and a playlist, the person families hired to make their celebrations go smoothly. But here is where the story slows down again, and it is important that it does. A family tree is not evidence of guilt. It is a lead. Genetic genealogy can point to a person, but it cannot, on its own, prove anything. District Attorney Craig Stedman was clear about this later. "We can't arrest based on what we got from Parabon," he said. CeCe Moore said the same from her side of the work. "No arrests are made on our work alone." To charge Raymond Rowe, investigators needed his DNA, collected directly, compared directly with the evidence from 1992. They could not simply ask him for a sample. If he was the killer, asking would warn him. So they did what investigators in these cases do. They waited for him to leave his DNA somewhere they could legally collect it. The Lancaster County Detectives worked with the Pennsylvania State Police Cold Case Unit, and an undercover operation began. Investigators learned that Rowe had an upcoming job. On Thursday, May 31, 2018, he was booked to DJ a party at Smoketown Elementary School in the Conestoga Valley School District. It was a celebration for Smoketown students who were moving on to Conestoga Valley Middle School. Now one detail must be made very clear, because it is often repeated incorrectly. Smoketown Elementary was not Christy's school. Christy had taught at Rohrerstown Elementary in a different school district on the other side of Lancaster. There is no evidence in the court records or in the reporting reviewed for this video that Raymond Rowe ever worked as a DJ at Rohrerstown, or that his work connected him to Christy's school in any way. The irony is real enough without exaggeration. A young sixth-grade teacher had been murdered in 1992. And twenty-five and a half years later, the man who killed her was working as the DJ at a party for elementary school children about to move up to middle school, while undercover officers watched. According to the charging documents, investigators at the event noticed Rowe drinking from a Kirkland-brand water bottle. When the party was ending and he was getting ready to leave, he threw away a water bottle and a piece of chewing gum. After he was gone, investigators retrieved them. That was it. That was the moment. Not a chase. Not a confession. A man throwing away his trash at the end of a job. The bottle and the gum were taken to a Pennsylvania State Police crime lab for DNA testing. Analysts extracted his DNA and compared it with the evidence that had been collected in December 1992. The DNA matched. It matched the DNA found on multiple locations of Christy Mirack's body. It matched the DNA from the carpet underneath her body. The piece of carpet that had waited in storage for a quarter of a century had finally found the man it belonged to. The whole process, from the genealogy work to identifying Raymond Rowe, had taken just over a month. Just over a month, after twenty-five years. Think about the people who had worked on this case over the years. The officers of 1992 who had secured the scene. The investigators who had conducted five hundred interviews. The FBI agents. The state police scientists who had kept testing and excluding. Every one of them had played a part in keeping that evidence safe enough to speak. And think of Gerry Mirack, who had died in 2002 with her dying wish unfulfilled. She did not live to hear the name. Investigators now had a match, a name, and a man who had no idea that the gum he had tossed away at a children's party had just ended twenty-five years of freedom. All that was left was to go and get him.

On Monday, June 25, 2018, investigators arrested Raymond Charles Rowe at his home. According to the district attorney's office, the arrest was made without incident. Lancaster County Detective Christopher Erb charged him with criminal homicide in the death of Christy Mirack. He was forty-nine years old. He was not eligible for bail. Twenty-five years, six months and four days had passed since the morning Christy put on her coat and gloves. That same day, District Attorney Craig Stedman stood before reporters and announced the arrest. It was, as the coverage noted, the first time in Pennsylvania that prosecutors had been able to make an arrest because of data from genealogical websites. The news traveled far beyond Lancaster. National networks picked it up. And the headlines all seemed to reach for the same two objects. A water bottle. A piece of chewing gum. For the people of Lancaster County, though, the shock was not the science. It was the name. DJ Freez. Many of them knew him. He was known as a popular wedding DJ. He had worked school events. Local reporting described him as a regular at events for children. A man who had been part of the soundtrack of so many family celebrations was now charged with raping and murdering a young teacher a quarter of a century earlier. Christy's family in Shamokin received the news they had waited twenty-five years for. Her mother was not there to hear it. Her father, Vincent, was. Her brother Vince, who had built the Facebook page in 2009 and told reporters in 2017 that the family finally had a face, now had a name. The legal process moved forward quickly. A preliminary hearing was scheduled for July 9. In July 2018, prosecutors filed additional charges against Rowe. Three counts of rape by forcible compulsion. Two counts of involuntary deviate sexual intercourse by forcible compulsion. And one count of burglary, a charge that in Pennsylvania reflects entering a home to commit a crime. Then, in August 2018, District Attorney Stedman announced that his office would seek the death penalty. Christy's brother Vince said publicly that he wanted the death penalty too, and that he wanted to understand why this had happened. For a few months, it seemed the case was heading toward a long and painful capital trial. Then, on Tuesday, January 8, 2019, Raymond Rowe walked into a Lancaster County courtroom and pleaded guilty. He pleaded guilty to first-degree murder, to rape and to the related counts, seven counts in all. He was fifty years old. The plea was part of an agreement. In exchange, the death penalty was taken off the table. The district attorney's office said the agreement was reached after discussions that included the Mirack family. Prosecutors said they still believed the death penalty was warranted. But a plea meant certainty. It meant no trial, no years of appeals over a death sentence, no chance of a jury verdict going the wrong way. President Judge Dennis Reinaker sentenced Raymond Rowe to life in state prison without the possibility of parole. On top of that, he imposed a consecutive sentence of sixty to one hundred and twenty years. Rowe's own defense attorney, Patricia Spotts, told the court plainly what her client had told her. "He has told us that he did it," she said. And she added, "The person standing beside me has admitted his guilt." Then Rowe spoke to the Mirack family. According to reports from the courtroom, he said, "I can't imagine what you are going through. I apologize." He told Christy's father that he was sorry. And then came the moment that reporters in that courtroom described. Vincent Mirack, Christy's father, was offered the chance to address the court. He had waited more than twenty-six years for this day. He had buried his daughter and then his wife. And when the moment came, he could not do it. He collapsed, weeping, into the arms of his daughter Alicia. "I can't talk," he said. "I can't talk." Vince Mirack spoke on behalf of the family. And District Attorney Stedman acknowledged the question that everyone in Lancaster was asking. Why? Why had Raymond Rowe done this? Prosecutors offered no motive. "Considering the time that has past," Stedman said, "some specific questions about motive might never be answered publicly." The next day, on January 9, 2019, the national television program 60 Minutes Overtime aired a segment in which CeCe Moore explained to correspondent Steve Kroft how she had used the crime-scene DNA to find the killer. The case was described as one of the first convictions to come from this kind of genetic genealogy work. It should have ended there. It did not. In November 2019, an episode of the television series On the Case with Paula Zahn aired about the Mirack murder. Rowe would later say that he learned things about the investigation from that episode that he had not known before, and that this was part of why he wanted to withdraw his guilty plea. On November 21, 2020, Rowe filed a post-conviction petition asking for new DNA testing. In August and September of 2021, on August 26, September 2 and September 8, he got his hearings in a Lancaster County courtroom. And there, the man who had told his own lawyers he did it, who had apologized to the Mirack family in open court, told a completely different story. He testified that he had met Christy at the Chameleon Club, where he said he was working as a DJ, and that they had begun a secret affair. He claimed that they had consensual sex on the morning she was murdered, and that she was alive when he left. Someone else, he said, must have killed her. His attorney, Todd M. Mosser, asked him directly, "Are you averring your actual innocence?" Rowe answered, "Yes, I am." He also attacked his former defense lawyers, saying they had been, quote, "more like a prosecution team coming in every day and telling me to plead, plead, plead." Those lawyers took the stand too. Patricia Spotts and David Blanck testified that Rowe had confessed to them at their very first meeting and said he wanted to plead guilty. A DNA expert for the prosecution testified that the samples had, at most, two contributors. Raymond Rowe. And Christy Mirack. In April 2022, Judge Reinaker issued a sixteen-page ruling denying Rowe's petition. He found Rowe's claim, that he and Christy had consensual sex and someone else killed her, unbelievable. He also denied Rowe's request for new DNA testing of two items from the scene, the sweater used to strangle Christy and the wooden cutting board used to beat her. Rowe appealed. In 2023, the Pennsylvania Superior Court upheld the judge's decision. But the fight did not stop there. On August 1, 2025, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court vacated that appellate decision and sent the case back to the Superior Court, ordering it to reconsider Rowe's request for DNA testing in light of a newer ruling interpreting Pennsylvania's broadened post-conviction DNA testing law. In 2026, the Superior Court issued a new opinion on remand. Reporting indicates that Rowe again sought review from the Pennsylvania Supreme Court, and the final outcome of that latest round could not be confirmed for this video. What can be stated with certainty is this. Raymond Rowe pleaded guilty. As of the latest reporting available for this video, his conviction has not been overturned. He is serving life in prison without the possibility of parole. And the Mirack family, who thought they had reached the end of a twenty-six-year road in January 2019, found themselves back in courtrooms, listening to the man who killed Christy tell a story that a judge called unbelievable.

When Raymond Rowe pleaded guilty in January 2019, the people who had carried Christy Mirack's memory for twenty-six years reacted in ways that were complicated and deeply human. The Lancaster newspaper spoke with some of them, including her former principal and former students, and the headline it chose captured two feelings at once. "We needed this." Relief. And then, right beside it, a question that would not go away. Why? Relief because, for twenty-six years, the people who loved Christy had lived with a blank space. They had lived with the knowledge that the man who did this could be anywhere, could be anyone around them. Now they knew. He had a name and a face and a prison sentence. He would never be free again. And the question, because knowing who did it is not the same as knowing why. Prosecutors never offered a motive. The district attorney said publicly that some questions about motive might never be answered. Rowe himself, after apologizing in court, later took the stand to deny the crime entirely and tell a story about a secret affair that a judge found unbelievable. So the one person who could explain what happened on that Monday morning has offered only an apology, and then a version of events that the court rejected. The why remains locked inside him. The newspaper's editorial board wrote about it as well, with a headline that many people in Lancaster probably felt in their bones. We still struggle to understand the inexplicable, but Christy Mirack finally gets justice. Both halves of that sentence are true. Justice came. Understanding did not. And then there is what the case changed. The arrest of Raymond Rowe was the first arrest in Pennsylvania built on information from genealogy websites. Along with a handful of other cases from 2018, it showed the entire country what this new tool could do. But it also raised hard questions about privacy. The people who upload their DNA to genealogy websites do it to find relatives, not to help police. Rowe's half-sister had uploaded her DNA to learn about her ancestors. She did not know him, and she had no idea that her curiosity would help identify him. In 2019, GEDmatch, the database used in this case, changed its rules. Users would now have to opt in before their DNA could be used in law-enforcement searches. The Lancaster newspaper reported on that change directly, connecting it to the case of the man who killed Christy Mirack. It is a debate that continues to this day. On one side is the right of ordinary people to control their own genetic information. On the other is the right of families like the Miracks to finally know who took someone they loved. This story does not settle that debate. But it does show, very clearly, what is at stake on both sides. And there is one more change, a quieter one, that this case represents. For twenty-five years, the evidence from Christy's bedroom floor could only answer one question: is it this man, yes or no? Every time the answer was no, an innocent man was spared, but the killer stayed free. Genetic genealogy turned that question around. Instead of asking whether a suspect matched the evidence, investigators could finally ask the evidence to point toward a suspect. The carpet sample that had been waiting since 1992 did not change. The world around it did. Now let us go back, one last time, to where this story began. It is Monday morning, December 21, 1992, in a townhouse at 2071 William Penn Way, in Greenfield Estates, East Lampeter Township, Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. Christmas is four days away. Her roommate has left for work at seven o'clock. A twenty-five-year-old sixth-grade teacher named Christy Mirack has finished getting ready. She has put on her coat. She has pulled on her gloves. In a few minutes she will walk out to her car and drive across the county to Rohrerstown Elementary School, where a classroom of sixth graders is waiting for the teacher with the biggest smile. She is dressed to leave. She never leaves. That image, a young woman in her coat and gloves, ready for the day, is the image this case has carried for more than thirty years. It is the image her principal found. It is the image investigators described. It is the image the charging documents used a quarter of a century later. And it is the image her family has had to live with ever since. So let us say clearly what her life was, before it was a case. Christy Ann Mirack was a daughter of Shamokin, a child of the coal region. She was the little girl who played school with her brother and sister and always stood at the front of the room. She was a Millersville University graduate, class of 1989, who got the job she had dreamed about since childhood. She was a sixth-grade teacher at Rohrerstown Elementary, genuine, unpretentious, very outgoing and friendly, in the words of the principal who knew her. She was the teacher with the biggest smile, in the words of the students who remembered her. She was a friend whose friends married and had babies and never forgot her. She was a daughter whose mother's dying wish was justice. She was a sister whose brother kept her face on the internet and in the newspapers for decades, and whose sister held their father up in a courtroom when he could not speak. And she was a woman who fought back. Twenty-five years old. Four days before Christmas. Dressed to leave. Here is what this case teaches, if it teaches anything. Evidence that is collected carefully and kept faithfully can outlive the limits of its own time. The officers who collected that carpet sample in 1992 could not have known what it would one day reveal. They kept it anyway. The scientists who excluded suspect after suspect could not name the killer. They kept testing anyway. The family that waited could not make the science arrive any faster. They kept asking anyway. And a stranger, a half-sister curious about her own roots, without ever meaning to, gave the evidence the one thing it had been missing for twenty-five years. A direction. Raymond Rowe was forty-nine when he was arrested and fifty when he pleaded guilty. For all those years, he had stayed in Lancaster County, working weddings and children's events, as though the morning of December 21, 1992 had never happened. He did not get away with it. Not in the end. It took twenty-five years, six months and four days to arrest him. It took a little over a month of genealogy work, one children's party, one water bottle and one piece of chewing gum. And Christy Mirack, who wanted nothing more than to teach, ended up teaching a whole country something about patience, about family, and about the truth that waits.
