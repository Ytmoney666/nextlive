On Monday, February 25, 1957, a young college student made his way through a stretch of woods and brush along Susquehanna Road, in the Fox Chase section at the far northeastern edge of Philadelphia. It took its name from Eugène François Vidocq, a nineteenth century French criminal turned detective who is often described as one of the fathers of modern criminal investigation. Bring together retired detectives, medical examiners, profilers and scientists, meet over lunch, and offer their expertise, free of charge, to the families and police departments stuck on cases that had gone cold. It is hard to imagine a case that fit their mission more perfectly than the boy in the box. And by the late 1990s, several of the people closest to the case were part of it. Bill Kelly, the fingerprint man who had inked the boy's prints in 1957, had joined. So had Joseph McGillen, who had worked as an investigator for the Medical Examiner's Office. So had Sam Weinstein. Kelly and McGillen would go on to serve as co-directors of the society's Boy in the Box investigation. They were, in a very real sense, old men finishing the work of their youth. On the police side, the case had found a new champion. Tom Augustine, a Philadelphia homicide investigator, had asked to be assigned to the boy's case. According to CBS News, he turned to Sam Weinstein and the Vidocq Society for help. And together, in 1998, they did something that had not been done in the forty one years since the boy was buried. They went to court and obtained an order to exhume his body. The Vidocq Society helped pay for it. Their reason was a technology that had not existed when the boy died. DNA. By the late 1990s, forensic scientists could extract genetic material from old remains. They took a tooth. From it, they recovered what is called mitochondrial DNA. Think of mitochondrial DNA as a family heirloom passed down only through mothers. Your mother has it, her mother had it, and so on, unchanged, for generations. It is very good at answering one kind of question. Is this person related, through the maternal line, to that person? But it is very bad at answering the question investigators actually needed answered. Who is this? In 1998, there was no giant database of mitochondrial profiles waiting to be searched. And the sequence they obtained was limited. According to later accounts, it was enough to rule out one possibility that had haunted investigators for years, that the boy might be Steven Damman, a child who had disappeared on Long Island in 1955. But it could not tell them who he was. The 1998 exhumation brought one other change, and for the people who loved this case, it may have been the more important one. The boy was not returned to the potter's field. Instead, he was reburied at Ivy Hill Cemetery in the Northwest section of Philadelphia, in a donated coffin, under a new granite headstone. The original stone, the one that read Heavenly Father, Bless This Unknown Boy, was kept at the grave. The Vidocq Society later added a bench. And every year, people came. The society and others held memorial services at the grave. Visitors left flowers. People who had worked the case and people who had simply read about him stood together around the stone of a child none of them had ever known. Then, in February 2002, a new voice entered the case. It came by way of a psychiatrist in Ohio, who contacted Philadelphia authorities about a patient. The woman has been identified in accounts of the case only by pseudonyms, most often Martha, or simply M. She was living in the Cincinnati area. And she told a story that she said she had carried for decades. According to her account, her mother had acquired a young boy from his birth parents in 1954. She said the boy lived in their home, that she knew him as Jonathan, and that her mother eventually killed him and took his body to Fox Chase, where it was left by the side of a road. We are not going to repeat the details of what she alleged happened in that house. What matters for this story is that her account included details that the police had not widely released. She described a meal of baked beans. She described a bath. The autopsy had found beans in the boy's stomach and fingers wrinkled as if by water. The woman had a history of mental illness. Meanwhile, the boy's story was spreading far beyond Philadelphia. That same year, in California, a new kind of science had just caught one of the most notorious criminals in American history. And it was about to come for the boy's bones.

On April 24, 2018, police in Sacramento County, California, arrested a seventy two year old former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo. Investigators said he was the Golden State Killer, a man responsible for a series of murders and rapes across California in the 1970s and 1980s. For more than forty years, the case had been one of the most famous unsolved mysteries in the United States. What cracked it was not a confession, or a witness, or a fingerprint. It was a family tree. To understand how Joseph Augustus Zarelli got his name back, you need to understand what happened in that California case, because it changed everything. So let us take a few minutes and walk through the science, as simply as we can. Every human being carries DNA in almost every cell of the body. Most of that DNA is the same from person to person. That is what makes us human. But scattered through it are hundreds of thousands of small spots where people differ, a single letter of genetic code that might be one thing in you and another thing in me. Scientists call these single nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs, pronounced snips. Here is the everyday version. Imagine your DNA as a book of three billion letters. Almost every copy of that book in the world reads the same. But in a few hundred thousand places, there is a typo, and the pattern of typos in your copy is unique to you. You inherited half of those typos from your mother and half from your father. Your siblings share many of them. Your cousins share fewer. Your second and third cousins share fewer still, but still enough to be measured. That is exactly what home ancestry tests read. When someone spits into a tube and mails it off to learn whether they are Irish or Italian, the lab reads their SNPs and compares them with everyone else in its database. If two people share long matching stretches, the system reports that they are probably related, and estimates how closely. Now imagine you are a detective with the DNA of an unknown person. If you can turn that DNA into the same kind of SNP profile, and if you are legally allowed to compare it against a database where people have chosen to share their results, you will not find the unknown person himself. But you might find his cousins. And once you find his cousins, a genealogist can do something that is part science and part old fashioned detective work. Using birth records, marriage records, obituaries, census sheets and newspaper archives, the genealogist builds each cousin's family tree backward, to the ancestors they share, and then forward again, branch by branch, until the trees meet at a family that could have produced the unknown person. That is forensic genetic genealogy. It is slow, careful work. A single family tree can grow to hundreds of names, and every link has to be checked against a document, because one wrong assumption can send investigators down the wrong branch for months. The DNA points to a family. Only paper, and patience, can point to a person. In the Golden State Killer case, investigators uploaded a profile to a public genealogy site called GEDmatch, found distant relatives, and built trees until they narrowed in on one man. After April 2018, police departments across the country began asking a new question about their oldest files. Do we have any DNA left? In Philadelphia, the answer was yes. But it was a very difficult yes. The boy's remains had been in the ground since 1957. Bone that old breaks down. The DNA inside it fragments into tiny pieces and becomes contaminated with the DNA of bacteria and soil. Getting a clean SNP profile from it is a little like trying to read that book of three billion letters after it has been left out in the rain for sixty years and torn into confetti. In the fall of 2018, the first attempts to extract usable SNP data from the samples collected in 1998 failed. The DNA was simply too degraded. So investigators made a decision that tells you how seriously they now took this possibility. They would go back to the grave. On April 24, 2019, under a court order, the boy was exhumed for the second time. If that date sounds familiar, it should. It was exactly one year, to the day, after the arrest in California that had proved the method could work. The remains were examined and new bone samples were taken, in the hope that they would yield better DNA than the material collected two decades earlier. And this is where a new hero enters our story. Her name is Colleen Fitzpatrick. Fitzpatrick did not start out as a detective. She earned a doctorate in physics and worked in optics before turning to genealogy. She founded a company called Identifinders International, and in 2017 she co-founded the DNA Doe Project, a volunteer organization that uses genetic genealogy to identify unknown remains. By the time the Philadelphia police brought her in, she had worked on some of the hardest identification cases in the country. None of them, by her own account, was harder than this one. She would later call it the most challenging case of her whole career. The problem was not the genealogy, at least not at first. The problem was the DNA itself. According to Identifinders, the work involved a collaboration of international experts. Specialists worked to coax a usable profile from the fragile bone. Fitzpatrick would later say it took two and a half years just to get the DNA in shape to begin the genealogy. Two and a half years. Think about what that means in the context of this case. The boy had already waited sixty two years by the time he was exhumed in 2019. Now the people trying to name him had to wait again, through the spring of 2019, through the pandemic year of 2020, into 2021, while scientists worked on fragments of very old bone. Every month, the question was the same. Is it enough yet? Here is something important to understand about this kind of work, because it is the reason the science finally applied to this case when nothing else had. Every other method investigators had tried depended on someone, somewhere, having already recorded the boy. Fingerprints only work if prints are on file. Footprints only work if you find the right hospital record. Missing person files only work if someone reports a child missing. Even mitochondrial DNA only works if you have a relative to compare it to. Genetic genealogy is different. It does not need the boy to be in any database. It only needs his relatives to be in one, people who might not even know he ever existed, who simply wanted to learn about their own family history. Somewhere, without knowing it, a relative of Joseph Augustus Zarelli had already done the one thing that would make his identification possible. And by 2021 and 2022, the science had finally caught up with the bones. The profile was ready. The cousins were waiting. The only question left was whether a genealogist could follow their branches all the way back to a little boy who had been born in Philadelphia in 1953, and who had left no other trace that anyone had ever found. That was the moment the case stopped being about technology and started being about people again.

The breakthrough, when it finally came, did not arrive in a single dramatic moment. It arrived the way most real breakthroughs do, in layers, one careful step after another, each step checked before the next was taken. And the people who took those steps were not famous. They were genealogists at their keyboards and detectives in the Philadelphia Police Department's Homicide Unit, working a file older than almost anyone in the building. Here is how it unfolded, as police and Identifinders later described it. Once Colleen Fitzpatrick's team had a usable SNP profile from the boy's bones, it was compared with genealogy databases where people had chosen to share their DNA. And this time, there were matches. Not to the boy himself, of course. To relatives. People alive today who shared enough DNA with him to be family. The Philadelphia Inquirer would later report that some of those family members had been found because of an at-home DNA test kit. Somewhere, someone curious about their own roots had sent off a sample, never imagining that it would one day help name a child killed in 1957. The matches pointed to the boy's mother's side of the family. That gave the genealogists a starting point. From there, they did what genealogists do. They built the trees. Every match had parents, and grandparents, and great grandparents. Every one of those people had left traces in the records. Birth announcements. Marriage licenses. Obituaries listing the survivors. Census pages listing everyone in a household. Branch by branch, the trees were drawn backward until they met at shared ancestors, and then forward again, looking for a woman of the right age, in the right place, at the right time, who could have given birth to a boy in the early 1950s in Philadelphia. And eventually, they found her. Detectives were able to identify a woman they believed was the boy's mother. Then the investigation did something that genealogy alone could never do. It went to court. Detectives obtained a court order, signed by a judge of the Court of Common Pleas, directing the state to produce the birth, death and adoption records of every child born to that woman between 1944 and 1956. Think about that request for a moment. In 1957, Bill Kelly had gone from hospital to hospital, comparing footprints by hand, hoping that one drawer in one building might hold the boy's record. Sixty five years later, the detectives knew exactly which records to ask for. They did not need to search the whole city. They needed one family, and one window of years. According to police, the records came back with responsive results. Among them were birth certificates for two children born to the mother who were already known to investigators. One of those children had already provided a DNA sample. And then, there was a third record. A birth certificate for a boy, born in 1953. It listed the mother. And it listed a father. Joseph Augustus Zarelli. Born January 13, 1953. There, on a piece of paper that had been sitting in state records since the year Eisenhower took office, was the name that four hundred thousand flyers, hundreds of officers, a psychic, a foster home theory, a woman in Ohio, a relative in Memphis and two exhumations had never been able to find. The boy in the box had a name. He had always had one. It is worth pausing on how the pieces fit together, because neither half would have worked alone. The DNA could say, this family.
