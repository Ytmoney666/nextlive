Before we meet the woman who broke this case open, we need to understand the thing she unlocked. So what, exactly, was waiting in that freezer? Because the Brown's Chicken case is often described as a DNA case, and that is true, but it is true in a way that is easy to misunderstand. Many of the cold cases solved in recent years were cracked by genetic genealogy, by building family trees from the DNA of distant cousins in public databases. This was not one of those. This case was solved by something older and simpler, and in a way more human. The science could not find the killer. It could only recognize him, once someone else pointed. So let us start with the meal. When a person eats, saliva touches the food. Saliva is not just water. It carries cells from the inside of the mouth, and every one of those cells holds a complete copy of that person's DNA, the genetic instructions that make each of us who we are. Bite into a piece of chicken, and you leave a trace of yourself on the bone. You do not see it. You do not think about it. But it is there. Now, DNA left on food at room temperature does not last forever. Bacteria grow. Moisture breaks things down. Over days and weeks, the genetic material can degrade until there is nothing useful left. Freezing slows all of that down. It is the same reason you put food in your freezer at home. Cold preserves. That is why Jane Homeyer's decision to keep that meal, and the decision to freeze it about five days after the murders, mattered so much. Without those choices, the saliva on the chicken might have been worthless within weeks. With them, it could wait. Next, the profile. When a lab tests a sample like that, it does not read the entire genetic code. It looks at a small number of specific places in the DNA where people tend to differ from one another. Think of it as a barcode. Not your whole life story, just a short pattern of numbers that is, for practical purposes, unique to you. If two barcodes are identical at every place the lab examines, they almost certainly came from the same person. If they differ in even one place, they did not. In 1998, when the Illinois State Police crime lab tested the food, an analyst found that barcode. In fact, she found more than one. The food carried what scientists call a mixture, DNA from more than one person. Her analysis indicated that three or more people's DNA could be on the chicken, and that at least one of them was male. That is not surprising. Food in a restaurant passes through hands. It is cooked, served, carried. One profile was the main contributor, the one prosecutors would later tie to the person who had eaten from the meal. Another was weaker, a minor profile. And here is the crucial point. A barcode, by itself, is just a pattern. It does not come with a name attached. To find out whose barcode it is, you need a second sample, called a reference sample, taken from a known person. You put the two side by side and compare them. That is what DNA could do in 1998. That kind of comparison had been reshaping criminal justice for about a decade. In the late 1980s, DNA comparisons began to appear in courtrooms in Britain and in the United States, first to convict the guilty, and soon also to free people who had been wrongly convicted. By the late 1990s, it was routine. It could compare. It could include or exclude. It could tell you with enormous confidence whether a particular person was the one who ate that chicken. But it could not tell you who to compare it with. The national database that went live in October 1998 offered one possible shortcut. If the person who ate that chicken had ever been convicted of certain crimes and had his DNA entered into the system, a match might appear on a screen. But that only works if the person is already in the database. For the person who ate that chicken, no such match ever appeared. So the barcode sat there, the freezer kept the meal, and the case stayed cold. This is the gap that so many cold cases fall into. The science has done its job. The evidence is preserved. The profile is known. And the investigation still cannot move, because no one knows whose name to put next to the barcode. Genetic genealogy would later offer one way across that gap, by searching for relatives instead of the person himself. But in 2002 that technique was still years away from being used to solve murders. In the Brown's Chicken case, the gap would be crossed a different way. By a witness. Once investigators had a name, the process was straightforward. They needed a reference sample from the person they suspected. Then the lab would compare it with the profile from the chicken. Either it matched or it did not. If it did not match, the suspect was very likely not the person who ate that meal, though he could still have been involved in some other way. If it did match, it would place him at the scene, eating from a dinner from the restaurant's own kitchen on the night of the murders. There is one more idea worth understanding, because it would come up again and again in court. When a DNA profile matches, scientists can calculate how rare that profile is, how likely it would be for a random, unrelated person to have the same barcode by chance. In this case, prosecutors would later cite a figure of one in two point eight trillion for the relevant population. One in two point eight trillion. There are fewer than nine billion people alive on Earth today. A number like that is the reason DNA evidence is so powerful in a courtroom. But numbers like that only measure one thing, the rarity of the match. They do not answer every other question about evidence. How was it handled. Was it contaminated. Were the samples kept properly. In this case, those questions would be raised loudly. The swabs taken from the food during testing were later lost. The defense would argue that the chicken had been thawed again and again over the years, and that people had handled it without gloves. And the weaker, minor profile on the food would never be identified at all. Defense lawyers would point to that unidentified profile and ask the jury to consider who else might have touched that meal. Science, in other words, is powerful, but it is not magic, and it is never the whole story. In the Brown's Chicken case, the DNA would become one strong pillar of the prosecution. It would not stand alone. It would stand next to confessions, and next to the testimony of the women who had known the truth for nine years. Remember what Dr. Henry Lee had said in 1994. The half-eaten dinner would be the key. He was right that it was a key. But a key is useless without a door, and without someone willing to put it in the lock. For more than nine years, the key sat in a freezer, waiting. And in late March 2002, a woman who had carried a secret since January 1993 finally turned to a friend and said she knew who had done it.

Her name was Anne Lockett. In later years, in news coverage and court records, she would also appear under the name Anne England.
