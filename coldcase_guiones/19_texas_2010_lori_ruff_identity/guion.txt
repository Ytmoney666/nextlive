The name change in Dallas. The rapid chain of documents in the summer of 1988. And the blank wall before it. Then the newspaper did something unusual. Somebody out there, the paper reasoned, might recognize a face, a handwriting, a word. Newspapers usually report on investigations. Here, a newspaper was inviting its readers to join one. The response was enormous. The story was picked up and republished by news outlets around the world. Across the internet, in the forums where amateur investigators gather to work on unidentified persons and missing persons cases, the case of Lori Ruff became a sensation. Soon after publication, O'Hagan described the situation to Poynter, the journalism institute, in an email. So far, we've gotten a lot of response, but they haven't cracked the case yet. That sentence would remain true for a long time. The case was also listed in NamUs, the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System, the federal database where unidentified people and missing people are recorded so that families and investigators can try to match them. It is a strange place for a woman with a marriage certificate and a funeral. But that is where her file sat. Officially, Lori Erica Ruff was a known person with an unknown identity. Think about the numbers as they stood in June 2013. The woman in the strongbox had been dead for two and a half years. She had been Lori for twenty five years. Becky Sue Turner had been gone for forty two years. In Longview, a little girl was growing up without her mother, and without even knowing her mother's real name, because nobody knew it. In Seattle, Joe Velling kept working. Later reporting would describe him as a former Social Security investigator, yet he was still on the case. And somewhere in Pennsylvania, though no one in Texas knew it, a family was living through its own long silence, with no idea that the daughter they had last seen as a teenager had died in East Texas on Christmas Eve. This is the point in a story like this where it is worth stopping to count. Not to blame any single person, but to see clearly how a woman could stay hidden for so long. Here is the ledger. In 1971, a two-year-old girl died in a fire in Fife, Washington. Seventeen years later, her birth certificate was issued to someone else, a young adult, without anyone noticing that the child had died. Over twenty seven days in the summer of 1988, that certificate produced an Idaho photo ID, a Dallas court order, a federal Social Security number and a Texas ID card. The following spring, it produced a Texas driver's license. For twenty two years after that, a woman lived, studied, worked, married and raised a child under the name that sequence created. In all that time, no agency connected Lori Erica Kennedy to the death of Becky Sue Turner. It took a locked box, opened by grieving relatives with a screwdriver, to reveal the truth. And even then, it took about two years of federal investigation to establish the paper trail, without producing a name. That is not a story about one bad clerk. It is a story about a system that, in that era, was not built to catch this. Birth records and death records sat in separate files in separate states. A court that grants a name change is not asked to investigate the past of the person standing before it. Each office did its part correctly according to the rules of the time, and together they created a person who had never existed before 1988. In the decades since, many states and federal agencies have worked to connect birth and death records more closely, which is general context about the system and not something specific to this case. But none of that helped in 1988. And none of it could answer the question that mattered most in 2013. Not how. Who. There is another painful side to the ledger. On the other end of this story was a family who had lost a teenager in 1986. Whatever had happened between them, they had not seen her in decades. The reporting does not make clear whether a missing person report was ever filed for her. But if anyone had searched, every search would have ended in the same place. Because the young woman they were looking for no longer existed under the name they knew. She had become someone else. A family can search for a daughter for the rest of their lives, and never find her, if she is living under a name they have never heard. The web sleuths did not find her either. They built theories and chased leads from across the country. None of them led to the truth. The crowd could carry the story to readers around the world, and it did, but it could not do the one thing that was needed. It could not reach inside her family's blood. But the Seattle Times story did reach one person who could. Among the readers who followed the case online was a woman in California who had spent her career, first in physics and then in forensic genealogy, solving problems that other people had given up on. She had been following the case, and that same month, June 2013, she talked with Joe Velling. Her name was Colleen Fitzpatrick. Her approach did not depend on the papers in the box. It depended on DNA. And she was about to try something that, in 2013, very few people had ever attempted on a case like this.

Colleen Fitzpatrick did not start out as a genealogist. She was trained as a physicist, a scientist who spent her early career on precise measurements and hard problems. Over time, she turned that same discipline toward a very different kind of puzzle: identifying people. She became what is known as a forensic genealogist, someone who uses family history records and, increasingly, DNA to put names to the unnamed. Before this case was finished, she had already worked on others that seemed impossible. In 2015, she was part of the effort, alongside the genetic genealogist CeCe Moore, that identified a man known as Benjamin Kyle, who had lived for years without knowing who he was. She knew what was possible when ordinary records failed. And she had been following the case of Lori Ruff online. In June 2013, the month the Seattle Times story appeared, she talked with Joe Velling. What she proposed would become the heart of this story. To understand it, we need to step away from Texas for a moment and talk about what is inside every one of us. Every person carries DNA, the long chemical instruction book inside our cells. Most of that book is identical from one human being to the next. But scattered through it are hundreds of thousands of tiny spelling differences, places where one person has one chemical letter and another person has a different one. Scientists call these single nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs for short. Think of them as typos that run in families. You inherited your typos from your parents, and they inherited theirs from their parents. Now, the part of our DNA that matters most here is called autosomal DNA. It is the DNA we inherit from both parents, roughly half from our mother and half from our father. And each generation, it is shuffled. So you share about half of your autosomal DNA with each parent, and with each full brother or sister. You share about a quarter with a grandparent, an aunt or an uncle. With a first cousin, someone who shares a set of grandparents with you, you typically share around an eighth. With second cousins and third cousins, the amount keeps getting smaller, but it is measurable. Genealogists measure shared DNA in units called centimorgans, but the idea is simple. The more DNA two people share, the closer their relationship is likely to be. In the late 2000s and early 2010s, several companies began selling tests directly to the public that read those hundreds of thousands of SNPs. People bought them to learn about their ancestry, or to find distant cousins. Over the following years, millions of people took them. And as they did, those companies and the databases connected to them became something nobody had planned. They became a giant map of family relationships across America and beyond. If an unknown person's DNA could be placed into one of those databases, it might match some of her cousins. And from cousins, using ordinary family trees, birth notices, obituaries and marriage records, a skilled genealogist could work backward to the shared ancestors, and then forward again to find a family with a missing member. Today, this is known as investigative genetic genealogy. Most people first heard of it in April of 2018, when it was used to identify the man accused of being the Golden State Killer in California. But this case came before that. In 2013, when Fitzpatrick began working with Velling, using consumer DNA to identify an unknown person was still new and experimental. Later, in a presentation to the American Academy of Forensic Sciences in 2017, Fitzpatrick would describe the approach in this case as high-density SNP testing borrowed from the genetic genealogy community. In other words, she took the tools that hobbyists were using to find cousins, and she turned them into a forensic tool for finding a name. There was, however, a serious problem. Lori was gone. She had died in December 2010, and her family had said goodbye at the memorial in January 2011. If there was no usable sample of her DNA available, how could anyone test her? The answer was standing in Longview. It was her daughter. Remember that a child inherits about half of her DNA from her mother and about half from her father. So the little girl carried within her a large part of her mother's genetic code, including all those family typos passed down from the grandparents, great grandparents and cousins her mother had never mentioned. The trick was to separate the two halves. And for that, the father's DNA was the key. According to the reporting, the identification relied on direct-to-consumer autosomal DNA analysis of Blake Ruff and his daughter. Here is the logic, step by step. Test the daughter. Her DNA will match relatives on both sides of her family. Test the father. His DNA will match relatives on his side only. Now compare the two lists. Any relative who matches the daughter but does not match the father must be related to her through her mother. In one move, the father's DNA filters out his whole side of the family tree, and what remains is the hidden half. The mother's half. The half that Lori had kept locked away. If that sounds abstract, picture a guest list for a family reunion that a child has been invited to, with every relative she has on both sides. Now hand her father the same list and ask him to cross out everyone he is related to. The names left uncrossed are her mother's people. Nobody needs to know their names in advance. The subtraction does the work. Think about the quiet irony of that. A woman who had worked for twenty two years to keep her family out of her life had passed that family down to her own child, in every cell of her body. The secret she kept in a strongbox, she had also, without knowing it, written into her daughter. That is what this channel means when it says that no case stays cold forever. Paper can be forged. Names can be changed in court. But nobody can choose the DNA they pass on. The method still had limits. A match is not an answer. It is a clue. A DNA match to a distant cousin can lead to dozens or hundreds of possible family lines, each of which has to be traced through records, generation by generation. The closer the match, the easier the work. A third cousin might take months of tree building. A first cousin is far closer to the target, because a first cousin shares grandparents with the person you are trying to find. And in 2013, the databases were much smaller than they are today. Every year, more people tested. Every year, the odds of a close match improved. So the work was patient, and it was slow. Fitzpatrick and Velling were not chasing a suspect. There was no court date, no deadline, no reward. There was only a family in Texas who wanted to know, and a little girl who deserved one day to know. Week after week, the work depended on ordinary people somewhere in America sending off a tube of saliva out of simple curiosity about their own family history. Somewhere among those people, without knowing it, was a close relative of the woman in the strongbox. And once that relative's DNA was in the system, the wall that had stood since 1988 was about to crack.

Two and a half years passed between that first conversation in June 2013 and the moment everything changed. Two and a half more Christmases. The little girl in Texas went from toddler to schoolchild. The web sleuths moved on to other cases, though some never stopped talking about this one. And all the while, the DNA results sat in the databases, quietly comparing themselves against every new person who tested. Then, in December of 2015, Colleen Fitzpatrick found what she had been waiting for. The DNA that the Ruff family had submitted showed a close match, one that pointed straight at the hidden half of the family tree. According to the reporting, the match indicated that Lori had a first cousin. Not a fourth cousin, not a distant branch requiring months of guesswork through old census records. A first cousin. Someone who shared a set of grandparents with the woman in the strongbox. That cousin belonged to a family named Cassidy, from the Philadelphia area of Pennsylvania. It was almost exactly five years since that Christmas Eve in Longview. For the first time since the box was opened, the mystery had a direction on the map. Not Texas. Not Idaho. Not Washington State. Pennsylvania. Fitzpatrick's work told Velling where to look and which family to approach. A first cousin meant that one of Lori's parents was likely a brother or sister of one of the cousin's parents. Somewhere in the Cassidy family, there should be an aunt or an uncle who had lost touch with a daughter decades earlier. Fitzpatrick suggested that Velling reach out to the Cassidys. A first cousin narrows a search dramatically. A single set of grandparents usually has a limited number of grandchildren, a few, a dozen, perhaps a few dozen in a very large family. Among them, the investigators were looking for one person in particular: a girl who would have been born in the late 1960s, who had a parent from this same family, and who had dropped out of her family's life sometime before the summer of 1988. On paper, it was a short list. In real life, it was a family's most painful memory. But DNA alone, at that stage, was still a lead and not a name. To turn it into a name, someone had to go and knock on a door. That is old-fashioned investigating. And that is what Joe Velling did. In March of 2016, Velling flew from Seattle to Philadelphia, all the way across the country. He was not carrying a warrant. He was not there to arrest anyone. Picture what that visit meant for the family on the other side of the door. A stranger arrives from the other side of the country. He explains that he has been investigating the death of a woman in Texas, a woman who lived under a false name. He explains that DNA suggests she is related to them. She had been born in Philadelphia, to James and Deanne McLean. Her mother's maiden name was Cassidy, which is why the DNA had pointed to that family. She had a sister.
