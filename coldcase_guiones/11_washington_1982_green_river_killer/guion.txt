To understand what happened in 2001, it helps to understand, in plain words, what DNA evidence actually is and why it took so long to work in this case. Every human body is made of cells, and almost every cell carries a complete copy of a person's genetic instructions, a long chain of chemical letters called DNA. Most of that chain is the same in every human being. That is what makes us human. But in certain places along the chain, there are short stretches where the letters repeat, like a word typed over and over, and the number of repeats is different from one person to the next. One person might have a stretch that repeats seven times on one side and twelve on the other. Someone else might have nine and ten. Forensic scientists look at a set of these places at once. Taken together, the numbers form something like a barcode. With enough places compared, the chance that two unrelated people would share the same barcode becomes astronomically small. That barcode is what investigators mean by a DNA profile. To use it, you need two things. You need a profile from the evidence, built from traces left at a crime scene or on a victim. And you need a profile from a known person, built from what is called a reference sample, something that came from that person and no one else. Blood, a cheek swab, a hair with its root. Or saliva soaked into a piece of gauze. If the two barcodes match, the evidence and the person are connected. If they do not, the person is excluded. It is that simple to describe and, in the 1980s, it was enormously difficult to do. The first DNA methods, developed in England in the mid-1980s, needed relatively large amounts of fresh material. A small trace, or a sample that had been sitting in a package for years, often gave a result that was too weak or too damaged to read. Through the 1990s, that changed. Laboratories adopted a new generation of methods, built around copying tiny amounts of DNA many times over until there was enough to read, and then reading those short repeating stretches. Suddenly a speck of material that would have been useless in 1987 could produce a complete profile. Old evidence that had been written off began to talk. Across the United States, cold case units began to go back to their evidence rooms. In October of 1998, the FBI's national database began operating nationwide, so that profiles from crime scenes in one state could be checked against profiles in another. And in King County, under a sheriff who had once stood in the Green River, the decision was made to go back to the earliest evidence in the Green River case and try again. That is the cruel and beautiful thing about DNA in a case like this. It does not care how much time has passed, as long as someone has taken care of it. It does not get tired. It does not retire. To replace them, investigators turned to the newest tool in the long history of this case, the same one this channel has followed in so many other stories. It is called forensic genetic genealogy. In plain words, it works like this. Scientists build a detailed genetic profile from the remains, far more detailed than the barcode used to match a suspect. When the remains share DNA with distant relatives, genealogists can build family trees backward and forward until only one missing person fits.
