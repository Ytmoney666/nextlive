He went on to Sam Houston State University in Huntsville, Texas, a school well known across the state for training people who go into criminal justice. And he told Texas Monthly years later what his plan had been. He wanted to become a police officer, get promoted to detective, get his hands on Carla's files, and find her killer himself. Think about a boy making that plan. Not a fantasy about being a hero. A practical plan, step by step. School. Badge. Promotion. File. Name. It is the kind of promise a child makes when the adults around him have run out of answers. Whatever turns his own working life took over the decades, Jim never let go of the second half of that promise. He kept calling. For years, Jim Walker regularly called the Fort Worth Police Department's cold case unit to ask whether anything new had happened in his sister's case. And for years, according to Texas Monthly, the answer was the same. Nothing new. We will call you if there is a break. Picture that in numbers. A phone call. A polite answer. A promise. Then months of silence, and another phone call. Year after year. Decade after decade. It is the most ordinary thing in the world, a citizen calling a police department. And it is also one of the most important things in this entire story, because a cold case that no one asks about is a cold case that no one opens. While the Walker family waited, the world changed around the evidence on that shelf. Here is the ledger of those years. In 1974, there was no DNA testing anywhere. In 1984, ten years after Carla's death, a British scientist named Alec Jeffreys discovered that DNA could be used like a fingerprint to identify one person. In the late 1980s and 1990s, DNA began to transform courtrooms across America. In 1998, the FBI's national DNA database, known as CODIS, became fully operational, allowing labs across the country to compare crime scene profiles with the profiles of convicted offenders. In 2010, a small genealogy website called GEDmatch went online, a place where ordinary people could upload their own DNA results to find long lost cousins. And in April 2018, investigators in California used that same website to identify and arrest the man known as the Golden State Killer, a case that had been cold for decades. Each of those steps brought the tools a little closer to Carla's case. But tools do nothing unless someone picks them up. And for a long time, no one did. Count it the way the family had to count it. When DNA fingerprinting was discovered in 1984, Carla had been gone for ten years. When the national database came fully online in 1998, she had been gone for 24. When GEDmatch went online in 2010, she had been gone for 36. When the Golden State Killer was arrested in 2018, she had been gone for 44, and the 12-year-old brother who once planned to become a detective was a man in his mid-fifties. Through every one of those years, her clothing sat in storage, holding the same answer it had held in 1974. Earlier attempts to test the evidence in Carla's case could not produce what investigators needed. When a male DNA profile was eventually developed from material on Carla's bra, it was run through CODIS, and it did not match anyone in the national database. That result is worth understanding, because it explains why so many cold cases stayed cold even after DNA arrived. CODIS can only find a person whose own profile is already in it. If the killer had never been required to give a DNA sample after a qualifying arrest or conviction in the era of DNA databases, CODIS could look at his genetic profile all day long and see a stranger. A perfect sample of the right man's DNA is useless if there is nothing to compare it to. And the man who killed Carla Walker, for all those years, was not in the database. In 2000, a woman named Leah Wagner joined the Fort Worth Police Department. Like most officers, she started in patrol, answering calls on the streets of the city. In 2013 she was named a detective. And in 2018 she moved into the department's cold case unit. At that moment, according to Texas Monthly, she was the unit's only staff member. One detective. In a city with a long list of unsolved killings stretching back decades. It was Leah Wagner who began returning Jim Walker's calls. In 2018, according to Oxygen's reporting on the case, Jim reached out to the department and asked them to take a fresh look at his sister's murder. It was the same request he had been making, in one form or another, for most of his life. But this time someone was in a position to say yes. Wagner was joined by a partner, Detective Jeff Bennett. Together, they were the cold case unit. In April 2019, as part of that fresh look, Fort Worth police did something unusual. They released the anonymous handwritten letter from 1974 to the public, sharing it on social media. Investigators believed its author might have been a witness, and they hoped someone, somewhere, would recognize the handwriting or the strange notation, sign 10100. The story spread across national news. Police hope a mystery letter can solve a 45-year-old cold case, the headlines said. But no one came forward with the answer. The letter remained what it had always been, a fragment without a pattern. Still, the case was open again. In 2019, Wagner and Bennett formally reopened the murder of Carla Walker. They went back through the file from the beginning. And they did what good cold case detectives do. They refused to assume that anyone had been properly ruled out. They built a list of possible suspects, and it grew to more than 80 names. Rodney McCoy, the boyfriend who had been beaten in the car, was on it, because the person closest to a victim must always be examined. And somewhere in that file, among the papers from the first weeks of the case, was a report about two detectives who visited a Ruger owner in early March 1974, and a note that he had passed a polygraph. Then Wagner did the thing that would matter most. She sent Carla's clothing to be tested again. Her dress. Her bra. Her underwear. The same pieces of clothing that had been collected near Benbrook Lake in February 1974 and kept ever since. And here, finally, the arithmetic of time turns in the family's favor. Because the evidence had been preserved, it could be tested with the science of the twenty-first century. A piece of fabric that had waited in storage for 45 years was about to become the most important witness in the case. There was only one problem. The amount of DNA left on it was almost nothing.

Four nanograms. That was what Detective Jeff Bennett had to work with. A nanogram is one billionth of a gram. A single grain of table salt weighs tens of thousands of times more. Four nanograms of DNA cannot be seen, cannot be felt, cannot be picked up with tweezers. It exists only as a number on a laboratory report. And after the earlier rounds of testing on Carla's clothing, that tiny amount was most of what was left of the man's genetic material. Bennett would later describe it plainly. We had a very, very small amount left, he said, which is not typically usable. Think about what that meant in 2019 and 2020. The detectives had a male DNA profile from Carla's bra. They had run it through CODIS. It had matched no one. They had a list of more than 80 possible suspects. And they had, quite literally, one last chance in a tiny sample of evidence. If they used it on the wrong test, the sample could be consumed, and the last physical trace of Carla's killer would be gone forever. So Bennett picked up the phone and called someone who understood exactly that kind of problem. His name was Paul Holes. Holes was a retired investigator from California, best known for his role in the hunt for the Golden State Killer, the case that in April 2018 showed the world what genetic genealogy could do. He had since become a television host, presenting a program on Oxygen called The DNA of Murder with Paul Holes. Bennett told him the situation. He had four nanograms of DNA. Was there anyone who could do something with that? Holes knew a laboratory. It was called Othram, a private company founded in 2018 in The Woodlands, a community just north of Houston, about four hours' drive south of Fort Worth. Othram's chief executive was a scientist named David Mittelman. The company had built its business on a simple idea. Crime scene DNA is often too old, too degraded or too small for traditional tests. But if you read it a different way, with the kind of genome sequencing used in medical research, you can sometimes pull out an enormous amount of information from almost nothing. According to reporting on the case, Oxygen paid for the testing of Carla's evidence. To understand why that mattered, you have to understand two different ways of reading DNA. Here is the first. Since the 1990s, police labs have built DNA profiles by looking at a small number of places on a person's genetic code, around 20 of them in the modern American system. At each of those places, people differ in how many times a short piece of code repeats. Put those 20 measurements together, and you get a profile that is effectively unique to one person, like a 20-digit combination lock. That is what CODIS stores. It is excellent for one job, answering the question, is this the same person? But it is almost useless for another job, answering the question, who is this person related to? A 20-digit combination tells you very little about a cousin. Now here is the second way. Instead of measuring 20 places, you read hundreds of thousands of individual letters scattered across the genetic code, the places where human beings differ by a single letter. Scientists call these SNPs, single nucleotide polymorphisms. Think of them not as a combination lock but as a family album with hundreds of thousands of pages. You inherit about half of your pages from your mother and half from your father. Your siblings share about half of their pages with you. Your first cousins share less. Your third cousins share only a small fraction. But with enough pages, even distant cousins leave a measurable overlap. That overlap is what genetic genealogy measures. And that is what the consumer DNA tests that millions of Americans have taken are built on. So the process works like this. A laboratory like Othram takes the crime scene DNA and sequences it, producing a SNP profile, a long list of those individual letters. That profile is then uploaded to a genealogy database that allows law enforcement comparisons. The most important one in these years was GEDmatch, a website where ordinary people upload their own DNA files to find relatives, and where users can choose whether their profiles may be compared with law enforcement cases. The database does not say, here is your killer. It says something much more modest. Here are some people who share small amounts of DNA with your unknown sample. Perhaps a second cousin. Perhaps a third cousin once removed. Then the human work begins. Genealogists take those distant matches and start building family trees, backward through parents and grandparents, using birth records, marriage records, obituaries and census data, until they find the ancestors that the matches share. Then they build forward again, down through every branch of those ancestors' descendants, looking for a man of the right age, in the right place, at the right time. It is slow work. It can take weeks or months. And it never ends with a conclusion. It ends with a lead, a name or a handful of names that must then be confirmed the old-fashioned way, with a direct DNA comparison. That is exactly how the Golden State Killer was found in 2018. Investigators uploaded crime scene DNA to GEDmatch, found distant relatives, built family trees, narrowed the possibilities to one man, collected his discarded DNA, and confirmed the match. In the two years after that arrest, police across the country began trying the same method on their own coldest cases. Fort Worth's cold case unit was about to join them. Bennett and Wagner traveled to Othram's laboratory. They saw what the company could do. And then they made their decision. They would send Othram's chief executive, David Mittelman, a piece of Carla's bra. According to the online encyclopedia account of the case, Othram was contacted in the spring of 2020, around April or May, though one later account places the submission in September. The documented sequence of events in the investigation, as we will see, makes the earlier date the more likely one. Think for a moment about that piece of fabric, and about the distance it had traveled. It had belonged to a 17-year-old girl who went to a Valentine's dance at Western Hills High School on February 16, 1974. It had been collected near Benbrook Lake on February 20. It had been kept by the Fort Worth Police Department through five decades, through the end of Watergate, through the arrival of personal computers and cell phones and the internet, through the invention of DNA testing and the creation of a national database. And now a piece of it was traveling south to a laboratory near Houston, carrying four nanograms of the truth. At Othram, scientists worked with what was left. They developed a profile from that tiny sample. And then Othram uploaded the data to GEDmatch. Somewhere in that database, among the many thousands of people who had uploaded their DNA results out of curiosity about their family history, there were relatives of the man who had pulled Carla Walker out of a car. They did not know it. They had never heard her name. They had simply wanted to know where their family came from. And when the results came back, they pointed to one family. One surname. And that surname was about to send two detectives back to the oldest pages in the file.

The surname was McCurley. When the genealogy work on the profile from Carla's bra was finished, it pointed to one family, and within that family, to a small group of men. According to the reporting on the case, the possibilities narrowed to three brothers, all with the last name McCurley. Three men. After 46 years, after more than 80 names on a suspect list, after a letter no one could explain, the circle around Carla Walker's killer had shrunk to the size of a single family. And then the detectives went back to the file. The oldest pages of that case were the reports from the first weeks after the dance, when a task force was chasing a magazine from a .22 caliber Ruger through federal purchase records. About two dozen buyers. Two detectives knocking on doors. And there, in the record of early March 1974, was a name that had been in the file the whole time. Glen Samuel McCurley. He was the Ruger owner. He was the man who told detectives in 1974 that his gun had been stolen from his pickup truck while he was fishing, about six weeks before Carla was taken. He was the man who said he had never reported the theft because he was an ex-convict. He was the man who said his wife had been in West Texas that weekend. He was the man who agreed to a polygraph, passed it, and was cleared. The man who, according to Texas Monthly, the original detectives never thought about again. The answer had been in the file all along. Not hidden. Not lost. Written down in the first weeks of the investigation, by detectives who had been sitting in the right house, talking to the right man, and had walked away. It is hard to describe what that moment must mean for a cold case detective, and the detectives involved have not given a public account of their feelings that this story can quote, so it will not try. But the facts speak loudly enough. In 1974, the system had found him in under a month. It took 46 years for anyone to look again. Glen Samuel McCurley was 77 years old in 2020. In February 1974, he had been about 30 or 31, a grown man with a wife and a criminal record. Carla Walker had been 17. In the years since, he had gone on living in Fort Worth. When the DNA finally brought the detectives back to him, he was still there. Set the two lives side by side. In February 1974, Carla was a junior with more than a year of high school still ahead of her, and he was a man in his early thirties who owned a .22 caliber Ruger. In September 2020, Carla had been gone for 46 years. Had she lived, she would have been 63 years old, perhaps a grandmother, perhaps the veterinarian her brother once imagined. Her little brother Jim, 12 when she was taken, was now 58. And the man who took her was 77, an old man in the same city, whose name had been sitting in her file since the first weeks after she was found. But a surname and a name in an old file are not proof. Genetic genealogy produces leads, not convictions. The method had narrowed the field to three brothers. The 1974 file made one of them stand out. Now the detectives needed what every genealogy case needs at the end. A direct comparison. The man's own DNA, tested against the profile from Carla's bra. And they needed to get it without tipping him off. So they did what investigators in these cases often do. They waited for him to throw something away. In the United States, trash placed out at the curb for collection is generally treated as abandoned, and the courts have long held that people do not keep a reasonable expectation of privacy in it. Police can collect it. On July 7, 2020, Fort Worth investigators collected items from a trash bin in front of McCurley's home. According to the investigation's own account, three items from that bin were sent off for DNA testing. Three ordinary objects, the kind of things anyone throws away without a second thought, carrying the same genetic code that had been sitting on a piece of fabric in police storage since February 1974. Then came the wait. July. August. On September 4, 2020, according to the arrest affidavit later described in the Dallas Morning News, Leah Wagner heard back from the lab. The DNA from the items in McCurley's trash matched the male DNA profile from Carla Walker's bra. A match. After 46 years, six months and 18 days, the science had spoken. But the detectives wanted to be certain, and they wanted the evidence to be beyond challenge. A few days later, Bennett and Wagner went to McCurley's home to talk to him in person. It was, in a strange way, a repeat of a scene from 1974. Two Fort Worth detectives at the door of the same man, asking about the same murder. The first time, he had told them a story about a stolen gun. This time, according to the affidavit, he told them he did not know Carla Walker, and he denied killing her. And then he did something that is hard to explain. He agreed to give them a sample of his DNA. He consented to two swabs from the inside of his mouth. The record does not say what he was thinking, and this story will not guess. What the record does say is what the swabs showed. When the results came back, on Wednesday, September 16, 2020, according to reporting at the time of the arrest, McCurley's own DNA matched the DNA found on Carla Walker's bra. Two independent samples. The trash in July. The swabs in September. Both pointing to the same man. The same man whose name had been written in the case file in March 1974. Think about the pieces of the kaleidoscope now, and how they had finally fallen into place. The magazine in the parking lot had led to the Ruger buyers. The Ruger buyers had led to his name. The clothing had preserved his DNA. The family's phone calls had kept the case alive long enough for a detective to answer. A retired investigator had pointed them toward a laboratory. The laboratory had turned four nanograms into a genetic profile. Strangers in a genealogy database had pointed to a family. And the family had pointed back to a page from 1974. Nothing had been lost. Every piece had been there all along. It had only taken 46 years to turn the tube. There is something else worth noticing about the man the evidence named. He was not a mysterious drifter who passed through Texas and vanished. He was not a figure no one had ever heard of. He was a Fort Worth man, questioned in the first weeks of the investigation, who simply went home afterward and lived out the next four and a half decades in the same city where Carla's family was waiting for answers. He grew old there. And for 46 years, as far as the public record shows, he never said a word. Monday, September 21, 2020. The detectives had their match, twice confirmed. They had a warrant. And they were ready to go back to Glen Samuel McCurley's door one last time.

On Monday, September 21, 2020, Fort Worth police arrested Glen Samuel McCurley. He was 77 years old. According to the reporting on the case, the arrest was made without incident. There was no chase, no standoff, no dramatic scene. An old man was taken into custody in the city where he had lived for decades, and he was charged with capital murder in the death of Carla Jan Walker. He was booked into the Tarrant County Jail, and his bond was set at 100,000 dollars. From February 17, 1974, to that Monday, 17,018 days had passed. After his arrest, detectives sat down with McCurley for a recorded interview. That video would later be played for a jury, and it is the closest the public will ever come to hearing him explain himself. It did not begin with a confession. It began the way his story had begun in 1974, with a denial. Asked about Carla, he told them, I've never seen her. It was the same answer he had given them at his own door days earlier, when he said he did not know her and agreed to the swabs that would prove otherwise. But this time, the detectives were not asking him about a stolen gun. They were sitting across from him with a DNA match, confirmed twice. And as the interview went on, the denial gave way. According to the reporting from the trial, McCurley broke down in tears. He admitted that he had pulled the 17-year-old out of a car. He said that he had choked her when he got scared. And then, in words that would be repeated in courtrooms and newspapers, he said them out loud. I choked her to death, I guess. And then, I'm guilty. That small word, guess, kept surfacing in his recorded answers, as if 46 years had turned the worst thing he had ever done into something vague and distant. But there was nothing vague about what the evidence showed, and there was nothing vague about what his words meant to the Walker family. The next day, Tuesday, September 22, 2020, the Fort Worth Police Department held a press conference. Standing in front of the cameras was Jim Walker, the boy who had been 12 when his sister was taken, now a man of 58. He thanked the detectives. And he wept. He described the moment he heard the news. The word that came across my brain, he said, was finally, finally. This is a resolution, he said, that's been prayed for. And then he gave a number that only someone who had been counting could give. After 46 years, 7 months and 5 days, he said, we have a name, a face, and we're moving toward complete resolution. Forty-six years, seven months and five days. Not roughly half a century. Not decades. Days. A family does not keep count that precisely unless every one of those days has been felt. And notice the symmetry. In March 1974, two Fort Worth detectives sat with this man and cleared him. In September 2020, two Fort Worth detectives, Leah Wagner and Jeff Bennett, went back to the same man and did not let go. The badge was the same. The city was the same. The man was the same. What had changed was the science, and the patience of a family that had never stopped calling the police department to ask whether anything new had happened. The case moved into the courts. A Tarrant County grand jury indicted McCurley, then 77, on the capital murder charge. And in May 2021, the office of the Tarrant County Criminal District Attorney, Sharen Wilson, announced its decision. Prosecutors would not seek the death penalty. Their goal, as it was later reported, was simpler and more certain. They wanted McCurley to spend the rest of his days in prison. The trial opened in Fort Worth in late August 2021. By then McCurley was 78. In the courtroom were the members of Carla's family who had waited for this moment most of their lives, and members of McCurley's own family were in the courthouse as well. And then the past began to walk into the room. Rodney McCoy took the witness stand. The boy who had been sitting in the driver's seat in the parking lot of the Ridglea Bowl in February 1974 told a jury, 47 years later, what had happened that night. He described being struck. He described the moment he understood it was a gun, when the man pointed it at him, inches from his head. He described the gun clicking three times without firing. He described blacking out, and waking to find the passenger seat empty. He told the jury he had never seen the attacker's face. And he described driving to the Walker home with blood running into his eyes, banging on the door, and telling Carla's family what had happened. I was in shock, he said. Then the prosecution played the video. The jurors watched a 77-year-old man deny ever seeing Carla Walker, and then watched him come apart. They heard him say, I choked her to death, I guess. They heard him say, I'm guilty. For two days, the trial went on. And on the morning of the third day, Tuesday, August 24, 2021, something happened that almost no one in the courtroom expected. Glen Samuel McCurley stopped the trial. He waived his right to have a jury decide his case, and he changed his plea to guilty. After maintaining his innocence through a 1974 interview, a passed polygraph, a denial at his own door in 2020, and the first days of his own trial, he stood before the court and admitted that he was guilty of the capital murder of Carla Walker. Glen Samuel McCurley went to prison in the late summer of 2021, convicted of the capital murder of Carla Walker. He would never come out.
