Every August 23, the Loomis family marked another year. Jody would have been thirty in 1982. Forty in 1992. Fifty in 2002. Each round number was a reminder of a life that had stopped at twenty. Her parents grew older. Jana grew into a woman older than her sister had ever been allowed to become. Somewhere in those years, a younger sister becomes the older one, and that strange reversal is one of the quiet griefs that families of murdered young people carry. Let us lay out the ledger of time, because in this case, the numbers tell the story better than adjectives can. In 1972, Jody was killed, and the stain on Jana's boot could not be read. In 1984, twelve years later, a British scientist named Alec Jeffreys discovered that DNA could be used to tell one person from another. The method was first used in a criminal case in England in the late 1980s. It was a revolution, but it was a revolution that needed something to compare. Through the late 1980s and the 1990s, American states began collecting DNA profiles from people convicted of certain crimes, and laboratories began building databases. In 1998, twenty-six years after Jody's death, the FBI's Combined DNA Index System, known as CODIS, began operating nationally. CODIS is, in simple terms, a giant electronic filing cabinet of DNA profiles. Some profiles come from crime scenes. Others come from people who have been convicted or, in some states, arrested. When a lab enters a new crime scene profile, the system searches the cabinet for a match. It is enormously powerful. But it has one fundamental limit. It can only find someone who is already in the cabinet. For years after CODIS came online, the stain on Jody's boot still sat untested. That is not unusual. Across the country, there were, and still are, enormous backlogs of old evidence waiting for someone with the time, the funding and the reason to send it to a lab. Cold cases compete for attention with new crimes that have living suspects and urgent deadlines. A murder from 1972, with no suspect and a family that had already waited more than thirty years, was not at the front of anyone's line. Then, in January 2008, cold case detectives at the Snohomish County Sheriff's Office did what someone eventually had to do. They gathered up material evidence from the Loomis case and sent it to the Washington State Patrol Crime Laboratory for DNA testing. Among the items were the boots. A few months later, in May 2008, the lab reported back. Technicians had found spermatozoa on one of the boots Jody had been wearing. It was on the left boot. Thirty-six years after it had been left there, the stain had finally been found and identified for what it was. And from it, the lab was able to develop a male DNA profile. Some reports describe it as a partial profile, which is common with evidence that old. DNA breaks down with time, heat, moisture and handling, and a sample that has sat on leather for more than three decades rarely yields a perfect, complete reading. But it was enough to search with. And it was enough, if the right man were ever found, to compare. Remember the boots. This is the moment the boot began, at last, to speak. But it spoke only half a sentence. The profile from the boot was uploaded into CODIS. Investigators waited for the system to search its millions of profiles. And the answer that came back was the answer that so many cold case detectives have heard. No match. The man who had left that stain was not in the database. In a strange way, that made sense. His only known conviction, the 1968 case, had come long before anyone in America was collecting DNA from offenders. If he had stayed out of trouble with the law since then, or at least out of the kind of trouble that would have put his genetic profile on file, then he was invisible to CODIS. The system could search millions of people, and he was simply not one of them. Think about how close and how far that was at the same time. In 2008, the Snohomish County Sheriff's Office had, sitting in a computer, a genetic profile that belonged to Jody's killer, and only to him. And the man it belonged to was alive, in his mid-sixties, and when he was finally found, he would be living in Edmonds, the same area where investigators say he had lived in 1972. There was simply no bridge between the profile and the man. Detectives retired. New ones took over. Each new detective who opened the file found the same things waiting there. The date. The woods. The borrowed boots. And now, a genetic profile with no name attached to it. The case remained Snohomish County's oldest cold case, the one at the bottom of the pile in terms of age, and in some ways at the top in terms of symbolism. Years after the DNA profile was developed, a Seattle television station ran a story about the case under a headline that said everything about where things stood. The family, it said, was still seeking justice. Still. By then, Jody had been dead for more than forty years. And during those years, the two people who had perhaps waited hardest of all ran out of time. Jody's mother and father both died without ever learning who had killed their daughter. Jana would later say that out loud in a courtroom, in a sentence that carries the full weight of those decades. Both parents, she said, died without ever knowing. They had raised Jody, watched her grow up, seen her get engaged, and then spent the rest of their lives with a question that no one could answer. There is no CODIS for grief. There is no lab that can return lost years. So here is the ledger, one more time, in plain numbers. Twelve years before DNA profiling was discovered. Twenty-six years before CODIS. Thirty-six years before anyone found the stain on the boot. And still, after all of that, no name. In 2008, the boot had finally spoken, but no one in the database could answer it back. So what do you do when the database has no answer? To find the man who left that stain, investigators would need something new. They would need a way to search not only for a person, but for that person's family. And in a few short years, in a way that almost nobody saw coming, the ordinary hobby of millions of Americans tracing their family trees was about to give them exactly that.

To understand how the boot finally gave up its secret, you need to understand a revolution that happened not in police laboratories, but in people's living rooms. Starting in the late 2000s and exploding through the 2010s, millions of ordinary Americans began spitting into small plastic tubes and mailing them off to consumer DNA companies. They wanted to know where their ancestors came from. They wanted to find long-lost cousins, birth parents, half-siblings. It was a hobby, a curiosity, sometimes a deeply emotional search. Nobody doing it was thinking about cold cases. In 2010, two genealogy enthusiasts launched a free website called GEDmatch. The idea was simple. If you had tested with one company, and your cousin had tested with another, you could both upload your raw data to GEDmatch and find each other. Over the years, more than a million people did exactly that. And without anyone quite intending it, GEDmatch became something else. It became a place where a stranger's DNA could be compared against a huge pool of people who had voluntarily shared their own. Here is the key idea, and it is worth taking slowly, because it is the heart of how this case was solved. The DNA profile that crime labs use for CODIS looks at a small number of specific locations in the genetic code, around twenty markers. That is enough to say, with overwhelming confidence, whether two samples came from the same person. But it is not designed to find relatives. Consumer genealogy tests do something different. They read hundreds of thousands of tiny single-letter variations scattered across the genome, called SNPs, pronounced snips. Think of the CODIS profile as a fingerprint, perfect for matching one person to one sample. Think of the SNP profile as a family resemblance, written in genetic code, that you share in measurable amounts with your parents, your siblings, your cousins, even your third and fourth cousins. The more DNA you share with someone, the closer the relationship. Genealogists measure that shared DNA in units called centimorgans. A parent and child share about half their DNA. First cousins share, on average, around an eighth. By the time you reach third cousins the amount is much smaller, and it varies, but it can still be measured. Imagine a huge photograph taken at a reunion of everyone descended from one couple a century ago. Most of the faces look nothing alike. But if you know what to look for, the shape of a jaw, the set of the eyes, the way a smile turns, you can start to see who belongs to which branch. Genetic genealogy does the same thing with numbers instead of faces. When an unknown person's SNP profile is compared against a database like GEDmatch, it does not usually find the person themselves. It finds their relatives. Maybe a second cousin. Maybe a third cousin once removed. Each match comes with a number that measures how much DNA is shared. And then the real work begins, and it is not laboratory work at all. It is genealogy. A genetic genealogist takes those distant cousins and starts building their family trees backward, using birth records, death records, marriage records, census sheets, old newspapers and obituaries. They look for the place where the trees of different cousins meet, a shared set of ancestors, perhaps a couple who lived generations ago. Then they build forward again, down through that couple's children and grandchildren, looking for a man of the right age, in the right place, at the right time. It is painstaking. It can take days or months. At the end of it, if it works, there is not a confession and not a conviction. There is a name, or a short list of names. A lead. And that lead still has to be proven the old-fashioned way, by getting a direct DNA sample from the person and comparing it to the crime scene profile. The world learned what this technique could do in April 2018. In California, investigators announced the arrest of a former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo as the Golden State Killer, a man linked to a series of murders and rapes from the 1970s and 1980s. He had been identified using crime scene DNA, GEDmatch and family trees. He later pleaded guilty and was sentenced to life in prison. Overnight, cold case detectives across the country understood that the rules had changed. Evidence that had sat silent for decades might suddenly have a way to speak. One company moved quickly to offer this to police. Parabon NanoLabs, a DNA technology firm based in Virginia, launched a genetic genealogy service in May 2018, with a team led by the genetic genealogist CeCe Moore. Parabon could take a degraded crime scene sample, produce the kind of SNP data genealogists need, and help identify likely relatives. And the very first case Parabon's genealogy team announced came from Snohomish County, Washington. In May 2018, the Snohomish County Sheriff's Office announced an arrest in the 1987 killings of Jay Cook and Tanya Van Cuylenborg, a young couple from British Columbia. The detective on that case was Jim Scharf. Scharf was a cold case detective with the Snohomish County Sheriff's Office. He had seen, in one of the oldest cases in his own county, what genetic genealogy could do. And he had another case on his list. An even older one. The oldest of them all. Jody Loomis. By 2018, all the pieces existed for the first time. The boot had been tested, and a male DNA profile existed. A company existed that could turn crime scene DNA into genealogy data. A public database existed with more than a million people in it. And there was a detective in Snohomish County who had already seen the technique work, and who was ready to try it again. It is worth naming what made this possible at a human level. Every one of the distant relatives who would eventually help identify Jody's killer had done something completely innocent. They had sent off a tube of saliva because they were curious about their roots, and they had uploaded their results to a website to find family. None of them had any idea that their DNA would one day help solve a murder from 1972. Some people find that troubling, and the debate over privacy is real. In 2019, GEDmatch changed its rules so that users had to opt in before their data could be used for law enforcement searches. Those questions are legitimate, and they are still being argued. But for families like the Loomises, the technique offered something that nothing else in forty-six years had offered. A path from an anonymous stain to a human being. So in 2018, Snohomish County investigators sent the DNA from Jody's boot to Parabon. The sample was forty-six years old. It had been collected in an era when no one imagined it could ever be read. It had survived decades in storage. It had been found in 2008, profiled, searched and failed. Now it was being asked to do something far harder than match a single person in a database. It was being asked to find a family. Somewhere in GEDmatch, among more than a million strangers, were people who shared a piece of their DNA with the man who had killed Jody Loomis. They had never met him, perhaps. They might not even know he existed. But their genes knew. And for the first time in forty-six years, someone was listening.

The person who turned the DNA on Jody's boot into a family tree was not a detective, and not a lab scientist in a white coat. According to local reporting on the case, she was a genetic genealogist from Oregon named Deb Stone, working from the results Parabon produced. Some national reports would later credit Parabon's best-known genealogist, CeCe Moore, who led the company's genealogy team at the time. But most coverage of the Loomis case, including The Herald in Everett, names Stone as the genealogist who built the tree. Her tools were not handcuffs or interrogation rooms. They were public records, a list of distant DNA matches and a great deal of patience. The work began with strangers. When the genetic data from the boot was compared on GEDmatch, it did not produce a match to the killer himself. It produced matches to people who were related to him, people who had uploaded their own DNA for their own reasons. Each of those people was a branch on a tree that no one had drawn yet. Stone's job was to draw it. This kind of work is slow. You take a distant cousin and research their parents, grandparents and great-grandparents, using birth certificates, marriage licenses, obituaries and census records. You do the same for another cousin, and another. You look for the point where their lines cross, a couple, perhaps several generations back, whose descendants would account for the pattern of shared DNA. Then you reverse direction and trace every descendant of that couple forward, through marriages, children and grandchildren, eliminating anyone who is too young, too old, the wrong sex, or who lived somewhere else entirely. Step by step, branch by branch, the tree narrowed. And eventually it pointed to one couple. Their names were Jacquette and Albert Miller, and they had lived in Edmonds, Washington, in the same southern part of Snohomish County where Jody had lived. The Millers had six sons. The genealogist concluded that the man who left the stain on Jody Loomis's boot had to be one of them. Think about how far the case had travelled in that moment. For forty-six years, the suspect pool had been, in effect, every man who could have been on those roads that afternoon. Now it was six brothers in one family. Genetic genealogy does not deliver a name on its own. It delivers a direction. From there, it was up to the detectives, led by Jim Scharf, to do the traditional police work. Detectives focused on the six sons. And one of them stood out. His name was Terrence Miller. Some people called him Terry. He had been born in the early 1940s, which meant he had been about thirty years old in the summer of 1972. Investigators would later say he had been living in the Edmonds area at the time, about five miles from the woods where Jody was found. And there was something else. Miller had a record. In 1968 he had been convicted of lewd conduct. There would also be allegations, never charged, that he had molested several members of his own family. Of the six brothers, he was the one whose history and geography fit. By 2018, Terrence Miller was in his mid-seventies. To anyone who met him, he was an older man living a quiet life in Edmonds. He had worked as a heavy-equipment operator and was retired. He and his wife ran a ceramics shop. He had been married several times. He had divorced three times before marrying his current wife, and at the time of Jody's murder, according to ABC News, he had been living with his third wife, who has since died. He was, in other words, exactly the kind of person nobody looks at twice. A retiree. A small-business owner. A man of an age when most people are thinking about grandchildren and doctor's appointments. Put the ages side by side, because they say a great deal. In the summer of 1972, Jody was twenty and Terrence Miller was about thirty. Jana was twelve. In the summer of 2018, Miller was seventy-six. Jana was in her late fifties, a grown woman with decades of life behind her. And Jody, had she lived, would have been sixty-six years old. Instead she remained, in every photograph her family had, twenty years old forever. And the man investigators now believed had taken her life had grown old in the same county, a few miles from where it happened. What does it mean that the answer had been that close the whole time? But a family tree is not proof. A genealogist's conclusion, however careful, is a lead, and in American courts a lead is not evidence of guilt. Detectives needed what every genetic genealogy case needs at this stage. They needed Terrence Miller's own DNA, collected lawfully, so the crime lab could compare it directly with the profile from the boot. They could not simply ask him for a sample without alerting him. So they did what detectives in these cases often do. They watched him, and they waited for him to throw something away. In August 2018, undercover detectives followed Miller to the Tulalip Resort Casino, north of Everett. It is a large, busy place, full of slot machines and restaurants and people who have no idea they are standing near an investigation. Detectives watched as Miller sat with a cup of coffee. When he finished and threw the cup into the trash, they moved in and retrieved it. Under American law, once a person discards an item in a public place, they generally give up any expectation of privacy in it. The coffee cup was evidence. Think about the strange symmetry here. Forty-six years earlier, a man had left a trace of himself on a twelve-year-old girl's boot, never imagining that anyone could read it. Now, in 2018, that same man left a trace of himself on a coffee cup in a casino, never imagining that anyone was watching. The cup went to the Washington State Patrol Crime Laboratory, the same lab that had found the stain on the boot ten years earlier. Scientists developed a DNA profile from the cup. And then they compared it to the profile from the sperm on Jody's left boot. It was a match. The DNA on the coffee cup Terrence Miller had thrown away matched the DNA left on the boot Jody Loomis had borrowed from her little sister in August 1972. Remember the boots. They had waited in storage through nine presidential administrations, through the founding of an entire city on the land where Jody died, through the deaths of her parents. They had waited for science to be invented, then for a database to be built, then for a family tree to be drawn. And now, at last, they had given investigators a name. Terrence Miller was seventy-six years old. He had spent forty-six years as a free man. He was living, by every outward sign, an ordinary life in Edmonds, a few miles from the woods along what had once been Penny Creek Road. He did not yet know that detectives had his coffee cup. He did not know that the boot had finally spoken. And on a spring day in 2019, detectives would come to his door.

On Wednesday, April 10, 2019, detectives from the Snohomish County Sheriff's Office went to Terrence Miller's home in Edmonds and arrested him. He was seventy-seven years old. Jody Loomis had been dead for forty-six years, seven months and eighteen days. Miller was booked into the Snohomish County Jail for investigation of first-degree murder, and his bail was set at one million dollars. The next day, the Sheriff's Office announced the arrest to the public, and the story of Jody Loomis, a story most people in the county had never heard, was suddenly on television stations and news sites across the country. Sheriff Ty Trenary put it this way in the department's announcement. Thanks to the relentless persistence of our cold case team and new DNA technology, he said, we are one step closer to justice for Jody. One step closer. Not justice yet. A sheriff choosing his words carefully, because an arrest is not a conviction, and the man in custody was presumed innocent. The case was the second time in about a year that Snohomish County detectives had made an arrest in a cold homicide using genetic genealogy. For Jana, now known as Jana Loomis-Smith, the arrest meant that after forty-six years, there was a face and a name. It did not mean the waiting was over. In fact, a different kind of waiting was about to begin. Prosecutors charged Terrence Miller with first-degree premeditated murder. He pleaded not guilty. First-degree murder, under Washington law, requires premeditation, meaning that the intent to kill was formed beforehand, even if only briefly. It is the most serious homicide charge the state can bring. By pleading not guilty, Miller put the state to its proof. Prosecutors would have to convince a jury, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the man in front of them, now in his late seventies, was the man who had been in those woods in 1972. Until then, by law, he was presumed innocent. At a bail hearing in the case, Jana addressed the court. On that day, she said, the murderer stole Jody's life. A judge later lowered Miller's bail to seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. In June 2019, Miller posted bond and was released from jail. He went home to Edmonds to await trial, monitored through a private electronic home monitoring company. A seventy-seven-year-old man accused of a murder from 1972 now lived under a kind of house arrest, while the case against him moved slowly toward a courtroom. And then the world changed again. In early 2020, the coronavirus pandemic shut down much of public life, including much of the court system. Trials across the country were postponed. Jury selection, testimony, the ordinary business of justice, all had to be reinvented around masks, distancing and livestreams. The trial of Terrence Miller finally began in Snohomish County Superior Court in Everett in October 2020, forty-eight years after Jody was killed. Deputy prosecutor Craig Matheson led the case for the state. Miller was represented by defense attorney Laura Martin. Prosecutors listed twenty-seven witnesses whose testimony could be put before the jury. Eight were detectives and retired police officers. Others were forensic scientists, and people who had known Jody or seen her on the day she died. Some of the people who had been there in 1972 were no longer alive. The man who had found Jody in the woods had died while the case awaited trial. But his account had been preserved. His deposition, recorded before his death, was presented to the jury, so that a man who had stood on that dirt road on August 23, 1972, could still tell twelve jurors in 2020 what he had seen. And then Jana took the witness stand. She was a twelve-year-old girl when she lent her sister a pair of boots. Now she was about sixty years old. In that courtroom, she was shown the boots, the St. Moritz waffle stompers, size five and a half. She saw them for the first time in forty-eight years. Remember the boots. Remember that the last time Jana had seen them, they were on her big sister's feet, heading out the door. The case against Miller came down, in the end, to that boot and that coffee cup. Forensic scientists explained how the stain had been found on the left boot in 2008, how a male profile had been developed from the sperm, and how DNA from the coffee cup Miller discarded at the casino matched it. The defense attacked the evidence. Laura Martin argued that the DNA was tainted, that it could have been contaminated over the decades it had been stored and handled, and that the forensic work had been sloppy. The boots had been stored for more than three decades before anyone tested them, and the defense argued that decades of storage and handling had left room for contamination. That is the defense's job, and in a case built on a single piece of decades-old evidence, it was the obvious line of attack. The jury did not hear everything. Jurors were not told about Miller's 1968 conviction for lewd conduct, or about the allegations that he had abused members of his family. Courts limit that kind of information because a jury must decide the case in front of it, not a defendant's past. They decided based on the evidence of 1972 and the science of 2008 and 2018. After about two weeks of testimony, the case went to the jury. Deliberations began. And then, on the morning of Monday, November 9, 2020, something happened that almost no one in the courtroom had anticipated. At around ten o'clock that morning, Terrence Miller was found unresponsive at his home in Edmonds. A family member found him. He was dead. The Snohomish County Medical Examiner's Office would later confirm that he had died by suicide. He was seventy-eight years old. Hours later, in the courtroom in Everett, the jury returned its verdict. Guilty. Guilty of first-degree murder in the death of Jody Loomis. Miller's lawyer had argued against announcing the verdict at all, saying there would be, in her words, frankly no public benefit, since her client could no longer be sentenced. The court read it anyway. Forty-eight years after Jody rode her bicycle toward the stable, twelve people had looked at the evidence and decided that Terrence Miller killed her. He was not there to hear it. Jody's family was. According to The Herald, they showed no outward reaction as the verdict was read. When court went into recess, they embraced. The prosecutor, Craig Matheson, described the family's reaction as stoic. Asked about Miller's death, he was direct. It does not diminish the verdict, he said. Jody's brother, John, followed the verdict by livestream, the way so many people followed court in the year of the pandemic. He would tell reporters he would have preferred to see Miller go to prison. Detective Jim Scharf, who had carried the case to this point, said he was glad the family got to hear the verdict. I think that was good for the Loomis family, he said. There is a difficult question at the heart of that day, and it would soon be argued in court. Can a man be convicted of murder if he died before the verdict was read?

The question did not stay hypothetical for long. Miller's defense asked the court to throw out the verdict. Their argument rested on an old legal idea. In many American courts, when a defendant dies before his case is fully finished, the proceedings can be abated, which means wiped away, as if they had never happened, on the theory that a person who can no longer appeal or be punished should not carry a conviction to the grave. If the court agreed, the jury's verdict would disappear, and the official record would show that Terrence Miller had died an accused man, not a convicted one. On Friday, December 18, 2020, the parties returned to Snohomish County Superior Court. It was the day that should have been Miller's sentencing. Instead, Judge David Kurtz had to decide whether there was anything left to decide at all. He called the situation somewhat unprecedented. He called it truly an extraordinary situation. The defense itself acknowledged that the case law it had cited did not quite fit these circumstances, because Miller had not died after a conviction, while an appeal was pending, but in the hours before the verdict was even read. In the end, the judge was not persuaded. He refused to vacate the verdict. And instead of signing a sentence, which was impossible, he signed a document affirming that Terrence Miller's guilt had been proven beyond a reasonable doubt. Jana Loomis-Smith spoke at that hearing. She did not ask for anything dramatic. She said she did not want vengeance. What she wanted was simple. She wanted the truth written into the court record. She wanted the record to say that Terrence Miller murdered her sister. And she reminded the court of the two people who were not there. Both of their parents, she said, had died without ever knowing who killed their daughter. She also said something that looked beyond her own family. She said she hoped the outcome would give hope to other families and other victims who have waited years for their own cold cases to be solved. Think about who was saying those words. Forty-eight years earlier, she had been a twelve-year-old girl who lent her big sister a pair of boots. She had grown up in the shadow of that afternoon. She had outlived her parents' wait. She had sat in a witness box and looked at those boots again. And when the moment finally came to say what she wanted, she did not ask for revenge. She asked for the truth to be written down. That is what she got. As of this video, the conviction stands. Terrence Miller died on November 9, 2020, and was found guilty of first-degree murder in the death of Jody Loomis that same day. He was never sentenced, because he could not be. This channel has found no report of any appeal or later ruling that has changed that outcome. The oldest cold case in Snohomish County is closed. In the years since, the case has been retold on national television, in an episode of the long-running series Cold Case Files titled Woman in the Woods, and in true crime programs on other networks. Each retelling brings the same core facts to new audiences. A young woman on a bicycle. A borrowed boot. A coffee cup at a casino. A verdict that came hours too late for the man it named. We do not know why Terrence Miller did what a jury found he did. No confession appears in any public record, and he took whatever he knew with him. Now imagine a different version of this story, one in which someone, at some point in those decades, decided that a pair of old boots from a 1972 case was taking up space. Shelves get cleared. Evidence gets destroyed. It happens in police departments across the country, often for understandable reasons. What would have happened to this case if those boots had been thrown away in 1985? The honest answer is that nothing would ever have happened. There would have been no profile, no family tree, no coffee cup, no trial and no verdict. The man investigators identified would have died in Edmonds as a retiree who once ran a ceramics shop, and Jana would have lived out her life without the sentence she wanted written into the record. If there is one lesson in this case, it is about the value of keeping things. Not just evidence on shelves, though that matters enormously. Keeping faith. Keeping a file open. Keeping a name in the conversation when the rest of the world has moved on. The deputies of 1972 kept the boots. The detectives of 2008 sent them to the lab. The detectives of 2018 sent the profile to Parabon. And Jana kept waiting. Every one of those acts of keeping was necessary.
