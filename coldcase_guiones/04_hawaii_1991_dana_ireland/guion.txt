On the afternoon of December 24, 1991, a twenty-three-year-old woman from Virginia got on her sister's bicycle in Kapoho, a scattering of homes on the far eastern tip of the Big Island of Hawaii. It was Christmas Eve. Her name was Dana Ireland. Her parents had flown nearly five thousand miles to spend the holidays with their two daughters, and the family was planning a Christmas gathering for the next day. Dana had a small errand in mind and a short ride ahead of her. She never came back from that ride. Somewhere on Kapoho Kai Drive, a vehicle struck her bicycle and left it twisted at the side of the road. Dana was taken about five miles away, to a lonely fishing trail off Waa Waa Road. There she was sexually assaulted, beaten, and left behind in the brush. When a neighbor found her, she was still alive. It took more than two hours for an ambulance to reach her. Shortly after midnight, in the first minutes of Christmas Day, Dana Ireland died in a hospital in Hilo. The person who did this left something behind. There was DNA on her body. There was DNA on the sheet of the hospital gurney that carried her. And there was a T-shirt, found near the trail, soaked in her blood. For years, the police had no name. Then they had three. Three young Native Hawaiian men from Puna were arrested, tried, and convicted. One of them spent more than two decades behind bars. One of them died in a prison yard in New Mexico. And all that time, the DNA did not belong to any of them. It took thirty-two years, a small team of lawyers who refused to let go, and a science that did not exist in 1991 to put a name to the man the laboratory called Unknown Male Number One. The Island of Hawaii, the Big Island, is the largest and the youngest of the Hawaiian Islands, a single island bigger than all the others combined, still being built by its volcanoes. December 24, 1991, was a Tuesday. In Virginia, where Dana had spent every Christmas of her childhood, it was the middle of winter. Before it was a court case, though, it was a crime scene, or more precisely, two crime scenes. Remember what DNA could and could not do in 1991. A laboratory could take a sample like this and compare it with a sample from a man the police already suspected. If the two did not match, that man could be excluded. But the laboratory could not take the sample and ask who it belonged to. There was no national database to search, no list of names to run it against. A sample with no suspect was a sealed letter with no address on the envelope. Two retirees from the suburbs of Washington, living near the place where their daughter had been taken, refusing to let the case fade. Think about the pressure that created. On May 23, 1994, a man named John Gonsalves went to the authorities with a story. He said that his half-brother, Frank Pauline Junior, had been present when Dana Ireland was attacked, and that Pauline knew what had happened to her. At the time, Frank Pauline was already in custody on an unrelated case. He was facing a possible ten-year prison sentence for sexual assault and theft. He was, in other words, a man with very strong reasons to tell the police something they wanted to hear. Pauline was interviewed in June 1994, and then again, and again. According to a later review of the case by researchers who study false confessions, he was interviewed at least seven times, and each time his account was different. The details changed. The sequence changed. The roles changed. But over those interviews, a story took shape. Pauline placed himself at the scene, and he named two others: a pair of brothers from Puna, Albert Schweitzer, who went by his middle name, Ian, and his younger brother, Shawn. Both were Native Hawaiian. In 1991, Ian had been about twenty years old. Shawn had been a teenager, reported to be sixteen at the time of the crime. Years later, Pauline would say why he did it. He said he had offered the police details about the Ireland murder to get drug charges dropped against his half-brother. And in 1996, he took it all back. He recanted his statements. By then, though, the story he had told was already doing its work. It is worth pausing here to understand something that took the American justice system a long time to accept. People confess to crimes they did not commit. They do it far more often than most of us would believe. Researchers who have studied hundreds of wrongful convictions overturned by DNA have found false confessions and false statements by co-defendants again and again. They happen when someone is offered a deal, when someone is frightened, when someone is exhausted after hours of questioning, when someone believes that telling police what they want will make the pain stop. And they are often full of details that seem convincing, because those details can come from rumors, from news stories, or from the questions themselves. Frank Pauline had a deal on the table, a brother to protect, and an unsolved case under heavy public pressure in front of him. Every one of those factors is a known warning sign. In 1996, the Irelands came back to the Big Island. This time they brought a petition with several thousand signatures urging action on the case. That same year, the settlement of their lawsuit against the county over the rescue delays put Dana's name back in the headlines. The pressure did not ease. It grew. In October 1997, nearly six years after the crime, police made their move. Ian Schweitzer, Shawn Schweitzer and Frank Pauline Junior were arrested. On October 9, 1997, the Schweitzer brothers were indicted on charges connected to the kidnapping, sexual assault and murder of Dana Ireland. For the public, it looked like an ending. Three local men, charged. A grieving family, finally answered. Hold on to the ages, because they are part of the cost. Ian Schweitzer was in his mid-twenties when he was arrested. He would not walk out of custody as a free man, for good, until he was fifty-one. Shawn had been a boy of sixteen on the night of the crime. And Frank Pauline, the man whose words had started all of it, would never walk free again. There were now three families in this story, not one: the Irelands, who had lost a daughter, and the Schweitzer and Pauline families, who were about to lose sons and brothers to the prison system for decades. But almost immediately there was a problem, and it came from the evidence collected on Christmas Eve. Remember the sexual assault kit. Remember the swabs. In 1998, DNA testing was carried out to see whether the Schweitzer brothers could be connected to the sexual assault. The results did not connect them. The genetic material left by Dana's attacker did not come from either of them. The case against the brothers was dismissed. Stop on that for a moment, because it is one of the most important moments in this entire story. In 1998, seven years after the crime, the science available at the time looked at the most direct evidence of who had assaulted Dana Ireland, and it said: not these two men. The system had a choice in that moment. It could treat the DNA as a warning that the story built from Pauline's shifting statements was wrong. Or it could treat the DNA as a puzzle to be explained away. Years later, a co-director of the Hawaii Innocence Project would observe that when the victim is a young white woman, a case draws far more attention than cases involving people of color and Native Hawaiians. The Ireland case had all of that attention, and all of that pressure, aimed at three Native Hawaiian men from Puna. The DNA said one thing. The pressure said another. For a little while in 1998, it looked as if the DNA had won. The Schweitzer brothers were no longer facing charges. But the clock was running. Under Hawaii law, some of the crimes committed against Dana could only be charged within a limited number of years. The statute of limitations, the legal deadline for bringing those charges, was approaching. If no one was charged in time, some of what had been done to her could never be prosecuted at all. And then, in May 1999, with that deadline only days away, a man in a jail cell came forward and said he had heard a confession.

His name was Michael Ortiz. He was an inmate. In May 1999, he told prosecutors that Ian Schweitzer had confessed to him, that while the two of them were held at the Hawaii Community Correctional Center in Hilo, Ian had admitted being involved in Dana Ireland's death. With the statute of limitations only days from running out on some of the charges, police and prosecutors treated it as a break in the case. The Schweitzer brothers were indicted again in 1999. Jailhouse informants are one of the oldest tools in criminal justice, and one of the most dangerous. An inmate who claims to have heard a confession almost always wants something: a lighter sentence, a dropped charge, a better cell. Across the United States, testimony from jailhouse informants has been found again and again among the causes of wrongful convictions. In this case, parts of Ortiz's testimony were later found to be false. And in their 2025 lawsuit, the Schweitzer brothers allege that detectives told Ortiz to say that Ian had confessed. It heard about the scenes, the bicycle, the trail. In 1999, Frank Pauline Junior was convicted. His sentence was measured in lifetimes. Reports describe it in different ways, as a total of one hundred eighty years, as one hundred sixty years, or as consecutive life terms, but on any of those descriptions, it meant he would almost certainly die in prison. In 2002, the Hawaii Supreme Court upheld his conviction. Then came Ian. His trial ended in early 2000. The jury heard from Michael Ortiz. It heard about the T-shirt, which was a key exhibit at both trials. It heard about the vehicle. The state's theory put Ian's Volkswagen Beetle at the center of the crime. And it heard about the injury on Dana's body that looked like a bite mark. According to the Innocence Project, a forensic odontologist, a dentist who testifies about teeth and bite marks, had initially excluded Ian Schweitzer as the source of that mark. At trial, the same expert said Ian's teeth were consistent with it. On February 16, 2000, the jury found Albert Ian Schweitzer guilty of second-degree murder, kidnapping and first-degree sexual assault. He was sentenced to life in prison for the murder and to two twenty-year terms for the other crimes, all to be served one after another. News reports summarized the total as one hundred thirty years. And what about the DNA? The genetic evidence from the sexual assault kit still did not match Ian Schweitzer, or his brother, or Frank Pauline. The DNA testing available at the time of the trials excluded all three defendants. The DNA was there, in the case file, saying not these men. And the verdicts came anyway. The third conviction came in a different way. Shawn Schweitzer, the younger brother, had watched juries convict Frank Pauline and then his own brother. He was offered a deal. Part of that deal was that he had to blame his brother and Pauline, and take a lie detector test. On March 24, 2000, an examiner from the state Attorney General's office gave him that polygraph. In his plea, he said he had been in the back of the Volkswagen when his brother ran over Dana Ireland. The family could not bear to lose the other. Pause on that. A young man admitted to a crime he would later say he had nothing to do with, not because the evidence was strong, but because his family was terrified of what a jury might do. In the American system, more than nine out of ten criminal convictions come from guilty pleas, not trials. Most people never think about what it means to plead guilty when you are innocent. Shawn Schweitzer lived it. For the public, the year 2000 looked like an ending. All three men were convicted. The case that had consumed his family for nearly a decade appeared to be over. He had also spent those years turning grief into law. He helped pass legislation in Hawaii allowing family members to speak at the sentencing of people convicted of heinous crimes. He helped pass another law allowing a sentence of life without parole for murders found to be especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel. That second law could not be applied retroactively to anyone charged with his own daughter's murder. It was a law for other families. On or about October 31, 2000, John Ireland died in Virginia. He was seventy-six. He did not live to see any of what came next. The years after that are a ledger, and it reads like this. Ian Schweitzer, in prison. Year after year after year. Frank Pauline, in prison, his appeal lost in 2002. In December 2012, Pauline was transferred out of Hawaii to the Southern New Mexico Correctional Facility in Las Cruces, thousands of miles from home. And the evidence, the shirt, the swabs, the sheet, all still sitting in storage, still holding the same answer. The DNA had been there all along. Then, in April 2015, the Hawaii Innocence Project announced that new DNA testing in the Ireland case could point to a different attacker than the three men in prison. It was the first public crack in the story the state had told for fifteen years. Just days later, on April 27, 2015, Frank Pauline Junior was found dead in a remote section of the recreation yard of the New Mexico prison. He was forty-two years old. Frank Pauline died with his conviction still standing, at the very moment the evidence was beginning to say something different. He would never hear what it had to say. But two other men were still waiting, one in a prison cell and one in a life built around a guilty plea, and the science was finally catching up.

To understand how this case finally broke open, you have to understand two different kinds of DNA science, and why the first one was never going to be enough. Start with the evidence. On Christmas Eve 1991, investigators had collected three things that held the attacker's genetic material: the swabs from the sexual assault kit, the sheet from the hospital gurney that carried Dana, and the Jimmy Z T-shirt found near the trail, soaked in her blood. In the 1990s, testing on that kind of evidence could exclude a suspect. It could say, this sample did not come from that man. And it did, more than once, for all three men accused. But as the technology improved, laboratories could do much more with less. Newer methods could pull a full genetic profile out of small, old, mixed samples. When the Hawaii Innocence Project pushed for modern testing of the evidence, the results were striking. The same unknown man's DNA turned up on the T-shirt, in the material from the rape kit, and on the sheet from the hospital gurney. It was one man. Not three. The laboratory gave him a name that sounds like a placeholder because that is exactly what it was: Unknown Male Number One. And Unknown Male Number One was not Ian Schweitzer, not Shawn Schweitzer, and not Frank Pauline. Now comes the first kind of science, the one everyone has heard of. The FBI runs a national DNA database called CODIS, the Combined DNA Index System. Think of it as a giant filing cabinet of genetic profiles, mostly from people convicted of crimes, and in many states people arrested for them, along with profiles from unsolved crime scenes. Each profile is a short code, built from about twenty specific spots in a person's DNA. When investigators upload a profile from a crime scene, CODIS checks it against everything in the cabinet. If the person who left the DNA is in the cabinet, there is a hit. If he is not, there is nothing. Unknown Male Number One went into CODIS. There was no match. Whoever he was, he had never been convicted of a crime that put his DNA into the system. In the language of investigators, he was invisible. That is where this case would have stayed, perhaps forever, if not for the second kind of science. The people who refused to let it stay there were the lawyers of the Hawaii Innocence Project, a group of attorneys and law students in Hawaii who take on cases of people they believe were wrongly convicted. Its co-director, Kenneth Lawson, became the public face of the fight for the Schweitzer brothers. In January 2017, the Innocence Project filed a motion seeking to overturn the conviction in the Ireland case. In March 2018, the Hawaii Tribune-Herald ran a story under a headline saying that new video suggested Dana Ireland's killer was still at large. And in December 2018, West Hawaii Today published a story with a headline that, looking back, reads almost like a prophecy: genetics and genealogy could help the Dana Ireland case. That headline did not come from nowhere. Earlier that year, in April 2018, investigators in California had arrested a former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo and identified him as the Golden State Killer, the man responsible for a string of murders and rapes across California in the 1970s and 1980s. They had not found him through CODIS. He was not in CODIS. They found him through his relatives. This is the second kind of science: investigative genetic genealogy. Here is how it works, in plain words. Instead of a short code of about twenty markers, a laboratory reads hundreds of thousands of tiny genetic markers, known as SNPs. If the CODIS profile is a short barcode, a SNP profile is the entire book. That kind of profile can be compared with the profiles of people who have taken consumer DNA tests and uploaded their results to genealogy databases that allow law enforcement searches. The unknown man is almost never in those databases himself. But his cousins might be. His second cousins, his third cousins, people who share a great-grandparent with him, may have spat into a tube to learn about their family history. Each of those relatives shares a measurable amount of DNA with the unknown man. Genealogists then do what genealogists have always done. They build family trees, using birth records, marriage records, obituaries and newspapers, back to the common ancestors, and then forward again, generation by generation, looking for a man of the right age, in the right place, at the right time. It is slow. It is painstaking. And when it works, it turns an invisible man into a name. In this case, it would take years before the genealogy began. First, the courts had to deal with the three men already convicted. In October 2022, Shawn Schweitzer met with prosecutors and recanted his guilty plea from more than two decades earlier. He later took a polygraph examination, and according to his lawyers, it showed he was telling the truth when he denied any involvement in Dana's death. Then, on January 24, 2023, Ian Schweitzer came before Judge Peter Kubota of the Circuit Court in Hilo. His lawyers brought a list of new evidence the original jury had never heard. There was the DNA of Unknown Male Number One, on the shirt and in the rape kit, and the sperm on the hospital gurney sheet, all excluding the three convicted men. There was the bite mark. A forensic odontologist hired by the defense, Doctor Adam Freeman, had examined the injury and concluded it was not a human bite mark at all. By then, bite mark analysis had come under heavy criticism across the United States, from national scientific panels and from courts, after a series of cases in which bite mark testimony had helped convict people who were later proven innocent. There was the tire analysis. An expert concluded that Ian's Volkswagen Beetle had not left the tire tracks at either scene, not on Kapoho Kai Drive where the bicycle was run over, and not at the fishing trail where Dana was found. And there was Shawn's recantation. Prosecutors did not agree that Ian Schweitzer was innocent. They agreed only that there was new evidence the jury had never heard. That agreement, a stipulation, was enough. Judge Kubota ruled that the new evidence, taken together, showed that a new jury would likely acquit. He said the DNA evidence conclusively proved that a jury would likely reach a different verdict, a verdict of acquittal. He even raised the practical question of whether three men could have driven a Volkswagen Bug down that rough road at all. He vacated Ian Schweitzer's conviction, and on a motion from prosecutors, the charges were dismissed. That same day, Ian Schweitzer walked out of custody. He was fifty-one years old. Reports put his time behind bars at somewhere between twenty-three and twenty-six years, depending on how the years before his trial are counted. At a news conference with his mother and other relatives, he thanked his lawyers and the judge. Quote, I'm just grateful, very thankful, end quote. He said his feelings were, quote, all over the place. Nerves, anxiety, scared, end quote. Kenneth Lawson called the decision, quote, a long time coming, end quote. Dana Ireland's relatives could not be reached for comment that day. Ian Schweitzer also said something that surprised many people. He said he wanted justice for Dana, too, and that his team would work with prosecutors to find the man whose DNA it really was. Because the ruling had answered one question and left the other wide open. If it was not these men, then who was Unknown Male Number One?

The next nine months moved faster than the previous thirty years. In 2023, one of the three men convicted was free. A second was still living under the weight of his guilty plea. The third was dead. The Innocence Project went back to court for the second. On October 23, 2023, Shawn Schweitzer stood before the same judge, Peter Kubota. The deputy prosecutor in the case, Shannon Kagawa, agreed to let him withdraw his guilty plea from 2000. Judge Kubota vacated his conviction and dismissed his case. The boy who had been sixteen on the night Dana Ireland was attacked was now a middle-aged man. He had spent about a year in jail, five years on probation, and more than two decades carrying a conviction for a crime he said he had nothing to do with. Then the lawyers turned to the third man. Frank Pauline Junior had been dead since April 2015. Can a dead man be exonerated? In Hawaii, the answer turned out to be complicated. In January 2024, a hearing was scheduled in Hilo on a petition to overturn Pauline's conviction. It was expected to be postponed, for a reason that sounds almost absurd: the family's attorney, Myles Breiner, said they had not yet received an official copy of Pauline's death certificate from New Mexico, and the court needed it. Kenneth Lawson of the Hawaii Innocence Project pointed out that there was simply no system in Hawaii to help a wrongfully convicted person who had already died. The hearing was delayed. As of the most recent reporting this channel could find, there has been no public ruling formally vacating Frank Pauline's conviction. His name remains, in the official record, attached to a crime the DNA says he did not commit. And all the while, the search for Unknown Male Number One was under way. The Hawaii Innocence Project did not wait for the police to start it. Working with law enforcement, the lawyers sent the DNA samples to a private company called Indago Solutions. Indago had been founded by Stephen Kramer, a former federal prosecutor, and Stephen Busch, a former FBI special agent. The company combines genetic results from genealogy websites with public records and newspaper obituaries to identify unknown people from their DNA. Kramer is credited in news reports with helping to identify Joseph James DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer, in 2018, the case that showed the whole country what genetic genealogy could do. Now he turned the same method on a Christmas Eve crime on the Big Island. The first thing the DNA said was where the unknown man's family came from. According to a court filing, his genetic profile was estimated to be about eighty percent Filipino. That fit the history of the islands. For more than a century, beginning in the early 1900s, tens of thousands of workers from the Philippines had come to Hawaii to labor on the sugar and pineapple plantations, and their descendants are now one of the largest communities in the state. On the Big Island, plantation towns had been built around those workers and their families. For the genealogists, it was a first clue about which family trees to search. Then came the slow work. Matches to distant relatives in the genealogy databases. Family trees built back to common ancestors and forward again. Each branch checked against records, each candidate weighed by age, by ancestry, by where he had lived. On February 26, 2024, Indago reached a conclusion. Based on his ancestry, his age, his genetics and his address history, the company identified one man as the only potential candidate. His name was Albert Lauro Junior. According to the same filing, he had three Filipino grandparents. Count the time. From the afternoon of December 24, 1991, to February 26, 2024, thirty-two years, two months and two days had passed. Dana's father had been dead for more than twenty-three years. Her mother had been dead for more than seven. Frank Pauline had been dead for almost nine. Ian Schweitzer had been free for one year and one month. And a company working from a few strands of genetic material, preserved since the night of the crime, had produced what three decades of police work had not: a single name. Kramer turned the information over to the FBI, and the FBI passed it to the Hawaii Police Department. Now the police had a name. But a name produced by genealogy is not proof. It is a lead. To turn it into evidence, investigators needed a direct comparison: the man's own DNA against the DNA from 1991. And they needed to get it without tipping him off. That is where the oldest technique in detective work met the newest. By June 2024, an FBI agent and Hawaii County police officers were watching Albert Lauro Junior. The goal was to collect something he threw away. In the United States, an object a person discards in a public place is generally treated as abandoned, and police can collect it without a warrant. It might be a cigarette butt, a coffee cup, a napkin. In this case, it was a fork. Lauro used a fork and threw it away in public. Investigators picked it up and sent it to a laboratory. The DNA on that fork matched Unknown Male Number One. It matched the DNA from the swabs taken from Dana's body. It matched the DNA on the sheet that had carried her into the hospital in Hilo. And it matched the DNA on the T-shirt found near the trail. Every piece of evidence that had cleared three men now pointed in one direction. The DNA had been there all along. So who was Albert Lauro Junior? In the summer of 2024, he was fifty-seven years old and lived in Hawaiian Paradise Park, a large subdivision in Puna, the same district where Dana had been attacked. In December 1991, he would have been in his mid-twenties. One report said he lived in the Kapoho area at the time of the crime. His record, according to court records cited in news reports, contained nothing more serious than traffic citations and a civil case involving a bank. There was no history of violence. He had never been convicted of a crime that put his DNA in CODIS. That was why, for thirty-two years, the national database had nothing to say about him. Think about the contrast. For more than three decades, three men from Puna had carried the weight of this crime: arrests, trials, prison, probation, a death in a prison yard. And the man whose DNA was on the evidence had, as far as the public record shows, gone on living in the same district, with a record that held only traffic tickets. It is important to be precise here, because the law demands it. A DNA match is powerful evidence. But at this point, Albert Lauro Junior had not been arrested. He had not been charged with any crime. He had not been tried. In the eyes of the law, he was presumed innocent, as every person is. What investigators had was a match, and a decision to make about what to do with it. They decided to ask him to come in. On July 19, 2024, Albert Lauro Junior sat down with the police.

The meeting on July 19, 2024, was not an arrest. According to the police, Albert Lauro Junior agreed to speak with investigators voluntarily. He was not in custody. The encounter, the department later said, was consensual. But the detectives did not come empty-handed. They had obtained a court order allowing them to take a sample of his DNA directly from him, with a buccal swab, a cotton swab rubbed along the inside of the cheek. The idea was simple: the fork was a match, but an object collected in public can always be challenged in court. A swab taken directly from the man, under a court order, would remove any doubt about whose DNA it was. The swab was taken. Lauro answered some questions. Then he asked to leave. Because he was not under arrest, he was allowed to go. What he said in that interview has never been made public. The police later said they were still reviewing the transcript, along with his cell phone. The public record does not contain a single sentence of his account. Four days later, on July 23, 2024, Albert Lauro Junior was found dead in his home in Hawaiian Paradise Park. The police said he had died by suicide. He was fifty-seven years old. The next day, July 24, the laboratory results came back on the swab taken from his cheek. It was a match. The DNA from the man himself matched Unknown Male Number One, the DNA on the swabs from Dana's body, on the gurney sheet, and on the T-shirt. One day. The confirmation came one day after his death. On Monday, July 29, 2024, the Hawaii Police Department held a news conference in Hilo, streamed live, and told the world. Chief Benjamin Moszkowicz announced that the department had identified Albert Lauro Junior as the man whose DNA had been found on the evidence in the Dana Ireland case, the man the laboratory had known for years only as Unknown Male Number One. And then the chief had to explain why that man had never been arrested. His explanation turned on two legal ideas. The first was the statute of limitations. The DNA, the chief said, tied Lauro to the sexual assault. It gave the police probable cause for that crime. But the deadline for charging sexual assault in this case had passed years earlier. That crime could no longer be prosecuted, no matter what the evidence showed. Think of a statute of limitations as an expiration date stamped on certain crimes. The laws of that era were not written for a world in which a crime-scene sample could be matched to a name three decades later. Since the rise of DNA evidence, many states have extended or removed those deadlines for sexual assault, precisely because cases like this one showed how long the truth can take to arrive. For the sexual assault on Dana Ireland, the deadline had already passed. The second was the difference between being present and causing a death. Murder in Hawaii has no statute of limitations. It can be charged at any time. But to arrest someone for murder, police need probable cause to believe he committed that crime. And the chief said the DNA alone did not establish it. In his words, quote, the presence of Lauro's DNA at the crime scene was, in and of itself, not sufficient evidence to prove that Lauro intentionally or knowingly caused her death, end quote. He also explained that if officers had arrested Lauro without probable cause, any evidence or breakthroughs that followed could have been challenged in court as inadmissible. And he addressed why investigators had used a court order rather than a search warrant to take the swab. To get a warrant, he said, quote, investigators would have to have established probable cause for the crime of murder and explained specifically what evidence it was seeking, end quote. The chief ended on a promise. Quote, we remain committed, as we have since day one, of solving this case, end quote. The Hawaii County Prosecuting Attorney, Kelden Waltjen, released a statement of his own. Quote, it is our hope that law enforcement's continued investigation, the advancements in technology that weren't previously available, and the identification of Unknown Male Number One will afford the Ireland family some sense of closure, end quote. The office's victim assistance unit, it said, had been in contact with the Ireland family. Not everyone accepted the police explanation. Stephen Kramer, the genetic genealogist whose company had identified Lauro, disagreed publicly. He said the DNA match was enough probable cause to arrest Lauro for Dana Ireland's murder. And he argued that taking another DNA sample at the police station on July 19 served no real purpose, other than to tip Lauro off that he was a suspect in a very serious crime. The Hawaii Innocence Project also questioned why Lauro had not been arrested when the swab was taken. One newspaper reported that the way the swab was handled was outside recommended procedures. The lawyers for the men who had been wrongly convicted said something else, too: that with Lauro's death, the full truth about what happened to Dana on Christmas Eve 1991 might never come to light. This is where this story demands the most careful words. Albert Lauro Junior was never arrested. He was never charged with any crime connected to Dana Ireland. He was never tried, and he was never convicted. He died before any court could hear the evidence or his defense. Under American law, he was presumed innocent when he died, because no jury ever heard the case against him. What can be said is this: police identified him through DNA as the source of the genetic material found on Dana's body, on the hospital sheet, and on the T-shirt. Police said that DNA gave them probable cause for a sexual assault charge that could no longer be brought, and that it was not, by itself, enough for them to arrest him for murder. Everything else, what happened on that road, whether anyone else was involved, how she came to be on that trail, remains unanswered in any courtroom. It may remain unanswered forever. And there is one more thing that must be said with care. Albert Lauro Junior was also a person, with a family of his own, and his death by suicide was a tragedy for them. News reports at the time said attempts to reach his relatives were not successful. Their side of this story has not been told publicly. This channel will not try to tell it for them. Step back and look at the whole arc. In 1994, a man in custody, facing a long sentence, told the police a story that changed several times over at least seven interviews. On the strength of that story and the testimony of a jailhouse informant, three men went to prison. In 2024, a man with no record of violence was identified by DNA that had been sitting in evidence since the night of the crime. He was interviewed, swabbed, and allowed to go home, and he died before he could be charged. The first time, the system moved too fast on too little. The second time, critics say it moved too carefully on too much. The police say they followed the law. The question of who is right will be argued for years. But for the people who had spent their lives inside this case, the news conference of July 29, 2024, was not an ending. It was the opening of a new fight.

The new fight began almost immediately, and it was about information. The Schweitzer brothers' lawyers wanted to see the evidence police had gathered on Albert Lauro Junior, including the interview and the investigation that led to him. Hawaii County resisted. In August 2024, a judge in Hilo rebuked the county for its efforts to delay the release of new evidence, and ordered police to turn material over to the court for review. The county took the fight to the Hawaii Supreme Court. In October 2024, the high court ruled. It said the circuit court should not have handled the brothers' civil claims inside their old criminal case, and that the police had not been given enough time to respond. The brothers' requests for investigation records and for compensation would have to be brought as a new civil case. For now, the police did not have to release their records about the new suspect in that proceeding. The evidence against the man police had identified would remain, as one television station put it, secret. But the same ruling settled an important question about what the brothers would have to prove. Hawaii has a law for people who were wrongly imprisoned. It allows compensation of fifty thousand dollars for every year spent behind bars, if the person can show that he is actually innocent, by a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not. For Ian Schweitzer, that could mean more than one point two million dollars. For Shawn, who by one report's count spent about two years in custody, it could mean about one hundred thousand. The Supreme Court said that the brothers did not need a prior court declaration of innocence before seeking that money. But they would need to prove their innocence at trial. Actually innocent, the court said, means that a person did not commit the crime. The county prosecutor's office has fought that claim. In court, one deputy prosecutor put the office's position bluntly: quote, I don't believe that not guilty and actually innocent are the same thing, end quote. The office had agreed in 2023 that there was new evidence the jury never heard. It did not agree that the brothers were innocent. Their charges were dismissed, and according to one report, they were dismissed without prejudice, which in theory means they could be brought again. One report in 2025 said that county prosecutors still considered the brothers suspects. No new charges have been filed against them. The Innocence Project and the National Registry of Exonerations list both men as exonerated. In February 2025, a Honolulu newsroom reported that no one had ever been paid under Hawaii's wrongful conviction compensation law. In January 2025, Ian and Shawn Schweitzer went to federal court. They sued Hawaii County and its police department in the United States District Court in Honolulu, alleging conspiracy, malicious prosecution, violations of due process and the infliction of emotional distress. Their complaint alleges that police, under immense pressure to solve the high-profile murder, framed them, including by encouraging false statements. These are allegations. The county has not been found liable for anything. In a statement, Ian Schweitzer said, quote, my family and the Ireland family are all victims of this terrible injustice, end quote. In August 2025, a judge in Hilo set a trial date on the brothers' state compensation claim for March 30, 2026. Then the two cases collided. In September 2026, a Hawaii television station reported that the Hawaii Supreme Court was being asked three questions: whether the brothers could seek compensation from the state and also sue county law enforcement in federal court for the same years of their lives. Both the state case and the federal case were on hold. The brothers, the report said, had still not received any financial assistance. As of this recording, no court has awarded Ian or Shawn Schweitzer a single dollar. No ruling formally clearing Frank Pauline has been reported. And no one has ever been charged with the murder of Dana Ireland. Look at what changed along the way. When this case began, DNA could only say no. By the time it ended, it could say a name. And in this case, the people who brought genetic genealogy to the evidence were not detectives hunting a killer. They were lawyers for men they believed had been wrongly convicted. The same technique that led to the Golden State Killer was used here to finish the work of clearing the innocent, and only then to point toward someone else. That is a turn almost no one could have imagined in 1991, when a laboratory could only compare and never search. It is also a warning. Science can correct a mistake only if someone keeps asking for the test, keeps paying for it, and keeps pushing when the answer is unwelcome. For more than two decades in this case, the people pushing were defense lawyers and families, not the institutions that made the mistake. So what is left? A family that is almost gone. John Ireland died in 2000, only months after the last of the three convictions. Louise Ireland died in 2016. Neither heard the name Albert Lauro Junior. Sandy, the sister who found the bicycle, has not been quoted publicly in the recent coverage this channel could find. The Schweitzer and Pauline families on the Big Island, and what remains of the Ireland family, carry what is left of this case. And the place where it began no longer exists. Go back, one last time, to Christmas Eve, 1991. A Tuesday afternoon in Kapoho. A twenty-three-year-old woman, shy and kind and loyal, a tomboy and an athlete and a sweet daughter, chooses a bicycle over a car because she would rather get some exercise. She rides out onto Kapoho Kai Drive to do one small, generous thing, to invite a friend to her family's Christmas. Today that road lies under the black rock of 2018. The tide pools are gone. Vacationland is gone. But the shirt, the swabs and the sheet survived, kept in evidence for thirty-two years, long after the ground they came from disappeared. She might have found the physical therapy work she hoped for, helped people recover from their injuries, and spent long years near her sister on the island she had crossed an ocean to reach. A George Mason graduate. A little sister. A young woman at the very beginning. That is who this story is about.
