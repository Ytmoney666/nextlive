That morning, in a laboratory at the University of Leicester in England, Alec Jeffreys was studying patterns in DNA when he noticed that certain repeated sections varied enormously from one person to another. Each person's pattern was different. He realized almost immediately what that meant. A sample of blood, or other biological material, could be linked to one individual with a certainty no blood type could match. He called it DNA fingerprinting. Within a few years, that discovery left the laboratory and entered the courtroom. In England, in the late 1980s, DNA testing was used in the investigation of the murders of two teenage girls in Leicestershire. It cleared a young man who had falsely confessed to one of the killings, and in 1988 it led to the conviction of the real killer, a man named Colin Pitchfork, the first murderer in the world convicted with the help of DNA evidence. In the United States, prosecutors and police began using DNA in court around the same time. The science was new, it was argued over, it was challenged. But it worked. Through the 1990s, the technology improved in two crucial ways. First, a technique called the polymerase chain reaction, usually shortened to PCR, made it possible to copy tiny amounts of DNA millions of times over, the way a photocopier can turn one faint page into a thick stack. That meant a very small stain, the kind that would have been useless in 1984, might now be enough. Second, laboratories moved to a standard way of reading DNA, by measuring a set of specific locations in the genetic code where people differ, and writing down the results as numbers. The result was a DNA profile, a kind of genetic barcode that could be stored in a computer and compared against other barcodes. And once you can store barcodes in a computer, you can build a database. In 1998, the FBI's national DNA index began operating, part of a system called CODIS, the Combined DNA Index System. For the first time, a profile from a crime scene in one state could be searched against profiles from offenders in other states. Each state also built its own database. In California, in November 2004, voters approved Proposition 69, which greatly expanded the collection of DNA from people convicted of felonies, and later from adults arrested for felonies. With every passing year, the database grew. Somewhere in that growing database, eventually, the right barcode would be waiting. Now look at the calendar from the point of view of Tina's family. In 1984, DNA fingerprinting did not exist. By 1988, it had solved its first murder case. By 1998, a national database was running. By 2004, California was dramatically expanding the number of people whose DNA was on file. And through all of those milestones, the purse from the culvert remained on a shelf, its secret still unread. That is the ledger of a cold case, written not in failures of malice but in the slow arithmetic of priorities and resources. Fourteen years from the murder to the national database. Twenty years to Proposition 69. Twenty-three years before the re-examination that would finally break Tina's case open. For a family, each one of those years was a year of not knowing. For the person who killed her, each one was a year of freedom. Shirley never stopped. She was the voice of the case in the long middle years, the mother who would sit down with a reporter and tell her daughter's story one more time, so that Tina's name did not slip away. In 2008, she sat down with the Pleasanton Weekly for an interview. The paper published a cover story in April of that year, close to the anniversary, under a headline that asked the question the whole town had been asking for twenty-four years. Who killed Tina Faelz? It was in that interview that Shirley explained why Tina had been walking that day, the teasing on the bus, the decision to walk home instead. The article walked readers along the route, the path past Aster Court and Lemonwood Way, the underpass at Interstate 680, the neighborhood of Valley Trails. It reminded a new generation of Pleasanton residents that a child had been killed there, and that whoever did it had never been found. That cover story reflected something else that was happening behind the scenes. Late in 2007, the Pleasanton Police Department had gone back to the case once again. The wording of the police account matters here, because it says the evidence was re-examined again, which suggests this was not the first time anyone had looked back at those boxes over the years. But this time was different. Investigators re-examined the evidence that had been collected at the time of the killing, this time using scientific analyses that had not been available in 1984. They sent items to two different laboratories for examination. One of those items was Tina's purse. After twenty-three years, the purse was going to be asked, with tools far more powerful than any used before, the question that nobody had been able to ask in 1984. Whose blood is this? It is impossible to overstate how much depended on what had happened in that culvert decades earlier. If the purse had not been collected, there would have been nothing to test. If it had been stored carelessly, or thrown away when the department cleaned out old evidence, or tested so thoroughly in the 1980s that the stain was used up, there would have been nothing left. Many cold cases across the United States have died exactly that way, with evidence lost, discarded or destroyed before science caught up. In Tina's case, the evidence survived. The stain was still there. The people who first handled it, back in 1984, had done the most important thing anyone can do in a case they cannot solve. They kept it. Shirley was waiting. Drew, who had been eight years old, was now a grown man in his thirties. The fifth grader from Donlon Elementary, Joshua Suchon, had grown up and become a writer. The sixteen-year-old from Lemonwood Way was now around forty, with a long criminal record and no idea, as far as anyone knows, that a purse from 1984 was about to be opened in a laboratory. It would still take years. Laboratory work on old evidence is slow, careful and expensive. But by the end of 2007, for the first time since 1984, the case of Tina Faelz was moving again. And the answer that came back would surprise even the people who had spent their lives waiting for it.

To understand how a purse from 1984 could name a killer, it helps to imagine the journey of that stain from the shelf to the courtroom, step by step, in plain words. Start with the purse itself. It had been found hanging in a tree near the culvert, collected, sealed and stored. When it was taken out again, decades later, a forensic analyst would have examined it closely, looking for any mark that might hold biological material. Blood is one of the best sources of DNA there is, because white blood cells carry a full copy of a person's genetic code. And on Tina's purse, the analysts found what the reports describe as a small amount of blood. Trace amounts. Not a pool. Not a smear across the surface. A spot. Here is the first crucial question the laboratory had to answer. Whose blood was it? The obvious assumption, in a stabbing, would be that any blood at the scene belonged to the victim, and that her belongings might be expected to carry it. So the analysts compared the DNA from that stain to Tina's own DNA profile. And it did not match. The blood on the purse was not Tina's. That single sentence changes the entire case. If the blood was not Tina's, then it came from someone else. And the most obvious someone else, in a knife attack in a hidden culvert, is the person who carried the knife. Now, how does a laboratory read a stain that small, from that long ago? Think of DNA as an enormous instruction book, three billion letters long, that is almost identical from one person to the next. Almost. In certain places in that book, there are short sequences of letters that repeat over and over, like a word stuttered several times in a row. How many times each sequence repeats varies from person to person. At one location you might have the stutter eleven times on the copy you got from your mother and fourteen times on the copy from your father. Someone else might have nine and twelve. Forensic scientists call these short tandem repeats, or STRs. A modern DNA profile records the repeat counts at a standard set of these locations. The result is a list of numbers. That list of numbers is the genetic barcode we talked about. With enough locations, the chance that two unrelated people share the same barcode becomes astronomically small. But to read the barcode, you need enough DNA, and a small stain from 1984 does not contain much. That is where the copying technique comes in, the polymerase chain reaction. It works a little like a photocopier that can copy one faint page, then copy the copies, over and over, until there are millions of copies, enough to measure. The technique was not used in forensic laboratories in 1984. By 2007, it was routine. The purse that had been useless in the era of blood types was now, potentially, a witness. Once a profile was obtained from the stain, the next step was to find out whose it was. There are two ways to do that. One is to compare it to a known suspect. The other is to search it against a database of profiles, like the one the FBI had been running nationally since 1998, where profiles from people convicted of certain crimes are stored. A database search works like an extraordinarily precise version of a library catalog. You enter the barcode, and the system looks for the same barcode anywhere in its records. The news reports from 2011 credited the FBI crime laboratory with the DNA work in this case. They describe the evidence from Tina's case being re-examined from late 2007, sent to two laboratories, and the link to a specific man being announced in August 2011. One report dates the match itself to 2007. Most say 2011. That discrepancy is one of the details that the full court record would settle, and this channel will not pretend to know more than the reporting shows. What is certain is the result. The DNA from the stain on the purse was matched to one man. When the case finally reached a jury, the prosecutor told them what that match meant in numbers. The odds of that blood belonging to someone other than the defendant, she said, were about one in five quadrillion. That is a number so large that it is hard to hold in your head. A quadrillion is a thousand trillion. Five quadrillion is five followed by fifteen zeros. In 2014, there were roughly seven billion people alive on Earth. Five quadrillion is more than seven hundred thousand times larger than that entire population. In practical terms, it meant that if the science was sound and the sample was handled properly, the blood on Tina's purse came from one person and one person only. That last condition is important, and it is exactly where the defense would attack. DNA tells you whose material is on an object. It does not, on its own, tell you how it got there, or when. A skilled defense lawyer will always ask a series of questions about any old piece of evidence. When was it collected. How was it collected. Who handled it. Was it stored with other items. Could it have been contaminated in the decades it spent in storage. Could DNA have been transferred to it innocently, by touch, by contact with another object, by some ordinary event that had nothing to do with the crime. Police and laboratories answer those questions with something called the chain of custody, a written record of every hand that touched an item, every seal that was broken and every place it was stored, from the day it was collected to the day it is shown to a jury. These are legitimate questions, and in many cases they matter enormously. In this case, the jury would have to weigh them. But the science could do something that no detective in 1984 could do. It could put a name on that stain. And it could explain why the case had gone cold for so long. The killer had not left nothing. He had left the most personal thing a human being can leave, a trace of his own blood. The problem in 1984 was not that the evidence did not exist. The problem was that no one alive could read it. There is a quiet lesson in that, one that applies far beyond Pleasanton. The difference between a solved case and an unsolved one is often not the evidence itself, but the time. The same stain, on the same purse, was meaningless in 1984 and decisive in 2011. Nothing about the stain changed. In 1984 it was a spot. By 2011 it was a sentence with a name in it. What changed was the world around it. Science moved forward. Databases filled up. And a police department decided to look again. All of that brings us to the moment when the barcode from Tina's purse finally met a name. It was not the name of a drifter, or a stranger from out of town, or a man who had passed through Pleasanton once and left. It was a name that would have been familiar to the students of Foothill High School in 1984. It was the name of a classmate.

His name was Steven John Carlson. In April 1984, he was sixteen years old, a student at Foothill High School, the same school Tina attended. And he lived on Lemonwood Way, near the trail where she was found. Do you remember that street name? Say it again, slowly, because it has been in this story from the very beginning. Lemonwood Way. When Tina's mother described her daughter's route home to a reporter in 2008, she named it. The shortcut ran along a path through Aster Court and Lemonwood Way, and then down to the underpass at Interstate 680. Tina's quiet way home, the way she chose to avoid being teased, led past the street where Steven Carlson lived. For twenty-seven years, the answer had been, in a sense, sitting on the map. It is one thing to say that a killer was hiding in plain sight. It is another to see how literally that can be true. The man the DNA pointed to was not a stranger to Pleasanton. He was part of the same teenage world as Tina. One of the faces in the hallways. One of the people who walked the same streets home. The author Joshua Suchon, who was a fifth grader in town at the time and would later write the book on the case, described how he was drawn to the story. The thing that drew me to the story, he told the Pleasanton Weekly, was my sister knew Steve Carlson. That is how close this was. In a town of that size, in that era, the circles overlapped. Older siblings and younger siblings, neighbors, classmates, kids from the same streets and the same bus routes. Everyone knew someone who knew someone. The man who would be convicted of killing Tina was someone the families of Pleasanton knew by name. Now, the then and the now. In 1984, Steven Carlson was sixteen. By August 2011, when Pleasanton police were ready to act on the DNA results, he was forty-three. Tina would have been forty-one. She had been dead for longer than she had been alive, almost twice as long. Consider how much the world had changed in that time. In 1984, a teenager walking home had no phone in her pocket. In 2011, most teenagers in Pleasanton carried one. In 1984, the Olympic Games were coming to Los Angeles for the summer. By 2011, Los Angeles had hosted them, and a whole generation had been born, grown up and graduated from Foothill High School since Tina's death. The freeway was still there. The town was larger. The culvert had changed. And through all of that change, one small stain on one purse had stayed exactly as it was, until the day it was read. In the years between, Carlson's life, according to what prosecutors and reporters later described, was marked by an extensive criminal history. According to news reports of the case, his record included convictions for committing lewd acts with a child under the age of fourteen, and for assault. Joshua Suchon's book traces his life in detail, from the years after the killing up to the moment of his arrest. It is not a story of a man who turned his life around. And in the summer of 2011, Steven Carlson was not hard to find. He was already in jail. He was being held in the Santa Cruz County Jail, on the coast south of the Bay Area, on drug charges. Think about what that means for the investigators who had spent years on this case. There was no stakeout needed. There was no manhunt. There was no need to follow a suspect and wait for him to throw away a coffee cup or a cigarette. The man the DNA pointed to was in a cell, in the custody of another county, for another case entirely. On Sunday, August 7, 2011, Pleasanton police took Steven Carlson into custody from the Santa Cruz County Jail. He was charged with the murder of Tina Faelz. Twenty-seven years, four months and two days after the afternoon a fourteen-year-old girl was found in a drainage culvert under Interstate 680, the police of her hometown finally had someone in handcuffs for her death. This is the part of a cold case story where we usually talk about the hero. In some cases it is a genealogist working late at night on a family tree. In some it is a single detective who refused to let go. In Tina's case, the public reporting does not single out one famous name, and this channel will not invent one. The heroes here are quieter. They are the officers and technicians who collected a purse from a tree in 1984 and made sure it was kept. They are the investigators who, in late 2007, decided that the old evidence deserved another look with new science, and sent it to two laboratories. They are the analysts, including at the FBI crime laboratory that news reports credited, who found a small stain that did not belong to the victim and turned it into a profile. And they are a mother and a family who never let Tina's name slip out of the newspapers, and a local paper that put her picture back on its cover in 2008 and asked, in large type, who killed her. None of those people could solve the case alone. Together, across twenty-seven years, they did. When the news broke in August 2011, it moved quickly across the Bay Area. Television stations reported that the 1984 murder of a Pleasanton teenager had been solved by DNA, and that the suspect was a former classmate. The headlines repeated the details that had become familiar over the years. Fourteen years old. Foothill High freshman. Stabbed on her way home from school. And now, a new detail that changed everything. A drop of blood on her purse. For the people of Pleasanton who remembered 1984, it was a strange and painful kind of relief. The fear of an unknown killer had hung over the town for a generation. Now the unknown had a face. And it was not the face of a monster from somewhere else. It was the face of a boy from Lemonwood Way, now a middle-aged man with a jail record. For Tina's family, the arrest was not the end. In many ways it was the beginning of a new kind of waiting. An arrest is not a conviction. A man who is charged is presumed innocent until proved guilty in court. And Steven Carlson had no intention of admitting anything. When reporters from NBC Bay Area covered the case, their headline quoted his words directly. I didn't do it. Those four words would set the course of the next three and a half years. There would be no quick plea, no early confession, no moment of relief for a family that had already waited so long. There would be hearings, delays, motions, a fight over which court should even hear the case, and finally a trial. And there was an unusual legal problem. Because Steven Carlson was sixteen when Tina was killed, the law did not automatically treat him as an adult. He was forty-three years old, he had a long criminal record, and he was about to walk into juvenile court.

In August 2011, a forty-three-year-old man was charged with murder in juvenile court in Alameda County. Reporters who covered juvenile justice noted how strange it was. The juvenile system exists for children and teenagers. Its courtrooms are built around the idea that young people can still change, that rehabilitation is the goal, and that the punishments it can hand out are limited. But the law looks at the age of the accused on the day of the crime, not on the day of the arrest. On April 5, 1984, Steven Carlson was sixteen. So his case began where any sixteen-year-old's case would have begun. To move it to adult court, prosecutors had to ask a judge to find that he was not fit to be dealt with as a juvenile. At the time the crime was committed, that was the only way. Under the law that applied to more recent crimes in 2011, prosecutors could in some cases charge a juvenile directly as an adult, without that hearing. For a crime from 1984, the old rules applied. The law in this area would keep changing. In 2016, California voters approved Proposition 57, which ended the practice of prosecutors filing juvenile cases directly in adult court and returned that decision to judges. Carlson's case had already followed the older path, a hearing in front of a judge, which is the path the law requires again today. Carlson pleaded not guilty. He made his appearances in juvenile court, and the case moved slowly, as old murder cases often do. In October 2012, more than a year after his arrest, he was still appearing in court on the charge, still a defendant, still presumed innocent under the law. Then came the ruling that changed the course of the case. A judge found that Carlson should be prosecuted as an adult. According to NBC Bay Area, Alameda County Superior Court Judge Rhonda Burgess cited the degree of criminal sophistication shown in the killing, the severity of the crime, and previous failed attempts at rehabilitating Carlson, among other considerations. Another local report credits a different judge, Trina Thompson, who had handled earlier appearances, with that ruling, and the court file would settle the point. What matters is the result. Steven Carlson would stand trial as an adult, and if convicted, he would face an adult sentence. Now the case was set for trial. And here the story takes one of its cruelest turns. The trial was originally scheduled to begin on February 13, 2014. Shirley Faelz Orosco, Tina's mother, the woman who had sat outside her son's bedroom door in 1984, who had told her daughter's story to a reporter in 2008, who had waited almost thirty years for this courtroom, died unexpectedly on February 13, 2014. The same day. She would never hear a jury speak her daughter's name and the word guilty in the same sentence. Her sister, Karin Reiff, said that Shirley had been through almost thirty years of hell, and that she believed her sister had finally died of a broken heart. The trial did not begin that day. It began later that year, in the autumn of 2014, in Alameda County Superior Court, about thirty years after the afternoon on the path. The prosecutor was Deputy District Attorney Stacie Pettigrew. Her case rested on the purse. She told the jury that the purse had been found hanging in a tree near the scene, that tests had found a small amount of blood on it that was not Tina's, and that the blood came from Steven Carlson. The chance that it belonged to anyone else, she said, was about one in five quadrillion. The defense attorney was Annie Beles, and she did what a good defense attorney must do. She attacked the evidence. She called the DNA evidence flimsy. She raised questions about when it was collected, how it was collected, whether it had been contaminated and whether it might have been improperly transferred. And she pointed out what the prosecution did not have. No motive. No fingerprints. No weapon. Nothing, she argued, except a trace of DNA on a purse that had spent decades in storage. It was a serious argument. Many old cases turn on exactly these questions, and jurors are right to take them seriously. The jury also heard about Carlson as a teenager. According to a later account of the trial by Oxygen, a former friend testified that Carlson had been bullied and thrown into a dumpster at Foothill High School after a confrontation with members of the school's football team. That testimony painted a picture of a sixteen-year-old who was humiliated by his peers. It did not explain a killing, and nothing ever could. But it was part of the world the jury was asked to understand, the same world of teenage cruelty that Tina had been trying to escape when she stopped riding the bus. On Thursday, October 30, 2014, the jury came back. They had deliberated for about one day. After a trial built on evidence collected three decades earlier, about laboratories and contamination and chain of custody, about a culvert that no longer looked the way it had in 1984, twelve people had needed only a day to decide. Their verdict was guilty of murder in the first degree. Steven John Carlson, forty-six years old, had been convicted of killing his fourteen-year-old classmate, Tina Faelz, on April 5, 1984. Thirty years, six months and twenty-five days had passed. In the courtroom, one person was missing who should have been there more than anyone. Tina's mother had died eight and a half months earlier. But others were there, and they carried her with them. Steve Faelz, described in coverage as Tina's stepfather, spoke after the verdict. The family had their answer at last, at least the answer a jury could give. Sentencing came on January 9, 2015. The defense asked for a new trial. Judge C. Don Clay denied the motion. He then sentenced Steven Carlson to twenty-six years to life in state prison. A sentence of years to life in California does not end on a fixed date. The first number is the minimum term, before any credits the law allows. After that, a prisoner goes free only if a parole board decides that he no longer poses a danger, and the board can say no again and again. Even then, Carlson did not admit what he had done. A CBS headline from that day described him as professing innocence as he received the sentence. In court records and in public, he was still the man who had said, back in 2011, I didn't do it. For the Faelz family, that was its own kind of wound. A conviction is the law's answer. It is not the same thing as the truth spoken out loud by the person who knows it. Tina's family had a verdict and a sentence, but not an admission. They did not have the words they had waited three decades to hear. And as it turned out, the legal story was not finished either. Carlson appealed. The question on appeal was not whether he had been on that path. It was what exactly the prosecution had proved about what was in his mind when he got there. And in January 2017, a panel of three judges would give an answer that changed the case once more.

In January 2017, the California Court of Appeal ruled on Steven Carlson's case. A three-judge panel did not overturn the verdict that he had killed Tina Faelz. The DNA, the purse, the jury's conclusion that he was the person in that culvert, all of that stood. What the court changed was the degree. To convict someone of first-degree murder, prosecutors must prove more than a killing with malice. They must prove premeditation and deliberation, that the killer thought about it beforehand and made a considered decision to kill. The appeals court found that the prosecution had not proved that element. So the conviction was reduced to second-degree murder. According to CBS, the sentence was reduced from twenty-six years to life to sixteen years to life. For Tina's family, it was a bitter result. The man who killed her was still a convicted murderer. But the law now said that the state had not proved he planned it. Hold on to that, because three and a half years later, Steven Carlson himself would say something about that afternoon that no court had heard. In October 2020, Carlson was in the California Correctional Institution at Tehachapi, and he was approaching his first parole hearing. Inmates who come before the parole board in California are expected to show insight, to explain in their own words what they did and why, and what has changed. Carlson wrote a nine-page statement for the parole commissioners. He also wrote two letters, one to the Faelz family, and one to Tina herself. He gave prison officials permission to share them. The family released them to the San Francisco Chronicle. After thirty-six years, after his arrest, after I didn't do it, after a trial in which he denied everything, after professing innocence at his own sentencing, Steven Carlson admitted that he had killed Tina Faelz. In one of the letters, he wrote that he had been living in denial for many years and could not believe or take responsibility for the murder. He wrote about anger. One of the lines that was widely quoted was simple. I remember being full of rage. He tied that rage to being bullied, including over a house party he had tried to throw at his parents' home. And he wrote to Tina's family a sentence that, if it had been spoken thirty years earlier, might have spared them decades of not knowing. I want you to know, he wrote, you and your family did absolutely nothing to deserve what I did to you. There was more.
