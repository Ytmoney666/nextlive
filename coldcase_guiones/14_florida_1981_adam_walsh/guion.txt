On Monday, July 27, 1981, a young mother named Revé Walsh walked into the Sears department store at the Hollywood Mall in Hollywood, Florida, with her six-year-old son, Adam. It was summer. School was out. It was the kind of errand that millions of American families made every week, the kind no one remembers afterward because nothing ever happens. Inside the toy department, a group of older boys were crowded around a display where a new Atari video game system was hooked up and ready to play. Adam wanted to watch. His mother said yes, and she stepped away to shop nearby. By most accounts she was gone for only a few minutes. Some accounts say about five. Others say fewer than ten. When she came back to the video game display, the older boys were gone. And Adam was gone too. Two weeks later, about one hundred and twenty miles away, two men fishing in a drainage canal beside the Florida Turnpike near Vero Beach made a discovery that told the Walsh family their son was never coming home. What followed was one of the longest, most painful and most influential investigations in American history. It is measured in years. While the police followed their leads, John and Revé Walsh were learning, painfully, how the system for missing children actually worked in the United States in 1981. John Walsh would point out again and again, in interviews and in testimony, that the country had a national computer database that police could use to track stolen cars, stolen guns and other stolen property, but that there was no comparable national system for a missing child. If a child was taken across a county line, or a state line, the search could break apart into a dozen separate efforts that never connected. The Walshes decided they could not accept that. Ottis Elwood Toole was born in Jacksonville, Florida, in 1947. That reputation would follow Toole into the Walsh case and cast a long shadow over everything he said. On October 21, 1983, while in prison, Toole told investigators that he had abducted and killed Adam Walsh. In his account, he had taken the boy from the parking lot area outside the Sears store, luring him with promises of candy and toys, and had then driven north with him. He gave investigators an account of what he said happened next. Toole said he had buried Adam's body. He pointed investigators to an area near Vero Beach. Searchers went to look. They found nothing. For the Walshes, this was the first time in more than two years that anyone had claimed to know what happened to their son. It should have been the beginning of an answer. Instead, it was the beginning of a new kind of torment. Toole recanted. He took back what he had said. Then, at different times, he repeated versions of the confession, and then he withdrew them again. Police would later say that he had confessed to killing Adam at least twice, and had later recanted. Henry Lee Lucas, for his part, backed up Toole's confession, which might have seemed significant if Lucas had not been one of the least reliable confessors in American criminal history. At one point Toole also said that he had acted alone. To investigators, every version raised the same problem. A confession from a man known for confessing is not proof. A confession that is retracted is weaker still. And a confession without physical evidence to back it up is exactly the kind of thing that defense lawyers, judges and juries have learned to distrust, with good reason, because false confessions are real and have sent innocent people to prison. American courts have long been wary of convictions built on a confession alone, and prosecutors generally look for independent evidence that corroborates what a suspect says, details that only the real offender could know, or physical proof that ties the suspect to the victim. So the investigators needed something more. They needed evidence that connected Toole to Adam independently of anything Toole said. And for a moment, it looked as if they might have it. Toole drove a car, a 1971 Cadillac. Investigators examined it. In the carpeting of that car, they found blood. Think about what that could mean. A missing child. A man who said he took that child and drove him north. And blood inside that man's car. If that blood could be tied to Adam, the case would change completely. But this was 1983. The forensic tools of the time were limited. Laboratories could confirm that a stain was blood, and in some cases they could determine a blood type, the kind of broad category that millions of people share. What they could not do was say whose blood it was. They could not look at a stain and say, with scientific confidence, this came from one particular human being and no one else. The tests performed on the carpet from Toole's Cadillac could not show that the blood was Adam's. They could not show that it was not. It was a question the science of the day simply could not answer. Investigators from the Florida Department of Law Enforcement also documented the car. According to the account Joe Matthews later published, they took ninety-eight photographs of the Cadillac. Those photographs, Matthews would say, sat for many years without being properly examined by the investigators running the case. Remember that number. Ninety-eight. It will come back. Hollywood police did not charge Toole. Whatever the internal reasons, the result was simple. No case was brought against him in Adam's death. And over the years, investigators, reporters and legal observers would disagree, sometimes bitterly, about whether Toole had done it at all. A man who confessed to so much could, in their view, confess to anything. The physical evidence was inconclusive. The body he said he buried could not be found. And so the man who had told investigators he killed Adam Walsh was not charged with killing Adam Walsh. He remained in prison for his other crimes, and the case remained open. Here is the cruel arithmetic of that decision. The best suspect the police would ever have was already in their reach in 1983. They had his car. They had blood from its carpet. They had his words, however unreliable. What they did not have was a way to test the one piece of physical evidence that might have settled the question. That way was coming. DNA testing was only a few years away from American courtrooms. All the investigators had to do was keep the evidence safe until science caught up. Meanwhile, the Walshes' fight for other children was moving faster than the police investigation into their own. On June 13, 1984, President Reagan stood at the White House at a ceremony marking the opening of the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, a national resource center and clearinghouse that Congress had authorized to help find missing children and support their families. In his remarks, the president credited the courage of John and Revé Walsh with awakening the nation to the problem of missing children. Less than three years after Adam was taken from a toy department, his parents had helped build a national institution. And yet, in Hollywood, Florida, the most basic question about their own son still had no official answer. The confession was on record. The recantation was on record. Pieces of the stained carpet had been cut out and kept, a box of carpet samples taken from the car. Let us lay out the ledger as it stood in the summer of 1984. One confession, on October 21, 1983. More than one recantation. One body that Toole said he buried, never found. One 1971 Cadillac. One box of carpet samples with blood that could not be identified. Ninety-eight photographs. Zero charges. And a family that had now waited three years. The blood on the carpet was in a box. And the car was in police custody. Or so everyone believed.

To understand why the next chapter of this story is so painful, you have to understand what changed in forensic science during the 1980s. When the carpet from Toole's Cadillac was first tested in 1983, a laboratory could tell investigators that a stain was human blood, and it could sometimes tell them the blood type. Blood typing is a little like knowing the color of a car. It can rule some cars out, but it cannot tell you which car out of millions you are looking at. Huge numbers of people share each blood type. A match on blood type proved almost nothing. A mismatch could rule someone out, but the carpet tests in this case did not give investigators either kind of clear answer about Adam. Then came DNA. DNA is the genetic code inside almost every cell of the human body, and apart from identical twins, every person's DNA is unique. In the mid-1980s, scientists in Britain showed that small regions of that code could be compared to tell people apart, and within a few years the technique moved from the laboratory into the courtroom. In the United States, one of the earliest convictions based on DNA evidence came in Florida itself, in a rape case in Orlando in 1987. Within a few years, crime laboratories across the country were learning to use it. Here is why that mattered so much for Adam. Investigators did not need a sample of Adam's DNA from before his death to make a comparison. A child inherits half of his DNA from his mother and half from his father. John and Revé Walsh were alive, and they could provide reference samples. A laboratory could test blood from the carpet of Toole's Cadillac and compare it with the parents' DNA, and ask a simple question. Could this blood have come from their child? If the answer was yes, the case against Toole would no longer depend on the word of a man who confessed and recanted. It would depend on science. If the answer was no, it would point investigators away from Toole, and that would matter too. Either way, the carpet held an answer. All anyone had to do was keep it. While the science was catching up, John Walsh's life changed in a way no one could have predicted in 1981. On February 7, 1988, a new television program premiered on a handful of stations of the young Fox network. It was called America's Most Wanted, and its host was John Walsh. The idea was simple and powerful. Show viewers the faces and stories of dangerous fugitives, and ask the public for tips. It was the lesson the Walshes had learned in 1981 and 1983, turned into a weekly broadcast. Four days after the first episode aired, a viewer tip led to the arrest of David James Roberts, a convicted murderer and rapist who was on the run. Over the years that followed, the program would contribute to the capture of more than one thousand fugitives, with the thousandth capture recorded in 2008. The father who could not get an answer about his own son became the man who helped find more than a thousand people who were hiding from the law. Every week he asked the country for help. And every week the question of Adam's killer remained open in Hollywood, Florida. The family kept growing. A son, Callahan, was born in 1985, and another son, Hayden, in 1994. Adam's siblings grew up with his photograph, his story, and a father who was on television every week hunting other people's fugitives. In 1994, something else happened that would make Adam's name part of everyday American life. The retail company Walmart created a missing child protocol for its stores and named it Code Adam, in Adam's memory, promoting it with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. The idea was straightforward. When a parent reports a child missing inside a store, employees announce a code over the public address system. Staff stop what they are doing, search the store and watch the exits. If the child is not found within about ten minutes, the police are called. Other retailers, museums, hospitals and public venues adopted it in the years that followed. It was exactly the kind of response that did not exist in the Sears store on July 27, 1981. And in that same year, 1994, came the discovery that defines the failure of the Adam Walsh investigation. A Hollywood detective went to retrieve the carpet from Toole's Cadillac so that it could be submitted for DNA testing. The science was ready. The evidence was supposed to be waiting. It was not there. The carpet samples were gone. And so was the car. The 1971 Cadillac, the vehicle that a man who confessed to Adam's murder had driven, had been lost, and according to one account it had been released and destroyed. The box of blood-stained carpet samples had vanished as well. No one could produce them. The one piece of physical evidence that might have proven, or disproven, Toole's confession, the evidence that had waited eleven years for science to catch up, had disappeared from police custody. Pause on that. A department investigating the most famous child murder in the country, with a suspect who had confessed and blood found in his car, lost the car and the blood. In all the public reporting reviewed for this story, there is no full public account of exactly how, when, or by whose decision it happened. It was simply gone. When the Hollywood police chief closed the case in 2008, he apologized to the Walshes for mistakes made early in the investigation, before he became chief. The loss of that evidence sits at the center of those mistakes. By 1996, the case was fifteen years old. In March of that year, the Seattle Times published a long article under a headline describing how the six-year-old's murder still haunted the nation. Hollywood's police chief at the time, Richard Witt, was quoted saying that it was a physical impossibility for Jimmy Campbell to have been involved. The investigation was still open. No one had been charged. And Ottis Toole was running out of time. On September 15, 1996, Ottis Toole died in Florida State Prison at Raiford, in north Florida. He was forty-nine years old. He died of liver failure brought on by cirrhosis, while serving life sentences for murders unrelated to Adam. He had never been charged in Adam's death. He would never face a trial for it, never hear evidence presented against him, never have a lawyer test that evidence before a jury. Whatever he knew, he took it with him. Or almost all of it. John Walsh would later say that a niece of Toole's had come forward and reported that her uncle confessed to her, before he died, that he had killed Adam. That account comes from her, as relayed by John Walsh, and it was never tested in a court. It added one more voice to the story, but it could not replace the evidence that had been lost. In 1998, the FBI's national DNA index began operating, allowing crime scene profiles to be searched across the country. It would go on to help solve a great many cases across the country. It could not help this one. There was nothing left to test.

After Toole's death, the case drifted into a strange kind of limbo. The man most people associated with Adam's murder was dead. The physical evidence that might have tested his confession was gone. The investigation was still officially open, but there was no one left to arrest and nothing left to analyze. For many police departments, that is where a case like this stops moving. It sits in a file. It comes up on anniversaries. And slowly, the people who worked it retire. But the Walsh family did not let it stop. And in the years that followed, the national work they had started kept growing. In 2003, Congress passed a law requiring federal office buildings to adopt the Code Adam protocol, so that a child lost in a federal building would get the same immediate response that a child lost in a Walmart would get. On July 27, 2006, exactly twenty-five years to the day after Adam disappeared from the Sears store, President George W. Bush stood in the Rose Garden of the White House and signed the Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act into law. John and Revé Walsh were there. In his remarks, the president recalled that Adam had been abducted from a department store twenty-five years earlier and later found murdered. The new law created national standards for registering and tracking convicted sex offenders, a section known as the Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act. It sorted offenders into three tiers according to the seriousness of their crimes and set a deadline, July 27, 2009, for states and other jurisdictions to bring their own systems up to those standards. Twenty-five years earlier, there had been no national system for a missing child. Now there was a federal statute that carried that child's full name. And still there was no official answer about who had killed him. That is where the human hero of this story comes back in. Joe Matthews, the Miami Beach homicide detective and polygraph examiner who had been consulted on the case just four days after Adam disappeared, had been on the case and off it again for years. By the mid-2000s, he was retired. About twenty-five years after the abduction, John and Revé Walsh asked him to conduct an independent review of the entire case. Matthews accepted. He went back through the files, the reports, the confessions, the recantations, the evidence that remained and the evidence that did not. What he found, as he later described it, was a case where important material had not been properly examined. Among the items he described were the ninety-eight photographs of Toole's Cadillac that investigators from the Florida Department of Law Enforcement had taken back in the early 1980s. According to Matthews, those photographs had sat without being properly developed and examined by the investigators running the case. When he looked at them, Matthews said, he saw what he believed was evidence that Adam had been in that car. We are not going to describe those images. It is enough to know that a veteran homicide detective looked at them and believed they connected the boy to the vehicle. Matthews also reviewed other material that had been gathered over the years. John Walsh would later say that investigators had found, at a home connected to Toole in Jacksonville, a pair of green shorts and a sandal similar to what Adam had been wearing. That account comes from John Walsh, and like the photographs, it was never tested in a courtroom. Matthews also had to deal with the other names that had circled the case. Over the years, some people had suggested a very different suspect, Jeffrey Dahmer, who was living in South Florida in 1981, years before he was arrested in Milwaukee in 1991 for a series of murders there. The theory drew attention, and it still appears in documentaries and articles today. But Dahmer was questioned about Adam while he was in prison, in 1992, and he repeatedly denied any involvement. He was never charged in connection with Adam's case, and no physical evidence has ever been made public linking him to it. When Hollywood police closed the case, they said that their review of the file pointed to only one person, and it was not Dahmer. Matthews came to a clear conclusion. In his judgment, Ottis Toole was the man who had abducted and killed Adam Walsh. He put his findings in a report and submitted it to the police and to prosecutors before the case was closed. Later, he would tell the full story in a book co-written with the novelist Les Standiford, published in 2011 under the title Bringing Adam Home. It is worth pausing on what kind of breakthrough this was, because it was not the kind this channel usually describes. There was no DNA match. There was no genealogist building a family tree. There was no discarded coffee cup, no surveillance team, no laboratory result arriving at midnight. The evidence that might have produced that kind of breakthrough had been lost in 1994. What remained was paper. Confessions, recantations, photographs, reports, statements. A breakthrough in this case meant a person willing to read all of it again, with fresh eyes, and to say out loud what he believed it meant. Inside the Hollywood Police Department, there was now a new chief. His name was Chadwick Wagner, and he had not been in charge during the early years of the investigation. He reviewed the case. He looked at the material Matthews had assembled and at the department's own files. And he reached a decision that no Hollywood police chief had reached in more than a quarter of a century. He decided to close the case and to name the person the department believed was responsible. Ottis Toole could not be arrested. He could not be charged. He could not be tried. He had been dead for twelve years. There is a mechanism in American policing for exactly this situation. Under the national crime reporting rules used by the FBI, a department can clear a case by what are called exceptional means when it has identified the offender, believes it has enough evidence to support an arrest and a charge, and is prevented from making that arrest by something outside its control, such as the death of the offender. It is not a conviction. It is not a finding of guilt by any court. It is a police department's official statement of what it believes. But the department could do something it had never done before. It could stand in public and say who it believed had killed Adam Walsh, and it could say it to his parents face to face. The decision was not without controversy, even before it was announced. Toole had confessed and recanted. His associate Lucas was a notorious false confessor. The physical evidence was gone. Critics would argue that closing a case on the word of a dead man, with no new physical evidence, was a convenient ending rather than a proven one. Supporters would argue that the totality of the circumstantial evidence was overwhelming, and that the family had waited long enough. Both arguments would still be heard years later. By December 2008, the Hollywood Police Department was ready to make an announcement, and John and Revé Walsh would be in the room when it did. Twenty-seven years after Revé stepped away from a video game display for a few minutes, the police were finally ready to say a name. The Walshes went to Hollywood. And Revé brought a photograph with her.

On Tuesday, December 16, 2008, the Hollywood Police Department held a news conference in Hollywood, Florida. The cameras were there because the name Adam Walsh still meant something to almost everyone in the country. John Walsh was there, the father who had spent twenty years on television asking the public to help catch other people's fugitives. Revé Walsh was there. At one point, she placed a small photograph of Adam on the podium. The smiling boy. The face the country had known since 1981. Twenty-seven years after he disappeared, Adam was in the room. Chief Chadwick Wagner stepped forward and announced that the department was closing the investigation into the abduction and murder of Adam Walsh. The department, he said, had concluded that the person responsible was Ottis Toole. Then the chief said the sentence that would be quoted in newspapers across the country the next morning. If Ottis Toole were alive today, he said, he would be arrested for the abduction and murder of Adam Walsh. Listen carefully to what that sentence does and does not say. It does not say Toole was convicted. He was not. It does not say a court found him guilty. No court ever heard the case. It says that, in the judgment of the Hollywood Police Department, the evidence would have been enough to arrest him. Toole had died twelve years earlier, and so there would be no arrest, no charge, no trial and no verdict. Ottis Toole was never charged or convicted in Adam's death. Police named him. The chief was also candid about what the department did not have. He said there was no single piece of evidence that had led police to name Toole, but rather a large body of circumstantial evidence. And when reporters pressed him about what was new, he answered with a line that the Associated Press reported. If you're looking for that magic wand or that hidden document that just appeared, he said, it's not there. There was no new DNA result. There was no new witness. The physical evidence that might have provided scientific proof had been lost years before. The Associated Press summed it up bluntly. There were no DNA or blood tests and no slam-dunk eyewitness accounts. What the department had was the totality of what it had gathered over twenty-seven years, read again, and weighed. And then the chief did something that families of victims rarely hear from a police department. He apologized. Wagner apologized to John and Revé Walsh for mistakes that he said had been made early in the investigation, before he became chief. He acknowledged missteps. He said he regretted that the case had not been closed sooner, and he attributed that failure, in part, to flaws in his own department's investigation. For a family that had learned in 1994 that the car and the carpet were gone, those words carried a weight that no outsider can fully measure. Then John Walsh spoke. He had spent most of his adult life asking for answers, and he had been told for decades that there might never be one. According to the Associated Press, he was emotional as he spoke. We needed to know, he said. We needed to know. And today we know. He described the years of not knowing as a torture, and he said that journey was over. He also made clear that he was not there to attack the department that had made so many mistakes in his son's case. This is not to look back and point fingers, he said, but it is to let it rest. Think about that phrase. Let it rest. For twenty-seven years, nothing about this case had been at rest. Not the investigation, not the evidence, not the suspect, and not the family. A confession had been followed by a recantation. A car had been examined and then lost. A man had been named in the press for years and had died without ever facing a charge. And now, finally, the official question had an official answer. For John and Revé Walsh, that answer mattered. John Walsh had said for years that he believed Toole was the man. Now the police said so too. But an official answer is not the same thing as proof, and not everyone accepted it. In the days that followed, reporters and commentators raised questions. The Associated Press published an article noting that questions lingered in the Adam Walsh probe, pointing out once again that there was no DNA evidence and no new physical evidence. A South Florida weekly ran a commentary arguing that the announcement contained essentially no new information and questioning whether a long-running case had been closed because it was convenient to close it. The author Arthur Jay Harris, who had studied the case for years, went further, disputing the conclusion and raising questions about the original identification itself. His views remain disputed, and the official position has never changed. Later, the decision would be examined in other ways. In 2011, an investigative news site in South Florida reported on a public records lawsuit seeking the release of the report Joe Matthews had submitted to police and prosecutors before the case was closed. The same reporting described disputes over the chief's sworn account of how the 2008 decision was reached, including an official who said he had not attended a meeting the chief described. Those questions were about process, about how the decision was made and what it rested on, and they are part of the honest record of this case. So where does that leave the legal truth? It leaves it here. Adam Walsh was abducted on July 27, 1981, and murdered. Ottis Toole confessed to the crime in 1983 and later recanted, and confessed and recanted again. He died in prison in 1996 while serving sentences for other murders. He was never charged with Adam's murder and never convicted of it. On December 16, 2008, the Hollywood Police Department officially closed the case and named him as the person it believed was responsible. That is the full and careful statement of what is known. Anything beyond it is opinion. For the Walsh family, the news conference was not a celebration. There was no courtroom, no sentence, no moment where a judge looked a killer in the eye. There was a podium, a small photograph, an apology and a name. It was, in the language of this channel, a kind of justice. Not the justice of a verdict, but the justice of being told the truth as the police understood it, and of hearing a police chief say out loud that his department had failed them. Twenty-seven years, four months and nineteen days after a little boy stopped to watch a video game, his parents walked out of a news conference with an answer. Count it in days, and the number is ten thousand and four. Ten thousand and four mornings of waking up without knowing. Ten thousand and four nights. In that time, the Walshes had seen three more children born, two presidents sign laws connected to their son, a television program built on their pain capture more than a thousand fugitives, and a code word with Adam's name spread into stores across the country. And through every one of those days, the most basic question had stayed open. And the question became what that answer, and everything that came before it, would leave behind.

In the years after the case was closed, the story of Adam Walsh did not fade. If anything, it became more detailed. In 2011, Joe Matthews and the novelist Les Standiford published Bringing Adam Home, the book that laid out Matthews's review of the investigation and his conclusion that Ottis Toole was the killer. The book described the evidence that Matthews said had been overlooked for decades, including the ninety-eight photographs of Toole's Cadillac taken by the Florida Department of Law Enforcement, and material that, as one newspaper put it, had sat in a crime lab in Tallahassee for years without investigators looking at it. Reviewers and reporters used the book to revisit a question that had never really gone away. How could so much go so wrong in one of the most famous child murder cases in America? A newspaper in Tampa Bay ran its review of the book under a headline calling it a case of criminal errors. The skeptics did not go away either. Arthur Jay Harris continued to argue that the official conclusion was wrong, and that the case should be considered unsolved. The theory involving Jeffrey Dahmer continued to appear in articles and programs, even though Dahmer denied any involvement when questioned and was never charged in Adam's case. And the Hollywood Police Department's position did not change. The case remained closed. Ottis Toole remained the person the department named. That is where it still stands today, in October 2026. Forty-five years after Adam disappeared, the official record has not moved. No court has ever ruled on who killed Adam Walsh, and no court ever will, because the man police named died in 1996 without being charged. There has been no DNA test that settles the question, because there is nothing left to test. The rest of Adam's remains have never been found. Those are the open questions this case still carries. Where the rest of his remains are. Exactly how and when the car and the carpet left police custody. And whether, if that carpet had been kept, a laboratory could have turned a police conclusion into scientific proof. On July 27, 2026, South Florida marked forty-five years since Adam disappeared from the Hollywood Mall, with local news coverage revisiting the case and the investigation that once considered other suspects. That same date marked twenty years since the Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act was signed in the Rose Garden. Two anniversaries on one day. One for a loss. One for what the loss built. Because this is the other half of Adam's story, and it is the half that touches almost every family in America. In 1981, there was no national computer system dedicated to finding missing children. Since a law signed in 1982, the information of a missing child can be placed in the FBI's national crime computer, so that police anywhere in the country can see it. In 1981, there was no national center for missing children. Since 1984, there has been one, opened at the White House with a president crediting the courage of John and Revé Walsh. In 1981, there was no television program dedicated to asking the public to help find the people who hurt others. From 1988, there was one, hosted by Adam's father, and it helped capture more than one thousand fugitives. In 1981, there were no national standards for registering and tracking convicted sex offenders. Since 2006, the national standards for that system, with three tiers and requirements for every state, have carried Adam's full name. In 1981, a child lost in a department store had no procedure designed to protect him. Today, in stores, malls, museums and federal buildings across the country, an employee can call a Code Adam, and the doors are watched, and the staff stop and search, and if the child is not found within minutes, the police are called. It is impossible to count how many children have been found quickly because of those changes. No one can say how many lives they saved, and this channel will not invent a number. But it is not hard to imagine a parent somewhere, in a store, in a panic, hearing two words over a loudspeaker and watching a dozen strangers start to search. Those two words are a little boy's name. Now let us go back to where this story began. A Sears store at the Hollywood Mall. A Monday in the summer of 1981. A display in the toy department, with a new Atari video game system glowing on the screen and older boys crowding around it. A mother saying yes, because almost any mother would have said yes. A few minutes. That is all it was. A few minutes, and the life of a family, the work of a police department, and the laws of a country were changed forever. Remember who Adam was. He was the first child of John and Revé Walsh. He was born on November 14, 1974. He was six years old. On the last afternoon anyone who loved him saw him, he wanted to watch the big kids play a video game. He was missing his two front baby teeth. He never got to be anything other than a little boy. And yet his name is written into federal law, into the training of store employees, into the history of American television, and into the memory of millions of people who were children themselves in 1981 and grew up seeing his face. If there is one lesson in Adam's story, it is not about blaming a mother for a few minutes, or a teenage guard for doing a job, or even a single detective.
