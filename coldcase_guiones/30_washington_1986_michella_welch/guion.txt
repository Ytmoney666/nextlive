Laboratories learned to work with smaller and older samples. The methods became standardized. And in 1998, the FBI's national DNA index began operating, linking state databases so that a profile from a crime scene in one place could be compared with the profiles of convicted offenders across the country. Washington State, like every other state, contributed to that system. For the first time, a profile from an old crime could be searched against hundreds of thousands, and eventually millions, of known offenders. At some point in those years, as the technology matured, investigators had a male DNA profile from the evidence in Michella's case. It was exactly the kind of evidence the new databases had been built for. It was compared against the offenders in the system. It matched no one. To understand why, you have to understand what those databases are and what they are not. The national DNA index is a list of people who have already passed through the criminal justice system in particular ways, mostly people convicted of certain crimes, and in some states people arrested for them. If the person who left the DNA has never been convicted of a qualifying offense, or was convicted before the rules required a sample, he is not in the system. The database is like a phone book that only lists people who have already been in trouble. If your man is not in the book, you can search it forever and never find him. For the families, this was a particular kind of torment. They were told that the evidence existed. They were told that DNA could solve cases like this one. They watched on the news as other cases were solved. In the Seattle area itself, the Green River killings, the case that had cast such a long shadow over the region, were finally linked to a man in 2001 through DNA, after nearly twenty years. Science was clearly capable of miracles. It just was not producing one for Michella. Detectives came and went. The original investigators retired or moved on to other assignments. The binders from 1986 stayed on the shelves, waiting for someone with the time and the will to open them again. Every case like this one has a moment when it could be quietly forgotten, when the last detective who remembers it retires and the files become just another row of boxes. That someone arrived in 2009. Her name was Lindsey Wade, a detective with the Tacoma Police Department who was assigned to cold cases. Working alongside her in the cold case unit was another detective, Gene Miller. Wade reopened the files on both girls, Michella Welch and Jennifer Bastian, cases that by then were more than two decades old and that were still, in the minds of most people, a single mystery with a single killer. Wade did something that sounds simple and is, in fact, enormous. She went through the old files, dozens of binders of reports, and made a list of every man whose name appeared in them. Every suspect, every person interviewed, every name mentioned in a tip. When she was done, according to reporting by NBC News, the list contained about 2,300 names. Two thousand three hundred. That is the ledger of the first two decades of this case. Thousands of names, written down by investigators who were trying their best. Years of tips, interviews, and theories. And still, no answer. Then, in November 2013, came the moment that would break the case open, though it did not look like a breakthrough at first. The Washington State Patrol Crime Laboratory tested a piece of evidence from the other case, a swimsuit that Jennifer Bastian had been wearing. It had been in storage since 1986. According to NBC News, it had gone untested for decades. When it was finally examined with modern methods, the laboratory developed a male DNA profile from it. And that profile did not match the DNA from Michella's case. Think about what that meant. For twenty-seven years, from 1986 to 2013, the investigation had been built on the belief that one man had killed both girls. Now the science said something completely different. The man who killed Jennifer Bastian was not the man who killed Michella Welch. There were two killers. Two different men had taken two different girls from two different parks in the same city in the same year, four and a half months apart. Neither of them had been caught. Speaking about the DNA results years later, Lindsey Wade told NBC News, that was a big turning point in the investigation. It was more than a turning point. It meant that every assumption built on the single-killer theory had to be thrown out. Every suspect who had been cleared because he could not have committed both crimes had to be reconsidered. The two investigations had to be separated and rebuilt, each on its own foundation. It was, in a sense, like starting over, twenty-seven years late. Here is the system-failure ledger, in numbers. Twelve years old, the age of the victim. Twenty-seven years, the length of time two cases were treated as one. Decades, the time a key piece of evidence in the companion case sat untested. Two thousand three hundred, the number of names in the files. Zero, the number of database matches for the DNA from Michella's case. Add one more number. Thirty-two. That is how many times the anniversary of March 26 would pass before anyone was arrested. No single person caused those numbers. They were the product of the limits of science, of a reasonable assumption that went unchallenged, and of a database that could only find people who were already in it. But they add up to an enormous cost, paid by two families, year after year. The detectives now had two DNA profiles, two unknown men, and two cases that each needed a new path forward. The databases had failed. The old leads had failed. What they needed was a completely different kind of science, one that could find a man who had never been arrested at all. That science was about to arrive. And it would come not from a police laboratory, but from people looking for their own grandparents.

To understand how Michella's case was finally solved, you need to understand two very different kinds of DNA test, and why one of them could not do the job while the other could. The first kind is the one police had been using since the late 1980s and 1990s. Forensic scientists call it an STR profile. You can think of it as a short barcode. It looks at a small number of places in a person's DNA where the code repeats itself a different number of times from one person to the next. Two samples with the same barcode almost certainly come from the same person. It is extraordinarily good at answering one question: is this the man. But it is not designed to answer another question: who is this man related to. An STR profile can tell you that a sample matches a suspect, or that it matches someone already in a database. If there is no suspect and no database match, it has nowhere to go. That is exactly where Michella's case sat for decades. The second kind of test is the one used by consumer DNA companies, the kits people buy to learn about their ancestry or to find relatives. Instead of a short barcode, these tests read hundreds of thousands of tiny points across a person's DNA, called SNPs. Because so much information is read, the results can reveal not only who you are, but who you are related to. If two people share long stretches of identical DNA, they are family. The more they share, the closer the relationship. Parents and children share about half. First cousins share less. Second and third cousins share less still, but often enough to be detected. Imagine a huge family photo album with most of the pages missing. If you find a few distant cousins, and you know how much DNA each one shares with the unknown man, you can start to work out which page of the album he belongs on. In 2010, two volunteers launched a free website called GEDmatch. The idea was simple. People who had tested with different companies could upload their raw DNA results to one place and compare them, looking for cousins they had never met. It was a tool built for hobbyists and family historians, for people trying to break through brick walls in their family trees, or for adoptees trying to find their birth parents. No one designed it to catch killers. But it held exactly what police had never had: a large, searchable collection of DNA from ordinary people who had never been arrested for anything. Here is how the method works, in plain words. Start with the unknown DNA from a crime scene. If enough of it survives, a laboratory can read it the same way a consumer company would, creating a SNP profile. That profile is compared with the profiles of people who chose to upload their DNA to a public database. Most of the time, the killer himself is not there. But some of his relatives might be, often distant cousins who share a great-grandparent or a great-great-grandparent with him. Genealogists then take those matches and build family trees backward, using public records: birth and death certificates, marriage records, obituaries, census sheets, old newspaper notices. They search for the place where the trees of different matches meet, the couple from whom all of those cousins descend. Then they build the tree forward again, generation by generation, looking for a man of the right age, in the right place, at the right time. The result is not proof. It is a lead. It tells detectives where to look. The proof comes later, from the old-fashioned STR test, when police obtain a sample from the man himself and compare it directly with the crime-scene DNA. The method became famous in April 2018. On April 24 of that year, police in California announced the arrest of a man accused of being the Golden State Killer, a serial offender who had committed a string of murders and rapes in the 1970s and 1980s and had evaded detection for more than forty years. Investigators revealed that they had found him by uploading crime-scene DNA to GEDmatch and building family trees from distant cousins. Overnight, investigators across the country realized that hundreds of cold cases might suddenly be solvable. But in Tacoma, detectives had already started down that road. In 2016, before the world had heard of the technique, the Tacoma Police Department began working with a genetic genealogist on Michella's case. That same year, in April 2016, a company called Parabon NanoLabs, based in Virginia, released something called DNA phenotyping reports on the two Tacoma cases. Phenotyping is a prediction of what a person might look like, based on his DNA: ancestry, eye color, hair color, skin tone, even a likely face shape. Parabon's announcement made it public that the evidence pointed to two different male suspects, each believed to be individually responsible for one of the two deaths. For the first time, the public was told plainly what the laboratory had shown in 2013. There was not one predator in the parks of Tacoma in 1986. There were two. The announcement came with computer-generated composite images of the two predicted men, and the cases were back in the news. According to KIRO 7, Tacoma police collected new tips on the two 1986 murders. None of them, on their own, solved either case. The composites were predictions, not photographs, and they could not tell anyone a name. But they kept both girls in the news at a moment when the investigation was about to take its biggest step since 1986. Parabon would also play a role in the genealogy itself. According to the Washington Court of Appeals, investigators learned that several of the killer's cousins had DNA in the GEDmatch database, and Parabon used information from that database, together with public records, to construct family trees. Branch by branch, the unknown man who had left his DNA in the gulch behind Puget Park began to take shape, not as a face, but as a place in a family. Before Michella's case reached its end, the other case moved first. In May 2018, Tacoma detectives approached a man named Robert Washburn, who was living in Eureka, Illinois, and asked him for a sample of his DNA. Later reporting credited a combination of DNA, help from genetic genealogy, and old-fashioned police work for leading them to him. He gave the sample. It matched the DNA from Jennifer Bastian's swimsuit. Washburn was arrested at his home in Illinois and brought back to Pierce County to face a charge of first-degree murder in Jenni's death. Investigators had found no evidence linking him to Michella Welch. The second unknown man, the one from Puget Park, was still out there. But the science had now proved, beyond any doubt, that it could work in Tacoma. Nearly thirty-two years after two girls were taken, one of the two men had a name. For Michella's family, the news carried two truths at once. Another family was finally getting an answer. Their own wait went on. But the detectives working Michella's case were by then closer than anyone outside the investigation knew. The family tree had narrowed. The branches had been followed forward. And by the beginning of June 2018, the trail of cousins led to one family in the north end of Tacoma, and to two brothers who had lived there in 1986.

Every cold case that is finally solved has, somewhere at its center, a person who would not let it go. In this case, that person was not a single hero, but a detective who had picked up a stack of binders in 2009 and decided that two girls from 1986 deserved more than an annual mention in the newspaper. Lindsey Wade had spent years on these cases by the spring of 2018. She had compiled the list of 2,300 names. She had been on the cases when the testing showed there were two killers, not one. She had worked alongside her partner, Gene Miller, and with the laboratory and the genealogists, as the science caught up with the evidence. And now, in the space of a few weeks, the work of nearly a decade was about to pay off twice. The genealogy work on Michella's case had followed a path that is now familiar to anyone who studies these investigations. The DNA from the evidence collected in 1986 had been turned into a profile that could be compared against GEDmatch. Several distant relatives of the unknown man appeared there, people who had uploaded their own DNA for their own reasons, with no idea that they were connected to a murder. The genealogists built their family trees backward from those cousins, found the point where the trees met, and then built forward again, generation by generation, following the descendants down to the present day. At the end of that process, the tree pointed to one family. In that family, there were two brothers who had been living in the north end of Tacoma in 1986, the same part of the city where Puget Park sits. One of them was a man named Gary Charles Hartman. The other was his younger brother. It is important to be clear about the brother. He has never been charged with anything in this case. He simply happened to be one of the two men the family tree could not tell apart, and the only fair way to resolve that was science. Genealogy can narrow a search to a family, sometimes to a small group of siblings. It almost never names one man on its own. When brothers are involved, the DNA of the crime scene will look nearly the same from the outside, because brothers share the same parents and the same ancestors. The only way to know which brother left the DNA, if either of them did, is to compare a sample from each man directly. And to do that, the police needed something from them. In early June 2018, Tacoma detectives began surveillance on both brothers. They watched where they went. They waited for an opportunity to collect something that carried their DNA, an object discarded in a public place, something any person might throw away without a second thought. In legal terms, it is called abandoned property. When a person throws something away in a public place, courts have generally held that he no longer has a reasonable expectation of privacy in it. Police can pick it up, just as anyone else could. That opportunity came at a restaurant. A detective who was following Gary Hartman watched him use a napkin and leave it behind. The detective collected it as evidence. It was, in every way, an ordinary object. A paper napkin. The kind of thing that is thrown away by the millions every day in every restaurant in the country. But on that napkin was the one thing that thirty-two years of police work had never been able to obtain: a sample of the man's DNA that could be compared, directly, with the DNA preserved from Michella's case. The napkin went to the Washington State Patrol Crime Laboratory. There, scientists used the traditional test, the short barcode, the STR profile that police had relied on for decades. They compared the DNA from the napkin with the DNA collected during Michella's autopsy in 1986. On June 19, 2018, the laboratory called the Tacoma Police Department with the result. They matched. After thirty-two years, the second unknown man had a name. So who was Gary Hartman? He was sixty-six years old. He lived in Lakewood, a city just south of Tacoma. He was a registered nurse. At the time of his arrest, according to multiple news reports, he worked at Western State Hospital in Lakewood, the state's large psychiatric hospital, where he was described as a community nurse specialist. And according to records cited in news coverage, in 1986, the year Michella was killed, he had lived less than two miles from Puget Park. Less than two miles. When Michella rode her bike home for the sandwiches that Wednesday, the man who would be convicted of her murder lived close enough that it would have taken only minutes to drive to the park. He was not a drifter passing through Tacoma. He was not a stranger from another state. He was a man from the neighborhood, in the broad sense of that word, a man whose life ran through the same streets as hers. Then and now. In March 1986, Gary Hartman was in his early thirties. Michella was twelve. In June 2018, he was sixty-six. Michella would have been in her mid-forties. Hartman had lived those thirty-two years. He had worked. He had grown older in the same region where the crime had happened. Nothing in the public record of the case suggests that anyone, in all those years, had connected him to the murder. His DNA was not in the offender databases, and that is why the profile had never matched. He had simply gone on with his life. The detectives had their match on Tuesday, June 19. They did not wait. On Wednesday, June 20, 2018, officers pulled Gary Hartman over in a traffic stop in Lakewood and arrested him. It had been thirty-two years and eighty-six days since Michella disappeared from Puget Park. Think about the two days side by side. On a Wednesday in 1986, a twelve-year-old girl rode her bike home for lunch and never came home again. On a Wednesday in 2018, the man who would be convicted of her murder was stopped on a road in Lakewood, and the life he had built over three decades came to an end. Hartman was booked into the Pierce County Jail. Within days, the arrest was national news. Television stations across the country told the story of a napkin and a family tree. The headline wrote itself: a twelve-year-old's murder, solved after thirty-two years by DNA left on a restaurant napkin. In Tacoma, Police Chief Donald Ramsdell described the case as a great example of advancements in DNA identification and computer modeling combined with tried and true police techniques. It was both of those things. It was the newest science in the world, and it was the oldest kind of police work, a detective following a man and waiting for him to throw something away. For Michella's family, it was something else entirely. It was the phone call they had waited for since 1986. But an arrest is not a conviction. Gary Hartman had not admitted anything. He had a right to a defense, a right to a trial, and a right to be presumed innocent until the State proved its case. And the road from that traffic stop to a courtroom verdict would take almost four more years.

On Friday, June 22, 2018, two days after the traffic stop in Lakewood, prosecutors in Pierce County formally charged Gary Charles Hartman with first-degree murder and first-degree rape in the death of Michella Welch. The charging papers laid out the path the investigation had taken: the DNA preserved from 1986, the genealogy, the two brothers, the surveillance, the napkin, and the laboratory match. Bail was set at five million dollars. On Monday, June 25, 2018, Hartman appeared in Pierce County Superior Court for his arraignment. He pleaded not guilty. And sitting in that courtroom, facing the man accused of killing their daughter and sister, were Michella's mother, Barbara Leonard, and her sisters. It was the first time in thirty-two years that they had been in the same room with the person the State said was responsible. One local news headline from that day carried the family's feeling in four words: finally, a bit of closure. It was only a bit. An arraignment is not a verdict. It is the first step on a long road, and the family knew it. But after more than three decades without a name, even a bit of closure was more than they had been given in a lifetime of anniversaries. Nicole Eby spoke to reporters around that time. She spoke about the unfairness of it, about a precious life cut short. She also spoke about forgiveness, something she would return to again and again in the years ahead. She said that she forgave the man who had taken her sister. She explained that holding on to anger would only hurt her. That kind of statement is rare, and it is worth pausing on. It does not mean that she believed he should go free. It means that she had decided, after thirty-two years, that he would not take anything more from her. About seven months later, the other Tacoma case reached its end. In January 2019, Robert Washburn appeared in Pierce County Superior Court before Judge Elizabeth Martin and pleaded guilty to the first-degree murder of Jennifer Bastian. He said very little, answering the judge's questions with yes or no. Instead of speaking, he relied on a written confession, which the judge read aloud in court. In it, he admitted that he had grabbed Jenni as she rode her bike in Point Defiance Park on August 4, 1986, taken her into the woods, and strangled her. He wrote that he wanted to spare her family from having to go through a trial. He was sentenced to roughly twenty-six and a half to twenty-seven years in prison, depending on the report. Nothing in that case connected him to Michella. The two cases that had been treated as one for twenty-seven years ended, in the courtroom, as what they had always been. Two crimes. Two men. Two families. That same year, in 2019, Washington State changed its law, with Jennifer Bastian's family and Tacoma detectives among those pushing for it. It became known as Jennifer and Michella's Law. Among other things, it expanded the collection of DNA samples in Washington, including from people convicted of felonies and of indecent exposure, and it allowed police to submit biological samples from offenders who had died. The idea behind it was simple and painful. If the right DNA had been in the database years earlier, families like theirs might not have waited three decades. Michella's name was now attached, alongside Jenni's, to a law meant to spare other families the same wait. Hartman's case, meanwhile, moved slowly. Serious felony cases often take years to reach trial, and this one involved a crime from 1986, evidence handled across four decades, and a method, genetic genealogy, that was still new to the courts. The years from 2018 to 2022 also included the pandemic, which slowed courts across the country. For the family, it meant more waiting. Then, on Tuesday, March 22, 2022, the case came to its conclusion in a way that many people did not expect. Hartman did not go before a jury. Instead, his case was decided at what is called a stipulated facts bench trial. In plain words, this means that the defense and the prosecution agree on the facts the judge will consider, and the judge alone decides whether those facts prove the crime. It is not a guilty plea. The defendant does not stand up and say he is guilty. He gives up his right to a jury, and the case is decided on an agreed record. In Washington, a defendant who goes this route can generally still appeal the legal rulings made before trial, and, as we will see, Hartman did exactly that. But it is also not a contested trial, with witnesses and cross-examination and days of argument. According to the Washington Court of Appeals, Hartman stipulated that the DNA from the napkin he had used matched the DNA from Michella's body. The State's charge was first-degree felony murder, with the rape of the twelve-year-old girl as the underlying felony. Felony murder is a legal term that sounds complicated but is not. Under Washington law, a person who causes a death while committing certain serious crimes can be convicted of first-degree murder, whether or not the State proves a separate plan to kill. In this case, the underlying crime was the attack on the child. The law, in other words, did not ask the court to measure his intentions. It asked whether he had done it. The trial lasted less than two hours. Less than two hours, after thirty-six years. Pierce County Superior Court Judge Stanley Rumbaugh found Gary Hartman, now seventy years old, guilty of first-degree murder. Seventy years old. On the day of the verdict, he had lived more than five times as long as the girl he was convicted of killing. Michella, had she lived, would have been close to fifty. After the verdict, Hartman spoke. According to KOMO News, he apologized to Michella's family, and he said, I'm so sorry. Then the family spoke. Barbara Leonard asked the judge for the maximum sentence. According to reporting from that day, she said, in substance, that he should be locked up and the key thrown away. She acknowledged that nothing the court did would bring Michella back, but she said that justice would have been served. Nicole Eby spoke about her sister. She called Michella like a mom, a second mom to me. And she spoke again about forgiveness. She said she forgives him, because when she does not, in her words, it brings death to my life in one way or another. Michella's other sister, Angela Velasquez, also addressed the court. She urged Hartman to rely on God. Then Judge Rumbaugh imposed the sentence. Twenty-six years and six months in prison. One report gave the figure as three hundred and twenty months, which is slightly longer, but most coverage reported twenty-six and a half years. The judge noted what that number meant for a man of seventy. He said that Hartman would likely die in prison. Thirty-six years earlier, Michella had ridden her bike home for sandwiches. Now, in a courtroom in Tacoma, the man who had taken her had been convicted and sentenced. Her mother had lived to see it. Her sisters had lived to see it. And for the family, the question now was not what happened. It was what came after.

The verdict was not quite the final word. Gary Hartman appealed. His appeal went to the heart of the method that had found him. Genetic genealogy, after all, works through the DNA of people who never agreed to help police: cousins, distant relatives, strangers who uploaded their DNA to learn about their ancestors. Was it lawful for the police to use their DNA to find him, and then to take a napkin he had left behind and test it? Those were the kinds of questions courts across the country were beginning to face. On August 22, 2023, the Washington Court of Appeals issued its decision in State versus Hartman. The court affirmed the conviction. It agreed with the trial court that Hartman had no privacy interest in his cousins' DNA in the GEDmatch database, because those relatives had chosen to upload it themselves. And it found that he had no privacy interest in DNA he had abandoned, whether at the crime scene in 1986 or on a napkin in a restaurant in 2018. Hartman then asked the Washington Supreme Court to review the case, and the State filed its answer opposing review. As of this video, his conviction stands, and he remains in the custody of the Washington Department of Corrections, serving the sentence imposed in March 2022. In the years since 2018, the world of genetic genealogy has continued to change. Databases have changed their rules about police access. Courts and lawmakers have continued to argue about where the lines should be drawn. Those debates matter, and they will go on. But in Michella's case, a court has already looked closely at how the evidence was obtained, and found that it was lawful. Now step back and look at the whole story. Two girls from Tacoma were taken in 1986, four and a half months apart. For twenty-seven years, nearly everyone believed one man had killed them both. That belief was reasonable, and it was wrong. A swimsuit, untested for decades, finally proved it in 2013. Genetic genealogy, a tool built by hobbyists looking for their own family history, helped point detectives toward Robert Washburn in the Bastian case, according to later reporting, and led them to the Hartman family in Michella's. A detective following a man to a restaurant picked up a paper napkin. And a laboratory, comparing that napkin with a sample preserved since the night of March 26, 1986, gave Michella's family the answer they had waited thirty-two years to hear. There are lessons in that, and they are not only about science. The first is about evidence. None of this would have been possible if the people who collected the evidence in 1986 had not preserved it, and if everyone who touched it in the decades that followed had not kept it safe. The most important witness in this case did not speak until 2018, but it was there the whole time. The second lesson is about assumptions. The belief that one man killed both girls was not malicious. It was the kind of conclusion any experienced investigator might reach. But it stood for twenty-seven years without being tested, and every year it stood, it shaped the search. A theory is a tool, not a fact. When it cannot be proven, it has to be questioned. The third lesson is about people. The science was new. The databases were new. But the case was solved by a detective who opened old binders in 2009 and counted 2,300 names, by a partner who worked beside her, by genealogists who built trees from strangers' records, by laboratory scientists, and by a family that never let the city forget a twelve-year-old girl. Washington's law now carries her name next to Jenni's, so that the next family might wait less. Remember how this story began, with a promise that it would be about three things. A girl who was looking after the little ones on an ordinary day. A mother and two sisters who waited more than three decades for the truth. And detectives who refused to let a twelve-year-old be filed away. All three are still here, at the end. The girl is still twelve. Her family is still speaking her name. And the detectives who opened those binders did what they set out to do. Jenni Bastian's family, a few miles away, got their answer in the same season. Two cases that were once treated as one are now closed, separately, as they always should have been. And then there is the family itself. Barbara Leonard lost her oldest daughter on a Wednesday in March 1986. She waited thirty-two years for an arrest and thirty-six for a conviction, and she was there in the courtroom for both. Nicole Eby, the little sister who came back to an empty picnic table, grew up to say something many people would find impossible. She said she forgave the man who killed her sister, not to excuse him, but because, as she put it, when she does not, it brings death to her life in one way or another. Angela Velasquez, the other sister, told the man who had taken Michella from them to rely on God. Whatever you believe, those are the words of people who refused to let what happened in the gulch define the rest of their lives. If Michella were alive today, in 2026, she would be in her early fifties. Who she would have become, what work she would have done, whether she would still be the one her sisters call when something goes wrong, we will never know. What we do know is the shape of her last morning, and it is the same shape it was in the first minutes of this story. On Wednesday, March 26, 1986, a twelve-year-old girl named Michella Welch rode her bike home to pick up lunch for her two little sisters. She brought it back to the park. She set it on the table. Her sisters walked away for half an hour. When they came back, her bike was there, and the lunch was there, and she was gone. That is how this story began. But it is not all that Michella was. She was a daughter. She was a big sister. She was a sixth grader. She was a girl who took care of the little ones, who acted, in her sister's words, like a second mom. She was twelve years old, and she was precious, and she was loved, and for thirty-two years she waited, in the only way the dead can wait, for someone to finally say the name of the man who took her. In June 2018, someone did. If there is one thing to carry away from this case, it is this. Time does not erase the truth. It only hides it, sometimes for decades, inside a small sample in an evidence room, inside a family tree, inside a napkin thrown away after a meal. Someone has to keep looking.
