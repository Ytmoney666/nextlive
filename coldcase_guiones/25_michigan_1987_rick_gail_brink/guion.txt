Family gatherings. The shared times that, as he put it, had been wiped away, by that man, by his anger and by his jealousy. That is what twenty-five years of a cold case actually looks like from the inside. It is not a mystery that people think about every hour. It is an absence that shows up at the table, every year, in the same chair. It is worth putting the passing of time into numbers, because the numbers are what make this case so hard to accept. From the night of the killings in November 1987 to the arrest in January 2013, there were twenty-six Christmases. There were twenty-six Thanksgivings, counting the one that came just three days after the bodies were found. Rick would have been in his early fifties in the year of the arrest. Gail would have been in her late forties. If they had had children, as so many young couples in that community did, those children could have been grown, with homes and families of their own. None of that happened. And for every one of those years, according to the verdict that a jury would one day reach, the man responsible lived free in the same corner of Michigan, a short drive from where his sister had died. Ryan Wyngarden built his adult life in the Holland and Zeeland area. He married Pam, the young woman who had given him his alibi. By the time he was arrested, news reports described him simply as a Zeeland man. He was not a fugitive. He did not change his name or flee the state. He did not need to. As far as the investigation was concerned, he had been cleared in 1987, and nothing in the file had pointed back at him since. Now, consider the ledger of this case, the list of what was missed and what was waiting. In November 1987, detectives interviewed the victim's brother within days. That was one chance. His alibi rested on a single witness, his girlfriend. That was the weakness. The alibi was accepted, and he was not considered a suspect. That was the decision. The murder weapon, a .22 calibre pistol, was never presented in court. That was the missing link. No one was arrested in the first year, or the fifth, or the tenth, or the twentieth. And all the while, the most important evidence in the case, the typed transcripts of what people had said in those first days, sat in the case file, readable by anyone who took the time to read it closely. Nobody, for more than two decades, sat down and read all of it side by side with fresh eyes. That is not an accusation against any one detective. It is a description of how the system worked in most places at the time. A sheriff's office in a mid-sized Michigan county does not have unlimited people. When a case has no new leads, there is no one whose job it is to go back to page one and start again. Cases stay open, technically. But open is not the same as active. Across America, that began to change in the late 1990s and the 2000s. As DNA technology improved and national databases grew, police departments started to realise how many solvable cases were sitting in their storage rooms. Television helped too. Shows about cold cases made the public aware that old crimes could still be solved, and that pressure, along with the science, pushed agencies to create dedicated cold case units: small teams of detectives whose only job was to go back to the cases everyone else had stopped working. In 2009, the Ottawa County Sheriff's Office created its own cold case team. By then, the Brink case was twenty-two years old. The original investigators had moved on. The families had grown older. Both families had spent more than two decades without an answer. And the man at the centre of it all had spent more than two decades with his secret apparently safe. The new team had many cases to choose from, and it took time for them to reach the Brinks. But in 2011, the Brink double homicide was handed to two detectives. Their names were Venus Repper and David Blakely. By then the case was twenty-four years old. Think about what that meant. Many of the people in the original file had moved away. Some had married and changed their names. Some may no longer have been alive. Others had simply forgotten. Memories that were sharp in 1987 had blurred over almost a quarter of a century. There was no new technology that could magically produce a name. There was no DNA hit waiting in a database. What the detectives had was paper. And they had a method, the oldest one in police work, applied with a patience that the original investigation never had the luxury of. Read everything. Assume nothing. Talk to everyone again. Compare what people say now with what they said then. And pay attention to the places where the two do not quite match. It is slow, unglamorous work, and it almost never makes headlines until the very end. Thousands of pages of it. Reports, notes, photographs and those typed transcripts of interviews from 1987. And they had something the original investigators could not have had: distance. Twenty-four years is a long time. Friendships end. Marriages change. People who once felt bound to protect someone may no longer feel that way, or may find that the weight of what they know has grown heavier with every year. Loyalties that seemed permanent in 1987 can look very different in 2011. A cold case detective's greatest ally is not always a laboratory. Sometimes it is time itself. And there is something else time does. It makes the person who has kept a secret begin to believe it will never come out. After twenty-four years of holidays and anniversaries, after being interviewed once and cleared, after watching the case go quiet, a man in that position might well have believed that the danger had passed. He had no way of knowing that in an office in Ottawa County, two detectives had just opened a box of old files, and had decided to read every single page.

Most of the cold cases you hear about today are solved by science. A drop of blood is tested with new technology. A DNA profile is uploaded to a genealogy database. A family tree is built, branch by branch, until a single name appears. That is not this story. The case of Rick and Gail Brink was not solved by a laboratory. It was solved by something much older, and in its own way, much harder. It was solved by reading. Some retellings of this case talk about evidence being re-examined, and the detectives certainly went back through everything the original investigation had gathered. But when the case finally came to trial, the coverage described a prosecution built largely on testimony, on what people said, and on what one person in particular finally said after twenty-five years. So to understand how this case broke open, you have to understand the method behind it, a method that is as much about human nature as it is about police work. Here is how it works, in plain words. When a cold case detective opens an old file, the first job is not to look for new evidence. It is to understand the old evidence completely, better than anyone did the first time. That means reading every report, every note, every interview, in order, from the first call to the last entry. In a major homicide, that can mean thousands of pages. Detectives Venus Repper and David Blakely did exactly that. They combed through the old documents of the Brink case, thousands of them, looking at the investigation the way a historian looks at an archive. Why would that matter, when the original detectives had already read those same pages. Because the original detectives read them one at a time, as they came in, in the rush of a live investigation. A cold case detective reads them all together, at the same desk, with time to compare. Think of an old interview transcript as a time capsule. When someone sits down with a detective three days after a murder and tells them where they were, the words are frozen at that moment. They capture the story exactly as it was told then, with every detail, every time, every name. Years later, memory changes. People forget, embellish, simplify, or rethink. But the transcript does not change. And that makes it an extraordinary tool. If you can put a person's 1987 account next to that same person's account from 2012, every difference stands out like a crack in a wall. Some differences are innocent. People forget small details. They confuse one weekend with another. They remember a Tuesday as a Wednesday. But some differences are not innocent at all. They suggest that one of the two stories was never true. The second part of the method is interviews. Not just reading what people said, but going back to them, decades later, and asking again. Repper and Blakely did this on a scale that is hard to imagine. They conducted more than two hundred interviews. Some of the people they needed to find had left Michigan, and the detectives crossed state lines to reach them. Every one of those conversations was a chance for someone to remember something new, or to say something they had never felt able to say before. That is the hidden power of time in a cold case. In 1987, the people closest to a crime are often the people with the most reasons to stay quiet. They may be afraid. They may be protecting someone. They may be in love. They may simply not want to believe what they suspect. But people change over twenty-five years. Relationships end or grow strained. Children grow up. Fear fades. Guilt grows. A person who would never have said a word when they were young might, decades later, decide that they cannot carry it any more. A good cold case detective knows this, and goes back, again and again, to the people who were there at the beginning. The third part of the method is the hardest to describe, because it is really a matter of judgment. It is noticing. Among all those thousands of pages and hundreds of conversations, the detective has to recognise the one detail that does not fit. Not a dramatic clue, not a smoking gun, but a small inconsistency. A time that does not match. A place that changes. A name that appears in one version and not in another. This is where cold cases are won or lost. Hundreds of facts may line up perfectly. It is the one that does not line up that matters. In the Brink file, that is exactly what happened. As Repper and Blakely worked through the old interview transcripts, they noticed a discrepancy. The public reporting does not spell out precisely which line or which detail caught their attention, and this narration will not invent it. But we know where it led them. It led them back to the alibi. The reporting on the timing is not perfectly consistent. One account says the decisive conversation took place in 2012. The detectives' own account, as reported after the trial, places the key re-interview in January 2013, just before the arrest. Either way, the direction was the same. Back to the story of laundry and babysitting at a friend's house on that weekend in November 1987. And back to the one person who had confirmed it. By now, she was no longer Pam Maracchini, the new girlfriend. She was Pam Wyngarden, Ryan's wife. There is an important point to understand here about the strength and the weakness of this kind of evidence, because it would become the central battle of the trial. A confession to a spouse, or an account from a spouse, is not like a fingerprint or a DNA profile. It is not a fixed object that can be tested in a laboratory. It is a human memory, carried for decades, and told for the first time under questioning by detectives. That makes it powerful, because it can contain details that no stranger could know. But it also makes it vulnerable. Defence lawyers know that memory can be shaped by questions. They know that a witness who has told one story for twenty-five years and then tells another must explain why. And they know that a jury has to decide not just whether a witness is honest, but whether her memory is accurate. Scientists who study memory have spent decades on that question. How accurate are memories that are decades old. Under what conditions can they be trusted. How much can the way someone is questioned change what they remember, or what they say. Those questions would come up again and again in the Brink case, in the courtroom and in the appeals that followed. They are fair questions. They are exactly the questions that any justice system should ask when a conviction rests on what one person says. But as the detectives prepared to sit down with Pam Wyngarden, those arguments were still in the future. What mattered was that two detectives had found a crack in a twenty-five-year-old story. All they had to do now was find out whether the woman who had told it was ready to let it break.

Venus Repper and David Blakely were not famous detectives. They were not the subject of books or television series, at least not yet. They were two members of a small cold case team in a county sheriff's office in West Michigan, doing the slow, quiet work that the team had been created to do. By the time they were ready to sit down with Pam Wyngarden again, they had read thousands of pages, conducted more than two hundred interviews, and travelled beyond Michigan to find people who had scattered over the decades. They had done the work. Now they needed one person to tell the truth. The detectives re-interviewed Pam. And this time, the story of laundry and babysitting fell apart. According to the reporting on the case, Pam told the detectives that her husband had told her that he killed Rick and Gail Brink. Not a rumour. Not a guess. An admission, made to her, by the man she had been dating in 1987 and had married since. For twenty-five years, she had stuck to the story that they had spent that weekend at a friend's home doing laundry and babysitting. Now she said that story had been a cover. And then she told them something even more chilling. At the trial in 2014, she would describe it under oath to a jury, and the account she gave there is the one the public knows. After Ryan admitted the killings to her, she testified, he asked her to go back to his house with him. But on the way, they stopped at Rick and Gail's house first. He grabbed her by the wrists, she said, and walked her into the house, to the bedroom. And there, she saw Gail. This, according to her testimony, happened before the bodies were discovered. Before Rick's family drove out to the ranch house on Monday morning. Before the deputies came. Before anyone in either family knew that anything was wrong. Pause on that for a moment. If Pam's account was true, then the young woman who told detectives in 1987 that she and Ryan had been folding laundry had, in fact, been inside the crime scene. She had seen Gail. And she had then given the man who brought her there an alibi that would hold for a quarter of a century. Why would anyone do that? The defence would later argue that she did not do it at all, that her later account was unreliable, shaped by detectives and by the passage of time. The prosecution would ask the jury to accept that, after twenty-five years, she was finally telling the truth, and that the first story, the laundry and the babysitting, had been the lie. A jury would eventually decide which version to believe. But at that moment, in an interview room in Ottawa County, two detectives were hearing for the first time what might really have happened on that November weekend. And there was more. Because Pam did not just tell detectives what Ryan had done. She told them why. And the why was the family secret. According to her testimony, Ryan told her he killed the couple, in part, because he did not want his sister to reveal that the two of them had had a sexual relationship when they were teenagers. Police and prosecutors would describe it the same way: secret sexual encounters between a brother and sister when they were young, which Ryan wanted to keep hidden. It must be said plainly and only once, and this narration will say nothing more about it. Gail was the one who carried that history into her marriage. She was the one who, in the prosecution's theory, might one day tell her husband, and in doing so expose her brother. Rick, the husband she trusted, was the one person in the world she might tell. And so, in that theory, both of them had to be silenced. Police also pointed to another motive that ran alongside the secret. They said Ryan had been jealous of the newlyweds' success. Jealous of their marriage, their jobs, their new house. Jealous, in other words, of exactly the ordinary happy life that Rick and Gail had worked so hard to begin. There was one more detail that investigators would point to. According to the reporting, Ryan had made reference to owning a .22 calibre pistol, the same kind of gun that had been used to shoot Rick and Gail. The weapon itself was never put before the jury. But the reference fit the picture that was now emerging. Look at how the pieces of this story had been hidden in plain sight. The killer had been interviewed in 1987. His name was in the file from the very first days. His alibi witness was in the file too. Her account was typed up and preserved. The motive, the secret, had been inside the family all along. Nothing had been lost. It had simply never been put together, because the one person who could connect it all had stayed silent. Now she had spoken. Two detectives who had started with a box of old files in 2011 had, in less than two years, accomplished what more than two decades had not. They had not found a new clue in a laboratory. They had found an old truth in a person, and they had been patient enough, and careful enough, to let it come out. In January 2013, more than twenty-five years after Rick and Gail were found, Ryan Wyngarden was arrested. He was charged with two counts of first-degree premeditated murder, one for his sister, Gail Brink, and one for her husband, Rick Brink. News reports described him as a Zeeland man, a resident of the small Dutch city just east of Holland, a few miles from the township where the Brinks had died. The investigators said publicly very little at first about what had led them to him. There was no press conference describing a laboratory breakthrough, because there had been none. There was no DNA match to announce, no genealogy tree to display on a screen. The case that had broken was a case of words, and the details of those words would come out, piece by piece, in a courtroom. But a court document soon made the core of it clear. His wife had told police that he had admitted the killings. For the families, the news landed like a second blow. For twenty-five years, they had imagined a stranger, or an unknown enemy, or simply a question with no answer. Now they were told that the person charged was Gail's own brother. Someone who had been part of the family through all of those years. Someone who had remained part of the family's life through all the holidays that Rick's brother would later say had been wiped away. Think about the then and now of it. In November 1987, Gail was twenty-two and Rick was twenty-eight. Their whole adult life was in front of them. In January 2013, the man charged with taking that life from them had lived more than twenty-five years of his own since that weekend. He had married. He had built a life. He had lived, the prosecution would argue, with full knowledge of what he had done. Whether he would ever answer for it was now up to a courtroom in Ottawa County.

The legal case against Ryan Wyngarden began in a small courtroom in Holland. In February 2013, about a month after his arrest, he appeared in Holland District Court for a preliminary examination. In Michigan, that is the hearing where a judge decides whether there is enough evidence to send a felony case to trial. It is not a trial, and the standard is much lower, but it is often the first time the public hears what the prosecution has. And in this case, what the public heard was stunning. Pam Wyngarden took the stand and testified against her own husband. She said he had admitted the killings to her. And the reason she gave, the family secret, now became public in the plainest and most painful way. Local television ran the story under a headline saying that the man accused of murdering his sister was also accused of having had a sexual relationship with her. For the Brink and Wyngarden families, a private horror that had been hidden for decades was suddenly on the evening news. Ryan's defence attorney at that hearing, David Hall, pushed back. He argued that some of the testimony against his client amounted to hearsay. In plain words, hearsay is when a witness repeats in court something that someone else supposedly said outside of court. Courts are careful with it, because the person who originally said it cannot be cross-examined about it in the same way. But the judge found there was enough evidence to proceed, and Ryan Wyngarden was ordered to stand trial in Ottawa County Circuit Court, on two counts of first-degree premeditated murder. Those words matter. First-degree premeditated murder does not mean a killing in a moment of panic or rage. It means that the killer thought about it beforehand, even briefly, and chose to do it. To convict, the prosecution would have to persuade twelve jurors not only that Ryan Wyngarden had fired the shots, but that he had planned to. The trial began in March 2014, more than twenty-six years after the killings. Jurors heard about the victims. Witnesses took the stand to describe Rick and Gail's last day alive, bringing that November weekend back into a courtroom after more than a quarter of a century. For Gail's side of the family, the trial meant something almost impossible to bear. The person accused of taking her life was one of their own, and the secret at the centre of the case was their own family's history, now spoken aloud before strangers. Then came the case against the brother. The centre of it was Pam. For the second time, now before a jury, she testified that her husband had told her he shot Rick and Gail. She described the night he took her to their house, how he grabbed her wrists and walked her into the bedroom, where she saw Gail. She described the reason he gave her, that he did not want his sister to reveal what had happened between them as teenagers. Local reporters described the testimony as intense. The prosecution did not rely on Pam alone. Another witness took the stand, a man named Daryl Cain, who had been in jail with Ryan after his arrest. Cain testified that Ryan had admitted to him, in jail, that he murdered his sister and her husband. Jailhouse informants are always treated with caution, because they may hope for some benefit in exchange for their testimony, and juries are told to weigh their words carefully. But for the prosecution, Cain's account was a second, independent voice saying the same thing Pam had said. The defence attacked Pam's credibility head-on. The Holland newspaper's coverage of one day of the trial carried a headline about the defence highlighting discrepancies in Pam Wyngarden's accounts to police. That was the heart of the defence. Here was a woman who had told one story in 1987 and another in 2013. Which one was true? And if she had lied once, to detectives, why should a jury believe her now? The defence also wanted the jury to hear from an expert, Dr. Deborah Davis, a scholar who studies memory. Her work deals with how to evaluate someone else's memory, and the conditions under which a memory is most likely to be accurate. One of her presentations, described in the later federal court opinion in this case, carries a title that captures the whole issue: Memory on Trial, Clues to Witness Accuracy. The defence wanted her to explain to jurors how certain police interrogation techniques, as used on Pam, could affect what a witness says. The trial judge did not allow her to testify about that, and the defence would later complain that it had also not been allowed to put videotapes of Pam's interviews into evidence. Those decisions would become central to Ryan's appeals for years afterward. Then Ryan Wyngarden did something many defendants never do. He took the stand in his own defence. He denied killing his sister and her husband. A local newspaper headline that day summed up the impression he left in the courtroom, saying that he had trouble directly answering questions. On March 27, 2014, the lawyers delivered their closing arguments. The prosecution asked the jury to believe the account that had finally come out after twenty-five years. The defence told them that the statements could not be trusted. The next day, Friday, March 28, 2014, the jury came back. They had deliberated for only about four hours. Their verdict was guilty, on both counts of first-degree premeditated murder. In Michigan, first-degree murder carries a mandatory sentence of life in prison without the possibility of parole. Michigan, it is worth remembering, has no death penalty. It abolished capital punishment for murder in 1846, one of the first governments in the world to do so. So the only question left was not what the sentence would be, but what would be said when it was handed down. The sentencing hearing took place on Monday, April 21, 2014, before Ottawa County Circuit Judge Jon Hulsing. Rick's brother Budd asked the judge to impose the life sentence. He spoke about the holidays and family times that had been wiped away by Ryan, his anger and his jealousy. And he recalled the funeral more than twenty-six years earlier, and the priest's words that day. This is not the work of the Lord, the priest had said. This is the work of the Devil. Then Ryan spoke. And he spoke for more than an hour. He maintained his innocence. He criticised the investigators. And at one point he turned on the judge himself. You're lying now, he said. You are flat out lying. The exchange grew so heated that Judge Hulsing warned him to stay quiet, threatening, according to news reports, to have his mouth taped shut. One local station's headline captured the judge's words as a question: did he need to bind and gag him. When it was over, the judge imposed the sentence the law required. Two terms of life in prison without parole, one for Gail Brink and one for Rick Brink. More than twenty-six years after a young couple was found in their new home in Park Township, a court had finally named the person responsible. But the man convicted of their murders was not finished fighting.

A verdict is not always the end of a case. In America, a person convicted of murder has the right to appeal, and Ryan Wyngarden used that right again and again. His first appeal went to the Michigan Court of Appeals. The heart of his argument was the same one his lawyers had made at trial: that his wife was not a reliable witness, and that she had been manipulated by police. In August 2015, the Court of Appeals rejected that argument and upheld his convictions. The court found no errors in his trial that would justify reversing the verdicts. He did not stop. Michigan allows a person whose appeals have ended to file what is called a motion for relief from judgment, a request to reopen a conviction on grounds that were not, or could not be, fully raised before. Ryan filed one. It was denied by the Ottawa County Circuit Court. In February 2018, the Michigan Court of Appeals refused to take it up. And in September 2018, the Michigan Supreme Court rejected it as well. As the Associated Press put it at the time, three courts had now rejected his effort to reopen the case. Then he turned to the federal courts. Writing his own petition, without a lawyer, from the Thumb Correctional Facility in Lapeer, Michigan, he asked a United States District Court to grant him a writ of habeas corpus. In plain words, that is a request for a federal judge to review whether a state conviction violated the Constitution. Among his claims was the decision at trial to keep the memory expert, Dr. Deborah Davis, from telling the jury how interrogation techniques could have affected his wife's account. The case was heard in the Eastern District of Michigan by Judge Arthur Tarnow. Federal judges in these cases do not simply retry the case. Under a 1996 federal law, they must give strong deference to the decisions of the state courts, and they can grant relief only when a state court's ruling was clearly unreasonable. The judge denied the petition. At the same time, he granted what is known as a certificate of appealability, which meant Ryan was allowed to ask a higher federal court to review the question. As of this recording, there is no public report that any court has overturned the verdict, ordered a new trial, or released him. Ryan Wyngarden remains convicted of the murders of Rick and Gail Brink, serving two life sentences without parole. He has always maintained his innocence. The case did not fade from memory after the verdict. A popular true crime podcast told the story of Gail and Rick in a 2021 episode. A cable television series about marriages that ended in murder told it under the title A Secret to Kill For. Each time, a new audience heard the names Rick and Gail Brink, and heard how two detectives with a box of paper had done what decades had not. And there are still questions that the public record does not fully answer. The gun, the .22 calibre pistol, was never put before the jury. The exact line in the old transcripts that first caught the detectives' eye has never been laid out in public. Why Pam stayed silent for so long, and what finally moved her to speak, has been argued over by lawyers but never fully explained in her own public words. Those gaps are real, and an honest telling of this story has to leave them as gaps. Whatever anyone believes about Pam Wyngarden, about why she stayed silent and why she finally spoke, one fact stands. In every account of the trial, her testimony was the centre of the prosecution's case. Her words, given twenty-five years late, are the reason a jury ever heard the names Rick and Gail Brink. What is not in doubt is what was lost. Let's go back, one last time, to where this story began. Monday morning, November 23, 1987. A ranch house in Park Township, on the quiet lakeshore outside Holland, Michigan. A Chevy Blazer in the driveway. Two young people who did not show up for work. Rick Brink was twenty-eight years old. He was a son and a brother, and his brother would carry his memory into a courtroom more than twenty-six years later, still remembering the words spoken at his funeral. He was a husband of eighteen months. He drove a truck that suited the Michigan winters, and he had just bought a house with the woman he married. He was missed within hours, because the people around him counted on him. Gail Brink was twenty-two years old. She was a daughter and a sister. She was a young wife who had just begun to make a home. She carried a secret that was never hers to carry, and according to the prosecution whose case the jury accepted, it was that secret, and the jealousy of someone who should have protected her, that cost her her life. Together, they were a young couple who had done everything right. They worked. They married. They bought a home. And on a cold November weekend, three days before Thanksgiving, someone they had every reason to trust took all of it away. For twenty-five years, the answer sat in two places. It sat in the memory of a woman who had given an alibi. And it sat on paper, in typed transcripts in a sheriff's office, waiting for someone to read them side by side. In the end, it took two detectives, Venus Repper and David Blakely, thousands of pages, more than two hundred interviews, and the willingness to go back to the very first people in the file and ask again. There was no laboratory miracle in this case. There was patience, and there was persistence, and there was one witness who finally spoke. If there is a lesson in this story, it is a hard one. The people who hurt us are, far more often than strangers, the people we know. An alibi from someone who loves you is not proof. A secret kept inside a family does not stay buried simply because everyone stops speaking of it. And a cold case is not a closed case. It is a case waiting for the right person to open the file again. Rick and Gail Brink should have grown old in that ranch house. They should have spent hundreds of Thanksgivings around a family table. Instead, their families spent twenty-six years with two empty chairs, and a question that would not go away. That question has an answer now. It came late, far too late, and it came from the last place anyone wanted it to come from. But it came. And because two detectives refused to treat an old file as finished, a young couple from the Michigan lakeshore finally received what every victim deserves: the truth, spoken out loud, in a courtroom, with their names on it.
