Had she lived, she would have been about forty by 1998, and about fifty by 2008. By 2015, she would have been in her late fifties. Her niece carried her absence through all those years, and would one day stand in a courtroom and say her aunt's name. Her mother grew older while the boxes sat on a shelf. And the man whose bare footprints were in those photographs? In June 1993, he was in his twenties. Over the next twenty-five years he would become, by every outward measure, a success. He would live in Isanti, a small community north of the Twin Cities. He would become a businessman. He would become the father of three children. He would spend winters in hockey arenas, the kind of parent the news would later call a hockey dad. Hold those two timelines side by side. On one, a mother in hospice rooms, a sister defending a memory, a niece growing up with an empty chair. On the other, a family man in a small town, building a business, cheering at rinks. Two timelines running in parallel for a quarter of a century. Only one of them carried the weight of what happened in the Horn Towers. During those same years, the science was changing around the evidence. DNA testing became faster, more sensitive, and more routine. Laboratories learned to work with smaller and older samples. Across the country, states and the federal government built databases of DNA profiles from convicted offenders and from crime scenes, so that an unknown profile could be compared against many known ones. For the first time, a crime scene sample could sometimes produce a name without a suspect. But a database is only as good as the people in it. If the person who left the DNA has never been required to give a sample, the database stays silent. And nothing in the public record of this case suggests that any of those databases ever produced the name of Jeanie Childs' killer. He was not in them. He was a businessman and a hockey dad. Year after year, the search came back empty, or was never run at all, and from the outside it is impossible to know which. That is the ledger of this case, and it is brutal in its simplicity. Twenty-two years between the murder and the first serious review of the evidence. Twenty-five years between the murder and an arrest. Twenty-nine years between the murder and a verdict. Seven bloody bare footprints under one window, four of them clear enough to compare, and for more than two decades, no one to compare them with. One knife in a kitchen sink, photographed and never tested. One murder weapon, never found. One case, by the account of a Minneapolis television station, that got little, if any, press coverage at the time. And through all of it, the photographs of those footprints sat in the file, unchanged. That is the strange patience of physical evidence. A witness's memory fades a little every year. A photograph of a bloody bare footprint, dusted with black powder in June 1993, says exactly the same thing in 2015 that it said on the day it was taken. It does not get tired. It does not move away. It does not give up. It simply waits for someone to bring it the right foot. In this case, it would wait a very long time, and the foot it was waiting for was walking around a small Minnesota town the entire while. Then, in 2015, something shifted. The Minneapolis Police Department began reviewing its unsolved cases, working alongside a cold case task force that included the FBI and the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension. Jeanie Childs' file was among those pulled from the shelf. A Minneapolis homicide detective and an FBI special agent named Chris Boeckers decided to take another look, and the reason they gave was simple. DNA testing had advanced. Evidence that had been mute in 1993 might now have something to say. One of the first things that review did was revisit the old leads. The stairwell blood that had once seemed so promising was finally tied to a man. In 2015, he was interviewed. His footprints were compared with the bloody prints in Jeanie's bedroom. And that lead, like the ones before it, went nowhere. He was never charged. Imagine being on that review team. You open a file that is twenty-two years old. The detectives who first worked it have moved on. The victim's mother is two decades older. You chase the best lead in the box, and it collapses. Many cold case reviews end right there. A memo, a signature, back to the shelf. This one did not. Because the biological evidence from that apartment still held a male DNA profile. Years later, a state scientist would testify that it was not Arthur Gray's. And as far as the public record shows, it matched no one in any database. A complete stranger to the system. And in 2015, a stranger to the system was still a dead end. A DNA profile with no match was a locked door with no key. Unless, somewhere, someone found a different kind of key. Three years later, in April 2018, investigators in California announced an arrest in one of the most notorious cold cases in American history, the Golden State Killer, using a technique that most of the country had never heard of. Detectives in Minneapolis were paying attention. And one of them would later say plainly that their approach to Jeanie's case was inspired by exactly that.

To understand how a twenty-five-year-old murder was solved, you have to understand the difference between two ways of reading DNA. For most of the history of forensic science in America, crime laboratories have read DNA the way you might identify a house by checking a handful of specific details. Is the door red? Is there a chimney on the left? Are there two windows upstairs? Labs looked at a small set of particular places in the genetic code, places where people tend to differ, and recorded what they found there. That kind of profile is extremely good at one job. If you have a sample from a crime scene and a sample from a suspect, it can tell you, with enormous confidence, whether they came from the same person. It can also be stored in a database and compared with other profiles of the same kind. But it was never designed to find relatives. A handful of details about a house does not tell you who lives on the next street, or who built the house next door. Now imagine a completely different approach. Instead of checking a handful of details, you photograph the entire house, every brick, every shingle, every board, hundreds of thousands of tiny data points. With that much information, you can do something remarkable. You can compare your photograph with photographs of other houses and see which ones were built from the same plans, by the same family of builders. Some will share a great deal. Some will share only a little. And the amount they share tells you how closely they are related. That second approach is called a SNP profile. The letters stand for single nucleotide polymorphism, which is simply the scientific name for a single-letter difference in the genetic code. A SNP test reads hundreds of thousands of those letters. It is the same basic kind of test that millions of ordinary people have taken through consumer genealogy companies, spitting into a tube to learn about their ancestry or to find long-lost cousins. And it is the key to everything that happened next. Here is how the method works, step by step, in plain words. First, investigators take a sample of the unknown person's DNA from the crime scene, in this case evidence preserved since 1993. Second, a specialized laboratory converts that sample into a SNP profile, the full photograph of the house. Third, that profile is compared with profiles in genealogy databases where members of the public have uploaded their own DNA results. The killer is almost never in those databases himself. But his relatives might be. A second cousin. A third cousin. Sometimes much closer. Fourth, a genealogist takes the list of matches and starts building family trees, using the oldest tools in the world: birth records, marriage records, obituaries, census pages, and public information. Every relative points to a set of shared ancestors. Follow those ancestors forward, generation by generation, and the trees begin to overlap. Eventually, if the work is good and the luck holds, the branches narrow to a single family, and then to a handful of people of the right sex and the right age, who lived in the right place at the right time. Fifth, and this is critical, the genealogy does not convict anyone. It produces a lead. Investigators still have to obtain a direct sample from the person they suspect, and compare it with the crime scene evidence using traditional forensic DNA testing. Only that comparison can confirm or rule out a match. This step is not a formality. A family tree can tell investigators which family to look at. It cannot tell them which brother, which cousin, which son. Genealogy narrows the field. Only a direct comparison closes it. That is why the last chapter of a genetic genealogy investigation so often looks strangely ordinary: detectives waiting, patiently and legally, for a person to leave a sample behind. The world learned what this method could do in April 2018, when investigators in California announced an arrest in the Golden State Killer case. It was a case that had haunted the state for decades, and it was broken not by a confession or a tip but by a family tree. Overnight, investigators across the United States began asking the same question. What about our cases? What about the profiles sitting in our evidence rooms that have never matched anyone? In Minneapolis, the team reviewing Jeanie Childs' murder asked exactly that question. FBI special agent Chris Boeckers would later say that the approach they took was inspired by the Golden State Killer case. Hennepin County Attorney Mike Freeman would later make the same comparison in public, describing the method as similar to the one used in California. The case of a woman whose death had barely made the local news was about to be worked with the most advanced identification technique in American forensic science. In 2018, according to the criminal complaint later filed in the case, police working with the FBI sent a DNA sample from the crime scene to a laboratory called DNA Solutions, Incorporated. The goal was to create a SNP profile, that full photograph of the house, which could then be compared with profiles on commercial genealogy databases to find relatives of the unknown man. The comparison worked. Somewhere in those databases, there were people who shared DNA with the man who had left his traces in Jeanie's apartment. Then the genealogical research began. We do not know, from the public record, the name of every person who worked on those family trees, or exactly how many hours they spent. We know the result. According to the complaint, the genealogy search, combined with additional genealogical research, produced two possible suspects. Two men. And according to Hennepin County Attorney Mike Freeman, as reported at the time, either one of those men or a close family member had apparently submitted DNA to learn more about their family tree. Think about what that means. Somewhere, at some point, someone in that family had taken a genealogy test, curious perhaps about heritage, or cousins, or a family story. It was the most ordinary act in the world. And that ordinary act had placed a piece of their family's genetic code into a database where, years later, it would meet the DNA left behind in Jeanie's apartment in a south Minneapolis high-rise. There was one more science in this case, and it is older and simpler. Remember the bare footprints. The skin on the soles of the human foot carries friction ridges, the same kind of raised lines that form our fingerprints, with their own loops and branches and endings. Examiners can compare a clear print of that skin with a known print, detail by detail, the way fingerprints have been compared for more than a century. In this case, the Minneapolis police lab would determine that four of the seven bloody prints from 1993 were suitable for comparison, and the department's supervisor of forensic science, Mark Ulrick, would later be the one to compare them. In other words, investigators now had two independent ways to test any name the genealogy produced. They had DNA. And they had feet. The family tree was narrowing. Two men. Two brothers. Both in Minnesota. One of them had been in his twenties in 1993. And now, for the first time in a quarter of a century, the investigators reviewing Jeanie Childs' murder had something they had never had before. They had a name to follow.

Every cold case that is finally solved has a hero, and in this one the hero is not a single person. It is a relay. It is a security guard who stayed in a doorway in 1993 so that nothing would be disturbed. It is the technicians who dusted bare footprints with black powder and photographed them, knowing they had no one to compare them with. It is whoever kept those evidence bags intact for more than two decades. It is a Minneapolis homicide detective and an FBI special agent named Chris Boeckers, who in 2015 pulled a twenty-two-year-old file off a shelf because they believed science had caught up with it. It is a genealogist who traced strangers' family trees through records most people never look at. It is the scientists at the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension, who would test what came next. None of them knew Jeanie Childs. All of them refused to let her be forgotten. The genealogy pointed to two brothers in Minnesota. Boeckers would later describe it in exactly those terms: the search indicated a potential match to two brothers. One of them was a man named Jerry Arnold Westrom. By 2019, Jerry Westrom was fifty-two years old. He lived in Isanti, a small community north of the Twin Cities. He was known as a businessman. He was a husband and the father of three children. He was, in the phrase that reporters would soon attach to his name in headlines across the country, a hockey dad. People who knew him described a man well respected in his small rural community. Some who knew him personally would be shocked when they heard what happened next. On paper, he was the opposite of what most people imagine when they picture a killer. That, in the end, is one of the most unsettling parts of this story. In June 1993, he had been in his twenties. Now he was in his fifties. A quarter of a century had turned a young man into a respected middle-aged father, and nothing about that transformation had ever brought him to the attention of police in connection with Jeanie Childs. Until now. But a family tree is not proof. Two brothers matched the genealogy. Investigators needed to know which one, if either, had actually left his DNA in that apartment. And they could not simply knock on the door and ask for a cheek swab, not without tipping off the person they suspected. They needed what investigators call an abandoned sample: something a person touches, uses, and throws away in public, where the law treats it as discarded. So in January 2019, officers began watching Jerry Westrom, with the goal of collecting a sample of his DNA without his knowledge. They followed him to a hockey game. Reports later placed that game across the state line in Wisconsin. And there, according to the criminal complaint, the most ordinary moment of an ordinary winter day at a rink became the turning point of a twenty-five-year-old murder case. Westrom went to the concession area and ordered food. He ate a hot dog. He wiped his mouth with a paper napkin. Then he put the napkin inside a cardboard food container and threw both of them away. That was it. A napkin in a trash can at a hockey arena. The officers retrieved it. Think about everything that had to happen for that napkin to matter. A woman had to die in 1993. A guard had to protect the scene. Technicians had to collect a comforter and a towel and a washcloth. Someone had to keep them safe for twenty-five years. A cold case team had to reopen the file in 2015. A laboratory had to build a SNP profile in 2018. Someone in a family had to take a genealogy test. A genealogist had to build the trees. A team had to narrow them to two brothers. And then one of those brothers had to get hungry at a hockey game. The napkin went to the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension. The scientists there found what you might expect on a used napkin from a concession stand: a mixture of DNA, from more than one person. But one profile stood out. The major male profile, the dominant one, was consistent with the DNA recovered from the scene of Jeanie Childs' murder. Twenty-five years. Seven footprints. Two brothers. One napkin. And now, for the first time since June 13, 1993, the man whose DNA had been sitting in an evidence bag had a name. Hennepin County Attorney Mike Freeman would later defend how that name was obtained, in words that went around the country. When you discard things in the trash, the Supreme Court often says it's free game. That idea, that what you throw away in public is no longer private, would later be tested all the way up the court system in this very case. Westrom's lawyers would argue that analyzing the DNA on a discarded napkin was an unconstitutional search. Minnesota's Supreme Court would ultimately disagree, ruling that the genetic analysis of the napkin was not a search under either the United States or the Minnesota constitution, because the analysis could only be used to compare his DNA with the crime scene DNA, and he had no reasonable expectation of privacy in that identifying information. The case would even reach the Supreme Court of the United States. But all of that was still years away. In January 2019, what mattered was simpler. The napkin had spoken. And it was not the only witness waiting. The bare footprints were still in the file, the same ones that had been dusted with black powder in 1993. A match on DNA could, in theory, be explained away. A defense lawyer might argue that the DNA got there some other way, at some other time. A footprint in the victim's blood is much harder to explain away. If those prints matched Jerry Westrom's feet, it would mean he had been in that bedroom while her blood was on the floor. But to compare his feet, they would first need his feet. And to get those, they would need to arrest him. Before that day came, it is worth holding one more contrast in mind. Jeanie Childs is, and will always be, thirty-five years old. Every photograph of her that the public would see in the years ahead would show a woman frozen at that age. The man the napkin pointed to had been allowed to keep growing older, to watch his children grow, to sit in hockey arenas through more than two decades of Minnesota winters. Jeanie's mother had also grown older, still waiting for the answer that investigators were now close to holding. A family tree, two brothers and a napkin had done what twenty-five years of silence could not. Soon the rest of the country would hear about it too, because the story of how this case was cracked was about to become national news. On Monday, February 11, 2019, Minneapolis police arrested the businessman and hockey dad from Isanti in Waite Park, a city in central Minnesota. And the man who had walked barefoot out of Jeanie Childs' life twenty-five years earlier was about to be asked by detectives whether he had ever known her.

When detectives sat down with Jerry Westrom after his arrest, he was a fifty-two-year-old businessman facing questions about a night from his twenties. According to the criminal complaint, he denied any involvement in Jeanie Childs' murder. He said he did not know her. He said he had never been to her apartment. He said he had not had sex with any women in Minneapolis in 1993. And when he was told that his DNA had been found at the scene, he said he did not know why it would be there. Remember those words, because a jury would one day weigh them very carefully. Those denials were not the end of the science. They were the beginning of its final test. After the arrest, investigators were able to take a DNA sample directly from Westrom, no napkins, no trash cans, no mixtures. That known sample was compared with the evidence from the Horn Towers. It matched the semen found on the comforter from Jeanie's bed. It matched DNA on a towel from the bathroom. According to later reporting on the trial, it also matched DNA on a bloody towel, a washcloth, a T-shirt, and spots in the bathroom sink. And now they could also take something they had waited twenty-five years to collect: his footprints. On Thursday, February 14, 2019, the Hennepin County Attorney's Office charged Jerry Westrom with second-degree murder in the death of Jeanne Ann Childs. The news spread far beyond Minnesota. Hockey dad. Discarded napkin. Genealogy website. Twenty-five-year-old murder. Television stations and newspapers across the United States told the story of a man whose DNA had been pulled from a hot dog napkin at a hockey game. At last, the case that had barely made the news in 1993 was everywhere. He was held at first on one million dollars bail. At his first court appearance, his lawyer argued that not all of the evidence in the case had been examined, and Judge Martha Holton Dimick set his bail at five hundred thousand dollars, with conditions. Westrom was released on bail. And so, for most of the next three and a half years, the man charged with killing Jeanie Childs waited for his trial at liberty. Under American law, he was presumed innocent. The case against him grew heavier. In 2020, a grand jury indicted him on the most serious charge available, first-degree premeditated murder. Trial dates were set, and then set again. One date in March, another in April. The family waited. They had already waited twenty-six years, then twenty-seven, then twenty-eight. Finally, in mid-August 2022, in Hennepin County, Minnesota, the trial of Jerry Arnold Westrom began. The prosecution built its case on the DNA and the footprints, and, as the jury would later explain, Westrom's own words in that police interview would matter just as much. One by one, the people from that June in 1993 came back. The security guard took the stand and described the running shower, the window wide open, the missing screen, and how he never left the unit unattended. The retired sergeant, Robert Krebs, described walking into the bedroom and recognizing a woman he had once encountered on Lake Street, and finding the identification card in her fanny pack that told him who she was. Then came the footprints. The Minneapolis police lab had found that four of the seven bloody bare prints were suitable for comparison. Mark Ulrick, the department's supervisor of forensic science, had taken Westrom's footprints and compared them. He testified that the friction ridge detail in the prints matched Westrom's feet, and he said he was confident in that conclusion. One print in particular, from a left foot, showed the heel and the area below the big toe. And then came one of the most remarkable moments of the trial. The defense had hired its own footprint expert, an examiner named McCarthy, to check the work. According to reporting from the trial, when that expert determined that one of the prints, labeled E2, belonged to Westrom, the defense team released her. Both examiners, the one hired by the police and the one first hired by the defense, ended up testifying for the prosecution. The prosecutor summed up the meaning of those prints in two sentences that the jury would remember. You step in liquid blood and you put your foot down. His footprints were in her blood because he killed her. The defense, led by attorney Steve Meshbesher, fought back. They argued that the real killer was Arthur Gray, Jeanie's boyfriend. But a forensic scientist from the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension testified that Gray's DNA was excluded as a contributor to the unknown samples. The defense pointed to the knife photographed in the kitchen sink, which had never been tested for forensic evidence, and to the fact that no murder weapon was ever found. They challenged the footprint evidence and tried to poke holes in it. Meshbesher's message to the jury was blunt. They just have the wrong person. Jerry Westrom did not testify. That was his right, and it is never evidence of guilt. It meant only that the jury would hear his account of 1993 in one form alone: the denials he had given detectives after his arrest, set against the laboratory reports and the photographs of those footprints. On Thursday, August 25, 2022, after deliberating for about two hours, the jury returned its verdict. Two hours, after twenty-nine years. They found Jerry Westrom guilty of first-degree premeditated murder and of second-degree intentional murder. He was taken into custody in the courtroom. Hennepin County Attorney Mike Freeman issued a statement that day. Today's guilty verdicts show that we will pursue convictions for serious crimes, even if it takes years to gather the evidence. Westrom's attorney did not back down. As we said in the beginning, and we are saying now you got the wrong guy, Meshbesher said, and he made clear they planned to appeal. Later, the jury foreperson, a man named Derek Fradenburg, explained to a Minneapolis television station how the jury had seen it. It all boiled down, he said, to three things: Westrom's police interview, the DNA, and the bloody footprint. DNA alone, as he explained it, might be explained by some earlier contact. Two weeks later, on Friday, September 9, 2022, Westrom returned to court for sentencing before Hennepin County Judge Juan Hoyos. Before he pronounced the sentence, the judge looked at the man in front of him and acknowledged the life he had built. Then, according to reports from the courtroom, he told Westrom that he had taken Jeanie Childs' opportunity of a life. Westrom chose not to address the court. But earlier in that same hearing, before any sentence was pronounced, the people who had waited longest had finally had their turn to speak.

Betty Eakman had waited twenty-nine years to stand in that room. She had learned of her daughter's death from a television in a hospice patient's room. I've waited so many years to have this end, she said, and it's put my life through so much hell, and I just really feel I'll never forget and love her the way I always did. A woman who had lost her sister in the most violent way imaginable, who had spent decades defending her sister's memory against people who reduced her to a single word, looked across the courtroom and found room in her heart for the family of the man convicted of killing her. That is who Jeanie's family is. And perhaps that tells us something about who Jeanie was too. At that same hearing, Betty Eakman also found the words she had waited almost three decades to say. Honey, justice has been served, she said, and I'm happy about that. But a verdict is not always the end of a case, and this one was not. Westrom appealed. His lawyers took the case to the Minnesota Supreme Court, which heard arguments in 2023. They argued, among other things, that analyzing the DNA on the napkin he had thrown away was an unconstitutional search, and that the trial court had wrongly kept out evidence pointing to another possible suspect. On May 8, 2024, the court issued its decision. It held that the genetic analysis of the discarded napkin was not a search under either the United States or Minnesota constitutions. It held that the exclusion of the alternative-perpetrator evidence was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. It affirmed Jerry Westrom's conviction for first-degree premeditated murder. And it reversed the second-degree murder conviction, sending the case back with instructions to vacate it, because a defendant cannot stand convicted of both the greater offense and an included lesser one for the same killing. That technical correction changed nothing about his sentence. The court also noted that Westrom had not challenged, on appeal, the creation of the SNP profile from the crime scene DNA or the genealogy research that followed. Westrom then asked the Supreme Court of the United States to take his case. His petition, filed on September 6, 2024, raised two questions: whether people have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the DNA they shed in public, and whether testimony about laboratory work performed by other analysts had violated his right to confront the witnesses against him. The State of Minnesota waived its right to respond. On Monday, October 21, 2024, the Supreme Court of the United States denied the petition. The conviction stood. For anyone watching, that outcome carries a quiet message. Under the Minnesota decision, analyzing the DNA on a napkin someone throws away in public, in order to compare it with crime scene DNA, is not a search. And a genealogy test taken by a relative, out of simple curiosity, can help point investigators toward someone in the family. The same science that finally brought an answer to Jeanie's family also raises real questions about privacy, and those questions did not end with this case.
