Medgar Evers: How a Widow and Secret Files Solved a 31-Year Cold Case | Mississippi 1963

Just after midnight on June 12, 1963, Medgar Evers, 37, the NAACP's first field secretary in Mississippi, pulled into the driveway of his home in Jackson carrying T-shirts that read "Jim Crow Must Go." Inside, his wife Myrlie and their three children had waited up after watching President Kennedy's civil rights address. A single rifle shot rang out from the brush across the street. Medgar Evers died less than an hour later. Within hours, police found the rifle, with a fingerprint on its telescopic sight. Within days, a man was charged. And yet two trials in 1964 ended with all-white juries unable to agree, the charge was dropped, and the rifle and the trial transcripts vanished from official hands. For more than three decades, the case sat still. What finally broke it open was not a lab test. It was a widow who never stopped asking, a reporter who got his hands on secret state files, a prosecutor who went looking for lost evidence, an exhumation at Arlington National Cemetery, and new witnesses who had kept silent for years.

In this documentary: the life of Medgar Evers, the night of June 12, 1963, the two hung juries of 1964, the secret Sovereignty Commission files, and the 1994 trial that finally delivered a verdict.
