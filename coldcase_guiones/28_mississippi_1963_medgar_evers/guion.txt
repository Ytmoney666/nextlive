On the night of June 11, 1963, a thirty-seven-year-old man named Medgar Evers drove home through the dark streets of Jackson, Mississippi, after another long evening of meetings. Earlier that day he had called his wife, Myrlie, and asked her to do something for him. The President of the United States was going on national television that night to talk about civil rights, and Medgar wanted her to watch. So she did. Myrlie and their three children, aged nine, eight and three, waited up for him in the living room of their small house, with the television on. Just after midnight, now in the first minutes of June 12, they heard his car turn into the driveway. Medgar stepped out into the carport. In his arms he was carrying a bundle of T-shirts printed with four words. Jim Crow Must Go. Then the family heard a single shot. It came from a rifle fired out of a dark thicket of brush across the street. The bullet struck Medgar Evers in the back. His children did exactly what they had been taught to do, and dropped to the floor. His wife ran outside and found him collapsed and bleeding near the door of the home he had built his life around. Less than an hour later, at a hospital in Jackson, he was dead. Medgar Evers was the first field secretary of the NAACP in the state of Mississippi, and at that moment he was probably the most hated man in the eyes of those who wanted the old order to survive. What happened next is the strangest part of this story. Within hours, police found the rifle. It had been pushed into the brush not far from the house, and on its telescopic sight there was a fingerprint. Within about ten days, the FBI had traced that fingerprint to a man from the Mississippi Delta, and he was arrested and charged with murder. In most cold cases, the mystery is who. In this case, a name was on the charge sheet before the month was over. And still, for thirty-one years, no Mississippi jury would convict anyone. Two trials in 1964 ended with all-white juries unable to agree. The charge was later dropped. The rifle disappeared from the evidence room. The transcripts of the trials went missing from official hands. The man went home and lived his life. So how does a killer whose fingerprint was on the murder weapon stay free for three decades? And what finally changed? The answer was not DNA. It was not a new laboratory test. A Black citizen who tried to register could be handed a section of the state constitution and told to interpret it to the satisfaction of a white county clerk. He could lose his job. His name could be printed in the local paper. And because juries were drawn from the voter rolls, the same system that kept Black citizens from voting also kept them from serving on juries. In a courtroom in Mississippi in 1963, a jury was almost always white and almost always male. Now, the place. Jackson is the capital of Mississippi, and in 1963 it was the largest city in the state. It sits on the Pearl River in the center of Mississippi, and it was home to the Governor's Mansion, the state Capitol and the Hinds County Courthouse, the building where this story would eventually be decided, not once, but three times. Jackson was a segregated city. Lunch counters, buses, parks, libraries and schools were divided by race. But it was also home to a growing Black middle class, to churches that served as the meeting halls of the movement, and to Black colleges such as Jackson State and nearby Tougaloo College, whose students were ready to put their own bodies on the line. On the northwest side of the city, in a newer neighborhood of modest single-story homes built for Black families, stood a small ranch house with a carport on a street called Guynes Street. Today that street carries a different name, Margaret Walker Alexander Drive, and the house itself is a national monument. But in June 1963, it was simply the place where a family lived, where three children slept and played, and where a husband and father came home late every night, always a little more tired, always aware that someone might be waiting for him. He had every reason to believe it. Just two weeks earlier, someone had already tried to burn that house down.

But before we go to that night, we need to meet the man. Not the case, not the headline, the person. Medgar Wiley Evers was born on July 2, 1925, in Decatur, a small town in Newton County in east central Mississippi. His father, James Evers, worked as a farmer and in a sawmill. His mother was named Jesse. Medgar was one of several children in the family, and among them was an older brother, Charles, who would remain at his side through much of his life and would go on to become a well-known figure in Mississippi politics in his own right. Decatur was a rural place of farms, timber and small churches, and like every town in Mississippi at the time, it was ruled by the rules of segregation. Black children went to separate schools that received a fraction of the money given to white schools. Black adults stepped aside on sidewalks, entered through back doors and knew that a wrong word to the wrong person could cost them everything. Medgar grew up inside those rules, and he never accepted them. When he was seventeen, during the Second World War, he left high school and enlisted in the United States Army. The Army was still segregated. Black soldiers served in separate units, often under white officers, and were frequently assigned to labor and supply rather than combat. Medgar's unit went to Europe as part of the great Allied push that followed the D-Day landings in June 1944. He served in France and in Germany. He saw a continent at war, and he saw something else as well. In Europe, many people treated a Black American soldier first of all as an American. He was honorably discharged in 1946 and came home to Mississippi. And here is the contrast that would shape the rest of his life. He had worn his country's uniform in a war against fascism, and when he came home, his own state still would not let him vote. According to accounts of his life, Medgar and his brother Charles registered and went to cast their ballots in Decatur in 1946, and they were turned away by a crowd of armed white men. He did not forget that day. He went back to school instead, using the help available to veterans, and finished his education at Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College, a historically Black college in the southwest of the state. He studied business, he was active in campus life, and he became known as a serious, purposeful young man. At Alcorn he met a student named Myrlie Beasley. She had grown up in Vicksburg, raised largely by her grandmother and an aunt, and she had a quick mind and a gift for music. On December 24, 1951, Christmas Eve, Medgar and Myrlie were married. He graduated the following year, in 1952. They began their life together in the Mississippi Delta, where Medgar took a job selling insurance. That work took him door to door into the cabins of Black sharecroppers and farm workers across the Delta, and what he saw there, the poverty, the fear, the families living almost entirely at the mercy of the landowners, pulled him further into the fight for civil rights. Their first child, a son named Darrell Kenyatta, was born in 1953. A daughter, Reena Denise, followed in 1954. Then came the decision that would set the course of the rest of his life. In May 1954, the Supreme Court decided Brown versus Board of Education. Later that year, Medgar Evers applied for admission to the law school of the University of Mississippi in Oxford, the oldest public university in the state, which had never admitted a Black student. He was denied. One account says the university claimed he had not included the required letters of recommendation. But the attempt itself had been noticed. Leaders of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, the NAACP, were so impressed by his poise and his determination that instead of a law degree, they offered him a job. He became the organization's first field secretary for the state of Mississippi. The young family moved to Jackson. The job was enormous and it was dangerous. A field secretary was the organization's eyes and ears and hands in the state. Medgar Evers travelled thousands of miles on Mississippi roads, organizing local branches, signing up members, helping people try to register to vote, and documenting acts of violence and intimidation against Black citizens that local newspapers often refused to report. Myrlie worked alongside him in the office. When Emmett Till was murdered in the Delta in the summer of 1955, Medgar was among those who worked to investigate the killing and to find witnesses willing to speak, at a time when speaking could get a person killed. He organized boycotts of businesses that refused to treat Black customers with dignity. He pushed for the integration of schools, parks and public facilities. In 1960, a third child was born, a son named James Van Dyke Evers, whom the family called Van. In the fall of 1962, when James Meredith fought his way into the University of Mississippi, the same university that had turned Medgar away eight years earlier, Medgar Evers was among those who helped and advised him. That success made him even more visible, and visibility in Mississippi in 1962 was a kind of target. Accounts of the Evers family in those years describe a home that tried to be normal under extraordinary pressure. There were threatening phone calls, at the office and at the house. His name and his face were known across the state, because he was the man the newspapers quoted whenever the NAACP spoke in Mississippi. The family had practiced what to do if they were ever attacked. The children knew that if they heard gunfire, they should drop to the floor and get away from the windows, and the National Park Service, which today cares for the house, describes how the children would later follow that drill on the worst night of their lives. Even so, by every account, Medgar was a devoted father. He worked long hours, often into the night, but the house on Guynes Street was the center of his world. There is a line widely attributed to Medgar Evers that has been repeated in schools, museums and memorials ever since. You can kill a man, but you can't kill an idea. Whether or not he said those exact words on a particular occasion, they describe the way he lived. He knew the danger. He chose the work anyway. In the spring of 1963, that work reached its peak in what became known as the Jackson Movement. Students from Tougaloo College and Jackson State, along with local ministers and NAACP members, launched boycotts of downtown stores, picket lines and sit-ins aimed at ending segregation in the capital. On May 28, 1963, students sat down at the whites-only lunch counter of the Woolworth's store in downtown Jackson, and a white crowd poured mustard, ketchup and sugar over them and beat them while police stood outside. Photographs of that day went around the world. That same night, according to histories of the movement, someone threw a firebomb into the carport of the Evers home. It did not kill anyone. It was a warning. And Medgar Evers had already decided that he would not heed it.

To understand the last day of Medgar Evers's life, you have to understand the three weeks that came before it. In May 1963, the Jackson Movement was the most intense civil rights campaign in the state's history. Students marched downtown. Ministers led prayer protests. Boycotts emptied the cash registers of segregated stores on Capitol Street. The response from the city was arrests on a scale Jackson had never seen. So many demonstrators were taken into custody, many of them teenagers, that the city held them in makeshift pens at the state fairgrounds. At the center of all of it was Medgar Evers. He negotiated, he organized, he spoke to reporters, he raised bail money, and he carried the weight of a movement that was being watched by the entire country. On May 20, 1963, after the mayor of Jackson had gone on television to defend the city's position, Medgar Evers was given airtime of his own to answer him. It was an extraordinary moment. In a state where the television stations rarely gave a Black leader a microphone, he spoke directly to the people of Jackson, white and Black, from inside their own living rooms. In words preserved from that broadcast, he told them that whether Jackson and the state chose change or not, the years of change were upon them, and that in the racial picture, things would never be as they once were. For many Black Mississippians, it was the first time they had heard someone say such things on their own local television. For those who wanted to stop him, it made his face and his voice impossible to ignore. Eight days later came the violence at the Woolworth's lunch counter, and that same night the firebomb in the Evers carport. By early June, Medgar was exhausted. He was working day and night. And he knew, as everyone close to him knew, that the threats had become more serious. Now, the day itself. Tuesday, June 11, 1963. The news that day was not coming from Mississippi. It was coming from Alabama. In Tuscaloosa, the governor of Alabama, George Wallace, stood in a doorway at the University of Alabama to block two Black students, Vivian Malone and James Hood, from enrolling. The image of a governor physically standing in a schoolhouse door, in defiance of a federal court order, was broadcast across the country. A senior official of the Justice Department, Deputy Attorney General Nicholas Katzenbach, faced the governor at the door and asked him to step aside. Wallace refused and read a statement about the rights of the states. The Kennedy administration then federalized the Alabama National Guard. By the afternoon, Wallace had stepped aside and the two students were registered. That evening, President John F. Kennedy decided to speak to the nation. He went on television from the Oval Office with a speech that had been finished only shortly before he delivered it. It became known as his address on civil rights. And it was the moment the President of the United States finally put the full weight of his office behind the movement that Medgar Evers had been part of for almost a decade. We are confronted primarily with a moral issue, Kennedy said. It is as old as the scriptures and is as clear as the American Constitution. He described what it meant to be a Black American who could not eat in a restaurant open to the public, who could not send his children to the best public school available, who could not vote for the officials who represented him. He asked white Americans to imagine trading places. And he said that one hundred years of delay had passed since President Lincoln freed the slaves, and their heirs, their grandsons, were not fully free. He announced that he would ask Congress for sweeping civil rights legislation. Medgar Evers knew the speech was coming. Earlier that day he had called Myrlie and urged her to watch it. He himself would not be at home to see it. He had another evening of meetings, the ordinary, endless work of a movement in the middle of a campaign. So in the small house on Guynes Street, Myrlie Evers sat in front of the television with the children and watched the President of the United States say the words her husband had spent his life waiting to hear. Myrlie Evers was thirty years old that night. She had been married to Medgar for eleven and a half years. She had worked beside him in the NAACP office, she had answered the telephone when the threats came, and she understood better than anyone what his work cost and what it meant. Now a President was saying on national television the words the two of them had worked toward for years. Darrell was nine. Reena was eight. Van was three. On a school night, they might normally have been in bed. That night, according to the National Park Service, they waited up for their father, and they were watching television together when the hour grew late. Across the country, millions of families were watching the same broadcast. In Mississippi, plenty of people were furious about it. And somewhere in Jackson that night, someone was waiting with a rifle. According to the case that prosecutors would later present, the gunman took a position in a thicket of brush and trees across the street, a place with a clear line of sight into the driveway and the carport of the Evers home. Whoever chose that spot knew which house belonged to Medgar Evers, and knew that he would be coming home. Hours passed. Kennedy's speech ended. Medgar was still at his meetings. Neither he nor Myrlie could have known that the legislation the President had just promised would be signed into law a year later on a date that belonged to their family. July 2, 1964. Medgar's birthday. Then, a little after midnight, now on Wednesday, June 12, 1963, the meetings broke up and he drove home. One historical account puts the time of what happened next at about 12:40 in the morning. Others say simply just after midnight. He turned his car into the driveway of his house. He parked. He gathered up what he was carrying. In his arms was a bundle of T-shirts with a slogan printed across the front, the same four words the movement had been using all spring. Jim Crow Must Go. Inside the house, Myrlie and the children heard the car pull in. They knew the sound. It meant he was home. It meant one more day had ended safely. He stepped out of the car and turned toward the door. The house where his wife and three children were waiting was a few steps away. He was thirty-seven years old. He had been the NAACP's man in Mississippi for about eight and a half years, and in all that time he had never stopped coming home. He did not see the man in the brush across the street. He could not have. And then the family heard the sound that each of them would remember for the rest of their lives. A single rifle shot, loud and close, splitting the quiet of the June night. For a moment, the house was still. Then the children did what they had practiced. They hit the floor. Thirty-one years would pass before a jury would say out loud who fired that shot.

Myrlie Evers did not stay on the floor. She ran to the door and out into the carport, and there she found her husband. Medgar had collapsed near the door of the house, a few steps from safety. He had been shot in the back. He was bleeding badly. The National Park Service, which today tells the story of that night inside the house itself, describes how the children followed the drill their parents had taught them, dropping to the ground and making their way to the bathroom, and how the two older children helped their three-year-old brother into the bathtub, the safest place they knew. Then they came out, and they saw their father. There is no need to describe more than that. Neighbors came running. Others called the police, who arrived within minutes. Medgar Evers was lifted and rushed to University Hospital in Jackson, the teaching hospital of the University of Mississippi's medical center in the capital. Remember that hospital. It will appear in this story one more time, at the very end. He was still alive when he left the driveway. He did not survive. He died less than an hour after the shot was fired, from trauma and the loss of blood, at the age of thirty-seven. Myrlie was a widow at thirty. Three children, nine, eight and three, had lost their father. It was the early morning of June 12, 1963, only a few hours after the President had told the nation that civil rights was a moral issue as old as the scriptures. By dawn, the news was everywhere. Medgar Evers, the NAACP's leader in Mississippi, had been assassinated in his own driveway. The word assassinated was not an exaggeration. He was not killed in a robbery or a quarrel. He was hunted, at his home, because of who he was and what he did. Now the investigation began, and in this case, the first hours produced more physical evidence than many murder investigations produce in years. Investigators searched the area across the street, where the shot had come from. In a thicket of brush and vines, at a distance that one account puts at about 150 feet from the scene, they found a rifle. It was a military rifle of First World War design, a 1917 Enfield chambered for the .30-06 cartridge, the kind of powerful, accurate weapon that a hunter or a former soldier might own. Mounted on top of it was a telescopic sight. And on that sight, there was a fingerprint. Stop for a moment and consider what that means. The weapon that had killed Medgar Evers had been left behind, within sight of his house. And it carried a mark of the person who had last handled it. It was not a smudge on a wall or a print on a doorknob that anyone in the neighborhood might have touched. It was on the scope, the part of the rifle a shooter holds close and handles when he aims. In 1963 there was no DNA testing and no computer database of fingerprints that could be searched in seconds. Fingerprints were compared by trained examiners, card by card, by eye. But fingerprint identification was already more than half a century old in American courts, and it was trusted. If that print could be matched to a person, the investigation would have a name. Investigators also searched for the bullet. According to accounts of the investigation, the shot had passed through Medgar's body and continued into the house, through the front window and an interior wall, coming to rest inside the home where his family had been waiting. The physical evidence that night, then, was the rifle, the telescopic sight, the fingerprint on that sight, and the bullet. Remember those four things. They were gathered in a few hours on a June night in 1963, by local officers working a crime scene in the dark and the early light. Thirty years later, two of them would be missing, and one man would have to go looking for them. The reaction across Mississippi and the nation was immediate. In Jackson, the city's Black community was devastated and furious. The man who had carried their hopes in public for almost nine years was dead. In Washington, the White House condemned the killing. Newspapers across the country put the story on the front page, side by side with coverage of the President's speech of the night before. The contrast was impossible to miss. A President had asked the nation to choose justice, and within hours, a rifle in Mississippi had given an answer of its own. On Saturday, June 15, 1963, a funeral was held in Jackson. Thousands of mourners came. Many walked in a silent procession through the streets of the capital in the summer heat, behind the hearse. After the service, the grief boiled over. A crowd of young people marched toward downtown, and a large force of armed police met them. For a few tense minutes it looked as if Jackson would explode into violence. A Justice Department official named John Doar walked out into the street between the two sides and pleaded with the crowd to go home, and the moment passed. The work did not stop either. His older brother Charles Evers stepped forward and took over Medgar's post as the NAACP's field secretary in Mississippi, determined that the killing would not end what his brother had started. And the Federal Bureau of Investigation joined the hunt for the gunman, because the murder of a civil rights leader had become a national matter. Medgar Evers was not buried in Mississippi. He had served his country in the Second World War, and as a veteran he was entitled to rest in Arlington National Cemetery, across the Potomac River from Washington. His body was taken north, and he was buried there with full military honors. A man who had been turned away from the polls by armed men in his own hometown was laid to rest among the nation's soldiers, with a flag folded and handed to his widow. Accounts of those days also describe the President receiving Myrlie and the two older children at the White House. The killing reached far beyond politics. Within weeks, the Mississippi writer Eudora Welty, who lived in Jackson, wrote a short story told from the point of view of an imagined killer, and The New Yorker published it that summer. The young folk singer Bob Dylan wrote a song about the murder called Only a Pawn in Their Game, and he sang it in Greenwood, Mississippi, in July 1963 and again at the March on Washington that August. The singer Nina Simone wrote Mississippi Goddam in the angry months that followed, a song shaped by the killing of Medgar Evers and by the bombing of a church in Birmingham that September. In Washington, on August 28, 1963, about a quarter of a million people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial, and the dead man from Jackson was in the minds of many who were there. But in Jackson, the question was simpler and harder. Who had been waiting in the brush with that rifle. The answer was already sitting on the telescopic sight. And within days, the FBI would put a name to it.

The fingerprint on the telescopic sight went to the FBI. In 1963, the Bureau held the largest collection of fingerprint records in the country, cards taken from criminals, from government employees, and from millions of men who had served in the armed forces. Examiners compared the print from the scope with the cards on file, and the comparison led them to a man who had served in the United States Marine Corps during the Second World War. According to the FBI's own records of the case, the fingerprint on the gunsight was traced to Byron De La Beckwith of Greenwood, Mississippi. Investigators also traced the rifle. The 1917 Enfield, it turned out, had come into Beckwith's hands in 1960, through a trade with a friend. Within about ten days of the murder, Byron De La Beckwith was arrested, and he was charged with the murder of Medgar Evers. So who was he. Byron De La Beckwith was born on November 9, 1920, in Sacramento, California. As a child, after the death of his father, he was brought to Greenwood, Mississippi, a cotton town in the Delta, where his mother's family had deep roots. He grew up there. When the Second World War came, he joined the Marines and fought in the Pacific. After the war he returned to Greenwood, married, and worked as a salesman, most famously selling fertilizer to farmers across the Delta. He was not a stranger to the public. He was an outspoken white segregationist and a member of the White Citizens' Council, and he wrote letters to newspapers defending segregation in fierce terms. In June 1963, he was forty-two years old. Greenwood sits more than 90 miles north of Jackson. That distance would become the heart of his defense. Here is where this story turns from a murder investigation into something else. In almost any other case, a fingerprint on the murder weapon, a rifle traced to the suspect, and a suspect with a documented hatred of everything the victim stood for would be the foundation of a conviction. But this was Mississippi in 1964. The trial would take place in the Hinds County Courthouse in Jackson. The jury would be drawn from citizens on the rolls, and in Hinds County in 1964, that meant the jury would be white. The first trial began in the winter of 1964. The prosecutor was the district attorney of Hinds County, a lawyer named William Waller, who would later become governor of Mississippi. He presented the evidence. The rifle. The scope. The fingerprint. Myrlie Evers took the stand and testified about the night her husband was killed, facing a room full of white men who would decide whether anyone would answer for it. The defendant took the stand too. When he was asked about the rifle, according to an account of his testimony published at the time, he said that it could have been his, but that he was not sure. And during that trial, something happened that tells you more about Mississippi in 1964 than any statistic. Ross Barnett, who had been governor of Mississippi until that very January, the same governor who had fought to keep James Meredith out of the University of Mississippi, walked into the courtroom. According to accounts of the trial, while Myrlie Evers was testifying, the former governor went over to the defendant and greeted him in front of the jury. Some accounts describe a handshake. Others describe an embrace. Either way, the message to twelve white jurors was unmistakable. The most powerful political figure in the state was standing with the man accused of killing Medgar Evers. The jury deliberated for a long time. One account says that after more than thirty hours, the jurors told the judge that they could not agree on a verdict. They were split. A mistrial was declared. In legal terms, a mistrial after a hung jury is not an acquittal. It is a trial that produced no verdict at all. The state was free to try again, and it did. The second trial came in the spring of 1964. Again the jury was all white and all male. Again the prosecution presented the rifle and the fingerprint. And this time the defense brought forward witnesses that the jury would find hard to dismiss. Two police officers from Greenwood testified that they had seen Beckwith in Greenwood on the night of the murder, more than 90 miles away from Medgar Evers's driveway. If those officers were believed, he could not have fired the shot. Two sworn officers of the law, placing the defendant in his hometown at the hour of the murder. It was the kind of testimony that, in that courtroom and in that year, a white jury was unlikely to question. The prosecution could not overcome it. The second jury also deadlocked. A second mistrial was declared. What the public did not know in 1964, and would not learn for twenty-five years, was that the jury in that second trial had been examined in secret by an agency of the State of Mississippi. The Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, the segregationist agency created in 1956 and funded by the taxpayers of the state, had investigated the backgrounds of prospective jurors and passed that information along to help the defense of the man accused of killing Medgar Evers. Imagine it. The state was prosecuting a man for murder in its own courthouse, and at the same time, another arm of that same state was quietly helping his lawyers choose the jury. No one outside a small circle knew. The files were locked away. Meanwhile, the world was moving. On July 2, 1964, less than three months after the second mistrial, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act into law, the legislation that John F. Kennedy had proposed on the night Medgar Evers died. Kennedy himself did not live to see it. He had been assassinated in Dallas in November 1963. July 2 was Medgar Evers's birthday. He would have been thirty-nine. The law that outlawed segregation in public accommodations became law on his birthday, while the man charged with his murder was home in Greenwood. Count what the state had in 1964. One rifle. One telescopic sight. One fingerprint on that sight. A weapon traced to the defendant through a trade. A defendant whose views were no secret to anyone. Now count what it produced. Two trials. Twenty-four jurors, every one of them a white man, because in those years Mississippi did not allow women to serve on juries at all, a rule it did not change until 1968. And zero verdicts. Two trials, two hung juries. The case was not closed. Technically, Byron De La Beckwith remained under indictment for murder. But no prosecutor was going to try him a third time with the same evidence in front of the same kind of jury. In 1969, the prosecution of the case was formally dropped. In the language of the law, there was no verdict and no acquittal. But many people in Mississippi understood exactly what it meant. He had gotten away with it. Medgar Evers had been dead for less than six years. His children were growing up without him. And the rifle with the fingerprint on its scope was about to vanish.

After 1964, the Evers family had to decide how to live in the shadow of a murder that had no verdict. Myrlie Evers made a choice that many people understood and some criticized. She took her three children and left Mississippi. They moved to Claremont, California, east of Los Angeles, far from the house on Guynes Street and far from the people who had cheered the man accused of killing her husband. She went back to school and earned her degree from Pomona College. She wrote a book about her life with Medgar, called For Us, the Living, published in 1967, so that his story would be told in his family's own words. She spoke at churches and colleges and civil rights gatherings across the country. She ran for Congress in California in 1970 and lost, but she kept going. She built a career of her own, in business and in public service in Los Angeles. In 1975 she married again, to a longshoreman and civil rights activist named Walter Williams, and she became Myrlie Evers-Williams. And through all of it, she did something quietly stubborn. She kept the memory of the case alive, and she kept asking the authorities in Mississippi whether anything could be done. The answer, for years, was no. She was told, in one way or another, that the case was over and that nothing more could be done after so many years. Prosecutors came and went. The courthouse in Jackson stood where it had always stood. She kept asking anyway. Meanwhile, the country changed around the case in ways that Medgar Evers had fought for and never saw. In 1965, Congress passed the Voting Rights Act, and federal examiners began registering Black voters in Mississippi counties where they had been shut out for generations. Within a few years, the number of Black registered voters in the state had risen dramatically. In 1969, Medgar's brother Charles Evers was elected mayor of Fayette, Mississippi, and became the first Black mayor of a racially mixed Mississippi town since Reconstruction. In 1970, the City University of New York opened a new college in Brooklyn and named it Medgar Evers College. Schools, streets, and parks would carry his name. His name was everywhere. Except in a courtroom. The case did not move. In April 1968, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis, and the country was reminded again how many of the movement's leaders had been killed for their work. Medgar Evers had been one of the first. For a new generation of Americans growing up in the 1970s and 1980s, his name became a line in a history book, if it appeared at all. The man charged with killing him turned sixty in November 1980. And it is worth stopping here to tally what was happening to the evidence, because this is the part of the story where the system failed not by deciding something, but by forgetting it. The rifle with the fingerprint on its scope, the most important single piece of evidence in the case, was not where it should have been. Records compiled later from the files of the prosecutor who reopened the case show that the murder weapon had gone missing from the evidence room. The official transcripts of the two trials of 1964, the sworn testimony of every witness, could not be found in the court's files. Crime scene photographs had to be tracked down. The physical case against the man charged with killing Medgar Evers was quietly scattering, piece by piece, while everyone who might have cared assumed it was all sitting in a box somewhere in Jackson. And Byron De La Beckwith did not fade away. In 1973, he was arrested by police in New Orleans, with a bomb in his car. He was later convicted in Louisiana on charges arising from that arrest and spent time in prison there. After his release, he eventually moved out of Mississippi altogether, to Tennessee, near Chattanooga, where he married again and lived for years in the town of Signal Mountain. To the people who still remembered Medgar Evers, it seemed as if the man charged with his murder would grow old in peace. And then there were the files. Remember the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission. For about two decades, from 1956 into the 1970s, the agency investigated civil rights workers, kept files on thousands of people, paid informants, and worked to protect segregation in every way it could. By the mid-1970s, the world it was built to defend was gone, and in 1977 the agency was formally abolished. But its records were not destroyed. The Mississippi legislature ordered them sealed, locked away from public view for decades into the future. Civil rights groups and some of the people who had been spied on went to court to open them. The American Civil Liberties Union filed a lawsuit in 1977. The case dragged on for years. Inside those sealed boxes were the papers that showed what the state had done during the second trial of 1964, when an agency of the State of Mississippi had looked into the backgrounds of the people who might sit on the jury. Nobody outside knew it was there. Let us lay out the ledger of these years in plain numbers. From the night of June 12, 1963, to the end of 1988, twenty-five years passed. In that time there were two trials and zero verdicts. Twenty-four jurors, all white men. One prosecution formally dropped in 1969. One rifle missing from the place it was supposed to be. Trial transcripts missing from official custody. One secret state agency whose files were sealed by law. Medgar's children, nine, eight and three on the night he died, had grown into adults in their thirties and late twenties. Myrlie had gone from thirty years old to her mid-fifties. Medgar Evers himself had now been dead longer than he had been an adult. And the man charged with his murder had spent most of those years free, living in a house of his own, with a family of his own, the life that had been taken from the man in the carport. It is tempting to believe that the long silence was nobody's fault, that cases simply go cold and evidence simply fades. But that is not what happened here. The suspect had a name on the first indictment. The evidence was collected on the first night. What failed was not science. It was the will of a system that, in 1964, was designed to protect people like the accused, and that afterwards had little interest in reopening a wound it would rather forget. In a cold case, time is usually the enemy. In this case, time was something else as well. Time was changing who sat on Mississippi juries. Time was opening Mississippi's sealed archives. And time had brought a young reporter to the state's largest newspaper, a man who did not yet know that he was about to pick up a thread that would lead all the way back to that driveway. In February 1989, some of the Sovereignty Commission's secret records slipped out into the light. And a few months later, on the first day of October, a story appeared in the newspaper in Jackson that nobody in Mississippi's government wanted to read.

In most of the cases on this channel, the breakthrough comes from a laboratory. A new DNA test. A genealogy database. A scientist reading a sample that was unreadable twenty years before. This case is different, and in a way it is more unsettling. Nothing about the science changed between 1964 and 1994. The fingerprint on the telescopic sight in 1994 was the same fingerprint it had been in 1963. What changed was everything around it. So before we meet the people who reopened the case, it is worth understanding the tools they used, because none of them were new inventions. They were old tools, used by people who finally decided to use them. The first tool is the oldest. The fingerprint. The skin on the tips of human fingers is covered in ridges, the fine raised lines you can see if you look closely at your own hand. Those ridges form loops, whorls and arches, and along each ridge there are tiny features, places where a line ends, splits in two, or forms a small island. Examiners call these details minutiae. The pattern forms before birth and does not change through life, except for injury. No two people have ever been found to share the same arrangement, not even identical twins. When a finger touches a smooth surface, like the metal and glass of a telescopic sight, it can leave a copy of that pattern behind in the natural oils of the skin. Investigators lift or photograph the print, and an examiner compares it with known prints, point by point, looking for the same features in the same positions. In 1963, that comparison linked the print on the scope to a former Marine. In 1994, the same comparison would be presented to a new jury. A fingerprint does not age. It does not forget. It does not care who is on the jury. The second tool is the archive. When people talk about cold cases, they usually mean evidence in a property room. But some of the most important evidence in this case was not in a police locker at all. It was in the sealed files of the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission. For more than a decade after the agency was abolished in 1977, those files sat locked away under a law passed by the legislature, while a lawsuit brought by the American Civil Liberties Union worked its way through the courts. In February 1989, during that long legal fight, some of the commission's records became public. A reporter would eventually obtain about 2,400 pages of them. And inside were documents showing that, during the second trial of 1964, the commission had investigated prospective jurors and that this information was meant to help the defense of the accused man. Why did that matter so much, legally. Because it changed the way the two hung juries of 1964 could be understood. They were no longer simply juries that could not agree. One of them had been examined, in secret, by an agency of the very state that was prosecuting the case, with the information steered toward the defense. That raised a powerful moral argument, and a practical one. If a prosecutor wanted to bring the case back, the public now had reason to see the earlier trials as compromised, not as fair hearings that had run their course. And there was a legal key that had been sitting in plain sight for twenty-five years. Under the American Constitution, a person cannot be tried twice for the same crime after a verdict. That protection is called double jeopardy. But a hung jury is not a verdict. Neither trial of 1964 had ended in an acquittal. That meant that the door to a third trial had never been legally locked. It had only been closed. The third tool is the paper trail of the old trials themselves. By the late 1980s, some of the people who had testified in 1964 were dead. Memories had faded. But sworn testimony given under oath in a courtroom does not fade if someone keeps a copy of it. Under long-established rules of evidence, the earlier sworn testimony of a witness who has since died can, in certain circumstances, be read aloud to a new jury. That meant the transcripts of 1964 were not just history. They were witnesses. If they could be found, the voices of people long gone could speak again in a Mississippi courtroom. The same was true of the rifle and the crime scene photographs. Without the rifle, the fingerprint on its scope would be a story. With the rifle, it would be evidence. The fourth tool is the one that sounds the most dramatic, and it was handled with great care. The exhumation. To exhume a body means to lift it from the grave, with the permission of the courts and usually of the family, so that it can be examined again by a medical expert. It is not done lightly. It is a painful thing to ask of any family, and for a man buried with military honors in Arlington National Cemetery, it required the cooperation of authorities far beyond Mississippi. Prosecutors preparing a new trial needed modern forensic evidence about how Medgar Evers had died, evidence that would stand up in a courtroom in the 1990s. So in 1991, nearly thirty years after the burial, Medgar Evers's body was exhumed, and an autopsy was performed by the forensic pathologist Dr. Michael Baden. What he found was remarkable. The body, he would later say, was still intact, almost three decades after the funeral. One account suggests that the quality of the casket may have helped preserve it far better than anyone expected. For the family, the exhumation reopened an old grief in the most literal way. Medgar's youngest son, who had been three years old on the night of the shooting and had grown up with almost no memory of his father, was able to see his father's face. The autopsy produced a modern medical record of the fatal wound, and Baden's work became part of the case the state would bring to trial. The fifth tool is people. Over thirty years, a man who believes he has gotten away with something sometimes talks. He talks to friends, to acquaintances, to people he thinks share his views, to people he wants to impress. Each of those listeners becomes a potential witness, if anyone ever comes looking for them. Some of them, decades later, would decide that they could no longer keep what they had heard to themselves. And the sixth tool was not a tool at all. It was the jury box. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 had changed the voter rolls of Mississippi, and the voter rolls fed the jury pools. By the 1990s, a jury in Mississippi could include Black citizens and women, people whose own parents had been barred from that box. A jury that looked like Mississippi would hear the same fingerprint differently. None of these tools was new. All of them had been available, in one form or another, for years. What was missing was someone willing to pick them up. That is the real lesson of this case. And what happened next would become a model for a whole wave of reopened civil rights murders across the South, from Mississippi to Alabama, in the years that followed. But first, someone had to open the first box. It started with a reporter.

His name was Jerry Mitchell, and in the late 1980s he was a young investigative reporter at The Clarion-Ledger, the largest newspaper in Mississippi, based in Jackson. He was white, he was still early in his career, and by his own later account, he did not grow up knowing much about the civil rights killings that had happened in his own state. That changed around the end of the 1980s, when the release of the film Mississippi Burning, about the murders of three civil rights workers in Neshoba County in 1964, sent him digging into the real history behind the movie. He started asking questions that most of his elders in Mississippi preferred not to ask. Then, in 1989, the Sovereignty Commission's sealed files began to leak. In February of that year, in the middle of the long lawsuit over the records, some of them became public. Mitchell went after them. Over time, according to a profile of his work in the American Journalism Review, he walked away with about 2,400 pages of documents the state had kept secret for years. He read through them. Names. Reports. Memos written by investigators of a state agency about the private lives of Mississippians. And among those pages he found something about the Evers case. The documents showed that during Byron De La Beckwith's second trial in 1964, the Sovereignty Commission had investigated prospective jurors, and that the agency was working to help the defense. Mitchell wrote it up. His story ran in The Clarion-Ledger on Sunday, October 1, 1989. Years later, he described the date himself in an interview with the program Democracy Now. My story ran October 1st, 1989, he said. And he was candid about how hopeless it seemed at the time. When the story appeared, he said, the odds were literally more than a million to one against the case ever being reopened and prosecuted again. The story landed in Jackson like a stone through a window. And one reader who had waited twenty-five years for something like it was Myrlie Evers-Williams. She was living in California. She had never stopped asking. And now, for the first time in decades, she had something new to ask with. She contacted the prosecutor's office in Hinds County, the same office that had tried and failed to convict Beckwith twice in 1964, and she asked it to reopen the case of her husband's murder. The request reached an assistant district attorney named Bobby DeLaughter, who worked under the longtime district attorney of Hinds County, Ed Peters. He was a white Mississippian, born in the 1950s, too young to remember the night Medgar Evers died. He was a career prosecutor, not a crusader, and many people around him in Jackson saw no reason to dig up a case that powerful people in the state wanted buried. Reopening it would mean confronting old men, old families, old loyalties. It would mean going against the grain of a great deal of Mississippi history. And it would mean starting with a problem that seemed impossible on its face. The evidence was gone. The rifle was missing from the evidence room. The official transcripts of the two trials of 1964 could not be found. Crime scene photographs had to be tracked down from wherever they had ended up. Some witnesses were dead. DeLaughter agreed to look anyway. It was the beginning of an unlikely working partnership, between a Black widow in California and a white prosecutor in Jackson, that would last more than four years. And piece by piece, the evidence began to come back. According to the records of his own papers, now held by the Mississippi Department of Archives and History, his pursuit of the case led to the recovery of the crime scene photographs, the transcripts of the 1964 trials, and the murder weapon itself, the rifle that had been missing from the evidence room. Think about that rifle for a moment. It was the same 1917 Enfield that investigators had pulled from the thicket across from the Evers home on June 12, 1963. It had been in a courtroom twice. It had vanished from the place it was supposed to be kept. And now, after a quarter of a century, it was back in the hands of the State of Mississippi, with the same telescopic sight, and the same story to tell. At the same time, something else happened that no laboratory could have produced. Publicity brought witnesses. As news spread that the Evers case might be reopened, people began to come forward with things they had heard Beckwith say over the years. A woman named Mary Ann Adams. A woman named Peggy Morgan, who had lived in Greenwood with her husband. A former tenant of Beckwith's named Dan Prince. A former member of the Ku Klux Klan who had become an informant for the FBI, Elluard Davis, known as Dick. Each of them had a memory of the man charged in the Evers case speaking about the killing, sometimes in words they understood as an admission, sometimes in language so ugly that it cannot be repeated here with any dignity. For years they had kept those memories to themselves. Now they were ready to talk. In December 1990, a grand jury in Hinds County returned a new indictment. Byron De La Beckwith was charged once again with the murder of Medgar Evers. Twenty-seven years had passed since the night in the driveway. The man who had been forty-two years old at the time of his first arrest was now seventy. Myrlie Evers-Williams was fifty-seven. She had been thirty when she heard the shot. He was living in Signal Mountain, Tennessee. And he had no intention of coming back to Mississippi quietly. He fought extradition from Tennessee for about ten months. His lawyers argued that the passage of time itself made a fair trial impossible, that witnesses had died, that memories had faded, that his constitutional right to a speedy trial had been destroyed by the state's own delay. The fight over whether he could even be tried would go on for more than two years. In 1991, he was finally returned to Mississippi to face the charge. A Seattle Times report early that year described him as seventy years old and ailing, as Mississippi moved to bring him back for a third trial after two deadlocked juries. And in that same year, 1991, the state took the step we described a moment ago, the exhumation at Arlington, so that a new jury would have modern medical evidence of how Medgar Evers had died. So the pieces were in place. The fingerprint that had never changed. The rifle that had been lost and found. The transcripts that let the dead speak. The autopsy that gave the case modern medical proof. The secret files that showed how the earlier juries had been handled. And the new witnesses, people who had heard the accused talk about the killing and had carried those words for decades. What remained was the one thing that had always defeated this case. A jury. In January 1994, more than thirty years after the shot in the driveway, Byron De La Beckwith walked into the Hinds County Courthouse in Jackson to stand trial for murder for the third time.

The third trial of Byron De La Beckwith took place in the same city, in the same courthouse, on the same charge, as the two trials of 1964. The judge was L. Breland Hilburn of the Hinds County Circuit Court. The prosecutor at the center of the case was Bobby DeLaughter. And the man at the defense table was seventy-three years old, a long way from the forty-two-year-old fertilizer salesman who had sat in that building three decades earlier. But the most important difference was not in the judge's chair or at the defense table. It was in the jury box. In 1964, both juries had been made up entirely of white men. In 1994, the jury that would decide the case was made up of twelve Mississippians of both races and both sexes. Eight of the jurors were Black. Four were white. Seven were women and five were men. For the first time, the people who would decide whether Byron De La Beckwith had killed Medgar Evers looked like the people of Mississippi. The prosecution laid out its case much as the state had tried to do thirty years before, but with pieces that had not been there in 1964, and with pieces that had been there all along but had been lost. There was the rifle, the 1917 Enfield found in the brush across from the Evers home. There was the telescopic sight. There was the fingerprint on that sight, and the testimony that linked it to the defendant. Because some of the witnesses from 1964 had died, the prosecution relied on the transcripts of the earlier trials, the old sworn testimony brought back into the courtroom so that the jury could hear what had been said under oath when the events were fresh. In that way, voices from 1964 were heard again in 1994. Myrlie Evers-Williams took the witness stand once more. She had testified about her husband's murder in the same city when she was thirty years old. Now she was sixty, and she told it again. Then came the new witnesses, the people who had come forward after the case was reopened. The magazine Southern Changes, published in Atlanta, reported on them at the time under the heading The Six New Witnesses. Mary Ann Adams recalled hearing Beckwith speak of the killing in contemptuous, dehumanizing language about Medgar Evers. Peggy Morgan described an encounter with Beckwith from the years when she and her husband had lived in Greenwood. Dan Prince, who had rented from Beckwith, testified that Beckwith had admitted the killing to him. Elluard Davis, the former Klansman turned FBI informant, described Beckwith talking about the idea of selective killings. Each of them added the same thing to the case from a different direction. The man charged with killing Medgar Evers had not kept quiet about it. The defense fought back on every point. Beckwith's lawyers argued, as they had argued for years, that the long delay had made a fair trial impossible, that the witnesses who could have helped him were gone and that memories were no longer reliable. They attacked the new witnesses. According to the Southern Changes account, the defense portrayed Dan Prince as a drunk, and as a man trying to get even with Beckwith for evicting him. They pointed again to the alibi of 1964, the testimony that the defendant had been seen in Greenwood, more than 90 miles away. Byron De La Beckwith denied that he had killed Medgar Evers. He had always denied it. And then the case went to the jury. Under Mississippi law, as in every state, a murder conviction requires a unanimous verdict. In 1964, two juries of white men had been unable to reach one. Now twelve citizens, Black and white, men and women, would have to agree, every one of them, or the case would fail for a third time. Same rifle. Same fingerprint. Same courthouse. A different jury. On Saturday, February 5, 1994, the jurors came back. Their verdict was guilty. Byron De La Beckwith was convicted of the murder of Medgar Evers. It had been thirty years, seven months and twenty-four days since the shot in the driveway. In the courtroom, Myrlie Evers-Williams reacted with two words that LIFE magazine's history of the case would later quote, words spoken to the husband she had lost when she was thirty years old. Yes, Medgar. An Associated Press photograph from that day shows Myrlie and her daughter Reena, who had been eight years old on the night of the shooting, cheering the verdict. For many Black Mississippians, the verdict meant something larger than the conviction of one man. It meant that a jury in Mississippi had finally said out loud, in a court of law, what so many people had known since 1963. Judge Hilburn sentenced Byron De La Beckwith to life in prison. Some sources describe the sentence as life without the possibility of parole. Others describe it simply as life imprisonment. In practical terms, at the age of seventy-three, it meant he would almost certainly never be free again. In legal terms, from that day forward he was no longer a man charged with a crime. He was a convicted murderer. He appealed. His lawyers took the case to the Mississippi Supreme Court, arguing again that the decades of delay had violated his constitutional right to a speedy trial. On December 22, 1997, the state's highest court ruled against him. The vote was four to two. Justice Mike Mills wrote the opinion for the majority, and according to a Mississippi Today account of the ruling, he tied the long delay to the involvement of the Sovereignty Commission in the earlier trials. In other words, the court would not allow the defendant to benefit from a delay that the state's own secret agency had helped create. The conviction stood. Byron De La Beckwith spent his final years in the custody of the State of Mississippi, held at the Central Mississippi Correctional Facility in Rankin County, across the river from Jackson. In January 2001, he was taken from his prison cell to a hospital in Jackson. On January 21, 2001, he died there, at the age of eighty, still a prisoner, still convicted of the murder of Medgar Evers. The Associated Press identified the place where he died as the University Medical Center in Jackson. If you remember the beginning of this story, you will remember that hospital. Its teaching hospital, University Hospital, was where, on the night of June 12, 1963, Medgar Evers had been rushed from his driveway and had died less than an hour later. The man who had been convicted of killing him died in the same medical center, more than thirty-seven years later, as an inmate. Medgar Evers had been thirty-seven years old when he died. His killer had lived to be eighty. But he did not die a free man, and he did not die an innocent one in the eyes of the law. A jury of Mississippians had seen to that. What remained was the question that every family in a case like this has to face once the verdict is in. What does justice mean, when it comes thirty years late.

For Myrlie Evers-Williams, the verdict of February 5, 1994, was not the end of the story. It was a release. Accounts of her life describe a woman who had carried anger for three decades and who felt, at last, that she might be able to set it down. And she did not set down the work. The year after the verdict, in 1995, she was elected to lead the national board of the NAACP, the organization her husband had served in Mississippi until the night he died. She held that post until 1998, steering the organization through a difficult period of debt and internal division. In January 2013, half a century after the shot in the driveway, she stood before the nation at the second inauguration of President Barack Obama, the first Black President of the United States, and delivered the invocation. A woman who had once been the widow of a man killed for trying to help Black Mississippians vote was now speaking at the swearing-in of a Black President. The children grew up and made their own lives. Darrell Kenyatta Evers, who had been nine years old on the night his father was killed, died in 2001, the same year as the man convicted of killing his father. Reena, who had been eight, was photographed beside her mother cheering the verdict in 1994. Van, the three-year-old who had been lifted into the bathtub by his brother and sister, grew up to see his father's face for the first time in memory at the exhumation of 1991. The house on Guynes Street stayed in the family's hands until 1993, when the Evers family gave it to Tougaloo College, the historically Black college whose students had marched in the Jackson Movement. In 2019, Congress passed and the President signed a law creating the Medgar and Myrlie Evers Home National Monument. In 2020, the National Park Service took ownership of the house, and today visitors can stand in the carport where Myrlie found her husband, and in the rooms where three children dropped to the floor because their parents had taught them to. In 1996, two years after the verdict, the story of the reopened case reached movie theaters in a Hollywood film called Ghosts of Mississippi. The United States Navy named a cargo ship the USNS Medgar Evers. And on May 3, 2024, at the White House, President Joe Biden awarded Medgar Evers the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the nation's highest civilian honor, sixty-one years after his death. The reporter whose story had restarted it all did not stop with one case. In the years after the Evers verdict, Jerry Mitchell's reporting helped bring renewed prosecutions in some of the most notorious civil rights killings in the South, including the 1966 firebombing murder of Vernon Dahmer in Hattiesburg, the 1963 bombing of the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama, which killed four girls, and the 1964 murders of three civil rights workers in Neshoba County, Mississippi, the case behind the film that had first sent him looking. For that work, in 2009, he received a fellowship from the MacArthur Foundation, the award often called the genius grant. Bobby DeLaughter, the prosecutor who had agreed to look when almost no one else would, wrote his own account of the case, a book called Never Too Late, published in 2001. The conviction of Byron De La Beckwith had shown that it could be done. It had shown that an old case was not a dead case, that a hung jury was not an acquittal, and that the records a state wanted buried could become evidence. In 1998, after the long lawsuit, the files of the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission were finally opened to the public. In 2008, Congress passed a federal law named after Emmett Till, the boy whose murder Medgar Evers had investigated in 1955, to fund the reinvestigation of unsolved civil rights era killings. A line can be drawn from that law back to a driveway in Jackson. There are still things in this story that will never be fully known. We do not know everything about who helped, who looked away, and who kept silent in the years after 1963. We know that two juries of white men could not agree. We know that a state agency examined jurors to help the defense. We know that a rifle and the transcripts of two trials went missing from the places they should have been. And we know that it took a widow, a reporter and a prosecutor, working decades after the fact, to find what the system had let slip away. And we know that in the end, a jury of eight Black and four white citizens, seven women and five men, sat in the same courthouse that had failed twice, looked at the same rifle, and agreed. So let us go back, one last time, to where this began. It is just after midnight on June 12, 1963. A car turns into a driveway on a quiet street in Jackson, Mississippi. A man of thirty-seven steps out, tired after another long night of meetings, carrying an armful of T-shirts printed with four words. Jim Crow Must Go. Inside, a woman of thirty and three children, nine, eight and three, have waited up for him, with the television still on after a President told the nation that this was a moral issue. They hear the car. They know the sound. It means he is home. That was who Medgar Evers was. Not the case, not the headline, the person. He was a boy from Decatur who grew up under rules he never accepted. He was a soldier who went to Europe for his country and came home to find that his country would not let him vote. He was a college student who met a girl from Vicksburg and married her on Christmas Eve. He was a father of three who taught his children what to do if someone shot at their house, because he knew someone might. He was a man who applied to a law school that had never admitted a Black student, and who then spent the rest of his life making sure that others would not be turned away. He was a husband who, on the last day of his life, called his wife to make sure she would watch the President speak about civil rights. He was a voice on Jackson television telling his city that the years of change had arrived. And he was a man who came home every night, until the night he could not. The shot that killed him was fired in the dark, in a state where, for generations, killings like this one had gone unpunished. For thirty-one years, this one did too. And then it did not. If there is one lesson in this story, it is that time does not erase the truth. It only waits for someone to come and get it. A fingerprint on a telescopic sight waited thirty-one years for a jury that would listen. If this story stays with you, subscribe and share it. We tell the stories of forgotten victims and of the people who refuse to let them be forgotten. The next case is waiting. And remember, no case stays cold forever.
