For the first time, the killer had a genetic signature. It was not a name. It was not a face. But it was something that belonged to him and to no one else on earth, except an identical twin. It is hard to overstate what that meant. Since the night of the murder, every lead had depended on memories and interviews. Now there was a piece of evidence that could not forget, could not lie and could not be confused. If investigators ever found the right man, this profile could say yes or no with near certainty. But there was the old problem. To use it, you needed a name to compare it with. In the year 2000, investigators processed the DNA again, using more advanced technology, and entered the profile into CODIS, the FBI's Combined DNA Index System. CODIS is often described in television dramas as if it were a magic machine that knows everyone. It is not. Think of it as a very specific kind of phone book. It contains DNA profiles from people who have been convicted of certain crimes, and in some states people who have been arrested, along with unknown profiles from unsolved crime scenes. When you search a new crime scene profile, it can only find a match if the person who left it is already in that book. If the killer had never been convicted of a qualifying crime, if he had never been required to give a DNA sample, then CODIS would have nothing to say about him. Year after year, the profile from the bathroom at Bartlett Hall sat in that system. And year after year, it found no one. Here is the ledger of time, in plain numbers. From the murder in April 1993 to the first DNA profile in the late 1990s, several years. From the murder to the entry into the national database in 2000, seven years. From that entry to the moment, in 2018, when investigators finally tried something else, another eighteen years. Eighteen years in which the profile of Sophie's killer was searched, again and again, against a database in which he simply did not exist. Meanwhile, the file passed from one generation of investigators to the next. Each one inherited the file, reread the interviews and looked for anything that had been missed. Around 2010, cold case detectives with the Alaska State Troopers went back to one of the people who had come up in the early investigation, the former Bartlett Hall security guard who had been fired for having a gun in the dormitory. By then, he was no longer a college student. He was a lawyer, practicing in Portland, Oregon. Troopers traveled to his law office to interview him again, and they collected a sample of his DNA. The DNA from the crime scene would later be shown to belong to someone else. But this man had been close to someone else in Bartlett Hall. He had been the roommate and best friend of another freshman. And at some point in those years of questioning, he told investigators something that would, much later, become a key piece of the prosecution's case. He said that his roommate had owned a .22-caliber handgun when they were students in 1993. Remember the bullet. A .22. The roommate's statement did not by itself prove anything. Lots of people owned .22s. And years later, in court, the defense would argue that the roommate had pointed at his friend to deflect suspicion from himself. But it was a thread. And in the language of cold cases, a thread is everything. It is worth noting the coincidence of timing. 2010, the same year troopers were sitting in a law office in Oregon, was the year a small free website called GEDmatch was launched by two genealogy enthusiasts. Nobody in Alaska knew it yet, but the tool that would solve this case had just been born. Beyond the investigation, the world around Sophie's case was changing too. Across North America, Indigenous families and activists were raising their voices about a crisis that had been ignored for generations, the crisis of missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls. In Canada and in the United States, the phrase began to appear on banners, in hashtags, and in government reports. Red handprints painted across the mouth, symbolizing the voices that had been silenced, appeared at marches. Red dresses were hung in empty spaces to represent the women who were gone. In 2018, the Urban Indian Health Institute, a Seattle-based tribal epidemiology center, published a national report on missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls in American cities. In the data it was able to gather, Alaska had 52 cases, the fourth-highest number of any state. And the report's central finding was not just about the numbers it found, but about the numbers it could not find. No one, it showed, was consistently tracking these cases at all. As one summary of the problem put it, no one knows the true scope of the problem, because no one regularly reports the data. Sophie's case belonged to that larger story. A young Indigenous woman, killed far from her village, her case unsolved for decades. In Alaska Native communities, almost every family knows a story like hers. Some of those stories end with an arrest. Far too many never do. By 2018, Sophie Sergie had been gone for twenty-five years. Twenty-five years. That is longer than the twenty years she had been alive. A baby born in Pitkas Point in the week Sophie died would by then have been a grown adult, old enough to have finished college and started a career. Sophie herself would have been in her mid-forties. The students who had lived in Bartlett Hall that spring were now in their forties. Every one of those milestones was a measure of the life that had been taken from her, and of the time the system had needed and still did not have an answer. Her mother, Elena, was still waiting. Her brother, Alexi, had lived a quarter of a century without his sister. The man whose DNA sat in the national database had been living in Maine for most of those years. He had built a life. He had become a nurse. Whatever his history, his DNA was not in CODIS. For the investigators searching that database, he did not exist. Then, in April 2018, something happened in California that changed everything, not just for one case but for hundreds of them across the country, including a file in Alaska that had been waiting since 1993.

On April 24, 2018, investigators in California arrested a 72-year-old former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo near Sacramento. For more than forty years, the man known as the Golden State Killer had been one of the most notorious unidentified criminals in American history, linked to a string of murders and sexual assaults across California in the 1970s and 1980s. He had never been caught. His DNA had sat in databases for years, matching no one, exactly like the DNA from Bartlett Hall. What changed was not the evidence. What changed was the question investigators asked of it. Instead of asking whether the killer's DNA matched anyone in a criminal database, they asked whether it matched any of his relatives in a genealogy database. To understand why that worked, and why it would work in Alaska too, we need to step away from Fairbanks for a few minutes and look at the science as a story of its own. The DNA profile that police had been using since the 1990s, the kind stored in CODIS, looks at a small number of specific locations in a person's genetic code, roughly twenty in the modern system. It is like checking twenty letters scattered across an enormous book. Those twenty letters are enough to tell two strangers apart with near certainty. But they say very little about who your cousins are. Consumer genealogy tests, the kind that millions of people bought in the 2010s to learn about their ancestry, work differently. They look at hundreds of thousands of locations across the genome, tiny single-letter variations called SNPs, short for single nucleotide polymorphisms. With that much information, you can do something the old profiles could never do. You can compare two people and measure how much DNA they share, and from that, estimate how closely they are related. A parent and child share about half. First cousins share about an eighth. Second and third cousins share smaller and smaller pieces, but those pieces can still be measured. Now imagine a public website where ordinary people upload those consumer test results, hoping to find long-lost relatives. That website existed. It was GEDmatch, launched in 2010 by two genealogy enthusiasts. It was free, it was open, and by 2018 it held the genetic data of roughly a million people. Most of them had uploaded their DNA to find family, to solve an adoption mystery, or to fill in a branch of their family tree. Very few of them imagined that their DNA might one day point toward a relative who had committed a crime. Here is how the method works, step by step. First, a laboratory takes the crime scene sample, the old biological evidence, and produces a SNP profile from it, the same kind of rich, detailed data a consumer test would produce. Second, that profile is uploaded to a genealogy database that allows it. Third, the database returns a list of people who share DNA with the unknown person, usually distant cousins, sometimes closer relatives. Fourth, genealogists take those matches and build their family trees backward, sometimes for generations, using birth records, marriage records, obituaries and census data, until they find the ancestors that the matches have in common. Fifth, they build those trees forward again, tracing every descendant, looking for a person who is the right age, the right sex, and was in the right place at the right time. And finally, when they have a name, investigators must confirm it the old-fashioned way, by obtaining that person's DNA and comparing it directly with the crime scene profile. The genealogy produces a lead. Only a direct DNA match produces evidence. After DeAngelo's arrest, police departments across the country with old, unmatched DNA profiles suddenly saw a door open. One of the companies that stepped through it was Parabon NanoLabs, a DNA technology firm based in Reston, Virginia. In May 2018, Parabon launched a genetic genealogy unit led by a genealogist named CeCe Moore. Moore was already well known in the genealogy world. She had spent years helping adoptees find their birth families and had appeared on television programs about family history. Now she was applying the same skills to cold cases. Within weeks of launching its service, Parabon's team was helping to identify suspects in cold cases across the United States, cases from Washington state to Pennsylvania that had been unsolved for decades. In Pennsylvania, for example, Parabon's genealogy work helped lead investigators in June 2018 to a man charged with the 1992 murder of a young teacher named Christy Mirack in Lancaster County. Detectives there confirmed the lead by collecting chewing gum and a water bottle he had discarded, and the lab found a match. He later pleaded guilty. Case after case followed the same pattern. An old profile that had matched no one for decades. A distant cousin in a genealogy database. Weeks or months of family-tree research. A name. A confirmation sample. An arrest. The cold case world had changed almost overnight. In Alaska, the troopers' cold case investigators were paying attention. According to reporting at the time of the arrest, it was the Golden State Killer case that inspired them to try the new technique on Sophie's murder. In July 2018, the Alaska State Troopers' cold case unit sent the unknown DNA profile from the Bartlett Hall evidence to Parabon NanoLabs. It is worth noticing what had to be true for this to work. The evidence collected in 1993 had to have been preserved carefully enough, for twenty-five years, to yield usable DNA. The investigators who inherited the file had to keep it alive long enough for the science to arrive. And somewhere in the world, at least one relative of Sophie's killer had to have tested their own DNA, uploaded it to a public database, and made it available for comparison. None of those things was guaranteed. In many cold cases, at least one of them fails. Evidence degrades, or is lost, or is used up in earlier tests. Files are closed. Families never test. And without relatives in a database, genetic genealogy can do nothing at all. There is also another side to this science that deserves to be said clearly. The people who uploaded their DNA to GEDmatch did so to learn about their own families. When police began using that data to search for suspects, it raised real questions about privacy and consent. A year later, in May 2019, GEDmatch changed its rules so that users had to actively opt in before their DNA could be used in law enforcement searches. The debate about where the line should be is still going on today. But in 2018, when the Bartlett Hall profile arrived at Parabon, the door was still open. Parabon's scientists produced a SNP profile from the evidence and uploaded it to GEDmatch. Then they waited for the database to answer. Somewhere in that database was a woman who had voluntarily tested her DNA and shared it on a genealogy website, the way hundreds of thousands of people had done, for reasons that had nothing to do with any crime. She was not a suspect. She was not a witness. She had done nothing wrong. She was simply a branch of a family tree. And her upload was about to answer a question that had been waiting for twenty-five years.

Every cold case that is finally solved has at least one person who refused to let it go. In this one, there were several, and two of them stand out. The first was Randel McPherron, known as Randy, an investigator with the Alaska State Troopers' cold case unit and the lead detective on the Sergie case in its final stretch. Cold case investigators do work that rarely makes headlines. They read files that are older than some of their colleagues. They track down witnesses who have moved, changed names, or died. They send old evidence to laboratories again and again, knowing that most of the time nothing will come back. McPherron was the investigator who carried the Sergie file into the age of genetic genealogy, and who would follow it all the way to a courtroom. The second was CeCe Moore, the chief genetic genealogist at Parabon NanoLabs. When the SNP profile built from the Bartlett Hall evidence was uploaded to GEDmatch, it was Moore and her team who studied the list of genetic relatives the database returned and began the slow work of building family trees. That work is less like a flash of insight and more like a jigsaw puzzle with thousands of pieces, assembled from birth announcements, marriage records, obituaries and public documents. Each match is a clue to a branch of the family. Each branch has to be followed backward to a common ancestor and then forward again to the living descendants. Genealogists sometimes spend weeks chasing a branch only to discover that it leads nowhere. In this case, the trail led to one particularly important match. A woman, reported to be living in Vermont, had uploaded her DNA to GEDmatch. According to the Associated Press, she had submitted it voluntarily. Her DNA showed that she was a close relative of the unknown man whose profile had been sitting in the Alaska evidence since 1993. Her family tree was built out. Her siblings, their children, the men of the right age. And as the tree came together, one branch began to stand out. According to the findings reported after the arrest, the person who fit was her nephew. His name was Steven Harris Downs. Now, finally, the name that this story has held back can be said. In April 1993, Steven Downs was eighteen years old. He was a freshman at the University of Alaska Fairbanks. And he lived in Bartlett Hall, the same building where Sophie Sergie was killed. When investigators reached that name, the pieces from twenty-five years of files suddenly fit together in a new way. Because Steven Downs was not a stranger to the case file. His roommate and best friend in 1993 was the same young man who had worked as a dormitory security guard, the one who had been fired for having a gun in the dorm, the one troopers had interviewed at his law office in Portland, Oregon, and whose DNA they had collected. And that roommate had told investigators that Steven Downs had owned a .22-caliber handgun when they were students. The bullet from the tub room was a .22. A genealogy lead. A dorm address. A roommate's memory of a .22. On their own, none of these things proved anything. Together, they pointed in one direction. What had happened to Steven Downs in the twenty-five years since Bartlett Hall. He had left Alaska and returned to Maine. And he had become a nurse. By 2019, he was forty-four years old and living in Auburn, a small city in southern Maine, across the Androscoggin River from its twin city, Lewiston. He was working in health care, a profession built on trust, on caring for people at their most vulnerable. Nothing in the public reporting suggests that anyone in his life in Maine had any idea that Alaska investigators were looking at him. Then and now. In 1993, an eighteen-year-old freshman in a dormitory in Fairbanks. In 2019, a forty-four-year-old nurse in a small city in Maine, thousands of miles away. Sophie Sergie, in that same span of time, had stayed twenty years old. But a genealogy lead is not evidence. It is only a direction. To move from a name to a charge, investigators needed the one thing the science had been built to provide, a direct comparison between Steven Downs's own DNA and the profile from the crime scene. And that meant going to Maine. Alaska investigators worked with the Maine State Police. On February 13 and 14, 2019, investigators searched Downs's home in Auburn. They also obtained a sample of his DNA. Reports differ on exactly how that sample was obtained, with one account saying it was collected during the investigation in Maine and another describing it as voluntarily provided. However it was collected, it was sent to the Maine State Police Crime Laboratory for analysis. The lab compared it with the profile from the evidence that had been taken from Sophie's body in April 1993. It matched. Think about the chain of people and places that had to line up for that single result. A young woman's body examined in Fairbanks in 1993. Evidence stored by the Alaska State Troopers for a quarter of a century. A DNA profile built in the late 1990s and entered into the national database in 2000. A woman in New England who decided to share her genetic data on a free genealogy site. A laboratory in Virginia. A genealogist who spent her days building family trees of strangers. A cold case detective in Alaska who saw that the name on that tree connected to a roommate already in the file. Police officers in Maine. A state crime laboratory in Augusta. Break any one of those links, and the answer never comes. How many other cases in Alaska are waiting for just one of those links to appear? Pause there. For almost twenty-six years, a profile had sat in a database, connected to no one. In a single comparison, it had a name. The same DNA that had been preserved in an evidence locker since Bill Clinton's first spring in office was now linked, according to investigators, to a nurse living in Maine. On February 15, 2019, Maine State Police, working with the Alaska State Troopers, arrested Steven Downs near the Auburn Fireside Inn, close to his home. He was charged with first-degree murder and sexual assault in the death of Sophie Sergie. At the moment of his arrest, he was legally presumed innocent, and he would remain so until a jury decided otherwise. In Alaska, the announcement came from the Department of Public Safety. Twenty-five years, nine months and twenty days after Sophie walked down a dormitory hallway to smoke a cigarette, investigators could finally say that they had arrested a suspect. The university's chancellor said the arrest was a step toward justice for Sophie and her family. News outlets across Alaska, across Maine, and across the country carried the story of the Alaska Native woman killed in a college bathroom, and of the nurse arrested more than a quarter-century later. In Pitkas Point and St. Mary's, the news reached a family that had waited nearly twenty-six years to hear it. And in Maine, investigators sat down with Steven Downs and began to ask him about a dormitory in Fairbanks and a young woman he said he had never met.

In early 2019, Maine and Alaska police interviewed Steven Downs three times, and the interviews were recorded. Almost three years later, a jury in Fairbanks would hear those recordings. In them, according to reporting from the trial, Downs denied knowing Sophie Sergie. He told investigators that he recalled the murder but that he never knew or saw anything to begin with. He said he recognized her face only from the posters that had been put up after she died. He denied ever going to the floor of Bartlett Hall where she was killed. He denied owning a gun during his freshman year. And at one point, confronted with what investigators were telling him, he said: there has to be a mistake. Those words would be played in a courtroom in front of the jurors who would decide his fate. Downs first fought extradition to Alaska. Then, in May 2019, he dropped that fight, and he was taken north to face the charges in the place where the crime had happened. In August 2019, he was arraigned in Fairbanks. He pleaded not guilty to first-degree murder and sexual assault. Bail was set at one million dollars. And on the line, by telephone, listening with the help of a Yup'ik language interpreter, was Sophie's mother, Elena Sergie. Think of that scene. A mother in western Alaska, more than twenty-six years after losing her daughter, following a court hearing in Fairbanks through an interpreter, as a man from Maine entered his plea. The case would take another two and a half years to come to trial. Downs was represented by James Howaniec, a defense attorney from Lewiston, Maine, the twin city of Auburn, who would fight the case hard at every stage. There were pretrial battles over the DNA evidence. Defense motions challenging that evidence were denied. In February 2021, the lead investigator, Randy McPherron, acknowledged that some of the information in sworn affidavits used to obtain search warrants in the case had been erroneous. It was an uncomfortable admission, and the defense pressed it. But the case went on. And then there was the pandemic. Courts across Alaska and the country slowed to a crawl as COVID-19 spread. At one point, both the defense and the prosecution jointly asked to delay the trial because of the pandemic. The judge denied that request. That decision would later become one of the grounds of Downs's appeal. A judge also ruled that the defense would be allowed to present evidence about three alternative suspects, other men whom the defense argued could have killed Sophie. None of them had ever been charged. On January 12, 2022, nearly twenty-nine years after Sophie's death, the trial of Steven Downs opened in Fairbanks before Superior Court Judge Thomas Temple. Prosecutors told the jury that DNA evidence and Downs's possession of a gun that could have been the murder weapon tied him to the scene. They told the jury about Sophie, about the orthodontic work she had been saving for, and about the friend she always stayed with in Bartlett Hall. That friend, Shirley Akelkok, was among the first witnesses. Nearly three decades after Sophie walked out of her room to smoke, Shirley stood before a jury and described her friend as a responsible student who was not a partier. Over the following weeks, former UAF students who had lived in Bartlett Hall in 1993 took the stand and described what they had heard and seen in the dormitory before Sophie was found. Downs's college girlfriend testified as well. Then came the roommate. Nicholas Dazer, the former security guard, now a lawyer, told the court that he and Steven Downs had been roommates and best friends at UAF in 1993. He said he could not imagine any connection between his friend and the crime. He recalled that Downs had owned an H&R .22 and a derringer. But when prosecutors showed him a photograph of the type of pistol the state said could have been the murder weapon, he said he remembered Downs's gun as having flat sides, not rounded like the one in the picture. Questioned further, he acknowledged that he might be misremembering. The defense seized on all of it. Howaniec told the jury that the roommate had cast suspicion on Downs years later to deflect it from himself, and that no one else had ever told investigators Downs had a gun at school. He argued that a handgun found at Downs's home in 2019 was not the gun that fired the bullet found at the scene. He argued that Downs's fingerprints were not found at the crime scene. And he pointed to other biological evidence from Sophie's body, hairs and saliva from a male, that did not match Downs. Those traces, the defense argued, showed that someone else was the killer. In early February 2022, on the thirteenth day of testimony, the jury heard the recordings of Downs's 2019 interviews, including the moment he said there has to be a mistake. The defense called its own witnesses and laid out its alternative suspect theories. Then the case went to the jury. On February 10, 2022, after deliberating for about two to three days, according to different reports, the twelve jurors returned their verdict. Steven Harris Downs, now forty-seven years old, was found guilty of first-degree murder and first-degree sexual assault in the death of Sophie Sergie. From that moment on, the legal language changes. He was no longer accused. He was convicted. Downs maintained his innocence. He asked for a new trial, and that request was denied. Sentencing came on Monday, September 26, 2022, in Fairbanks, with the decision reported widely the next day. Prosecutors asked for the maximum, ninety-nine years for murder plus eight years for sexual assault. They described Sophie to the court as a young vibrant woman, and spoke of the impact her death had had on her family and on the Fairbanks community. Her brother, Alexi Sergie, had planned to speak. He was on the phone from western Alaska before the hearing began, ready to tell the court what his sister's death had done to his family. But the call dropped before he could give his statement, and he did not rejoin the hearing, even after a recess. Friends of Sophie had also been prepared to testify, but the judge said that was not appropriate for that hearing. So in the end, after nearly three decades of waiting, the family's own voice was almost absent from the room where the final sentence was handed down. Judge Temple acknowledged that no sentence the court could impose would adequately restore Sophie's surviving family or her extended support network. Then he pronounced it. Sixty-seven years for murder. Eight years for sexual assault. Seventy-five years in all. According to one report, Downs would be eligible to apply for discretionary parole after twenty-five years. In practice, for a man in his late forties, it was a sentence measured against the rest of his life. One month later, on October 24, 2022, Downs filed an appeal of his convictions and his sentence with the Alaska Court of Appeals. Among other things, he argued that his constitutional rights had been violated when the judge refused the joint request to postpone the trial because of the pandemic, and he challenged rulings made at his sentencing. His trial lawyers asked to withdraw, saying he could not afford to pay for an appeal, and he sought representation from the state's public defender agency. At the time of this video, we could not find a published ruling on that appeal. Steven Downs remains convicted, and he continues to maintain that he did not kill Sophie Sergie. But for the first time in almost twenty-nine years, a jury had spoken her name in a verdict.

So what does justice look like after twenty-nine years? For Sophie Sergie's family, it did not look like a dramatic courtroom moment. It looked like a verdict read in Fairbanks while they were hundreds of miles away. It looked like a mother following a hearing through an interpreter. It looked like a brother's phone call dropping before he could say what he had waited decades to say. The Sergie family has largely kept its grief private, and that is their right. Much of what the public knows about how they felt comes from the people who stood beside them. When the jury returned its verdict, the Alaska Native Women's Resource Center, an organization that works to end violence against Native women, wrote that Sophie's family had been seeking justice for her for nearly twenty-nine years, and that they had never given up hope that someday it would come. Someday came. But it came late, and it came at a cost that no sentence can repay. Sophie's case did not exist in isolation. When Steven Downs was arrested in 2019, the news arrived in the middle of a growing national reckoning over missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls. Writers and advocates pointed to the Sergie case as a window into a much larger crisis. A publication covering the Arctic wrote that the breakthrough in her case shone a light on violence against Indigenous women across the North. That May, people gathered in Fairbanks for a rally for missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls. In Washington, Alaska's senior senator, Lisa Murkowski, helped push a bill named for Savanna LaFontaine-Greywind, a young Spirit Lake Nation woman murdered in North Dakota in 2017. Savanna's Act, along with a companion law called the Not Invisible Act, was signed into law in October 2020. The laws were designed to improve how federal, state and tribal agencies track, report and respond to cases of missing and murdered Native people, and to close some of the data gaps that had hidden the scale of the problem for so long. In 2022, a few weeks after the verdict, Alaska state legislators held a hearing on the issue. The Department of Public Safety told them that the state's missing persons database held about 1,300 cases going back to the 1960s, of which 203 were women and girls, and 55 were identified as Alaska Native. The department acknowledged that Alaska Native women are victimized at a higher rate than any other group in the state. At that same hearing, the conviction in Sophie's case came up. It was described not as proof that the system works, but as an exception, and not the rule, because too many cases remain unsolved. That phrase deserves to stay with us. An exception, and not the rule. Sophie's family got an answer. Many Alaska Native families never do. Their daughters, sisters and mothers remain names on a list, in files that no one has reopened, with evidence that may or may not still be sitting on a shelf somewhere, waiting. There are lessons in this case that reach beyond Alaska. The first is about evidence. Everything that happened in 2019 depended on what was done in 1993. A crime scene processed carefully. Biological samples collected and preserved even though no one could test them yet. A bullet kept even though the gun was never found. Every cold case solved by genetic genealogy rests on decisions made by people who had no idea that their work would matter decades later. The second lesson is about persistence. The investigators who inherited Sophie's file could have let it gather dust. Instead, they sent it to a lab in Virginia in 2018, months after a new technique had proven itself in California, and they followed it all the way to a courtroom. The third is about privacy and family. Sophie's killer was found because his aunt chose to share her DNA on a genealogy website. That choice did nothing wrong. But it reminds us that our DNA is not only our own. It carries information about everyone we are related to, and the rules about who can search it are still being written. There are also questions this story cannot answer. Downs has appealed, and at the time of this video we could not find a ruling from the Alaska Court of Appeals. He continues to say he is innocent. The jury heard the defense's arguments about other DNA and other suspects, and it reached its verdict. If the appeals court rules, that will be part of the record, and it will be reported with the same care. What we can say is that, according to the jury that heard the evidence, the man who killed Sophie Sergie was an eighteen-year-old freshman living in the same building, who walked away from that night, returned to Maine and went on to work there as a nurse, while her family waited in a village on the Yukon. Now let's go back to where we started. Shortly after midnight on Monday, April 26, 1993. A dormitory on a hill above Fairbanks. A cold spring night. A young woman visiting her friend decides to smoke a cigarette. Her friend tells her not to go outside into the cold, but to use the tub room down the hall, the one with the exhaust vents. It is a kind suggestion between friends, meant to keep her warm. Sophie walks down the hall. She never comes back. Remember who she was. She was a daughter of Elena Sergie. She was a sister to Alexi. She was a Yup'ik woman from Pitkas Point, a village of a little more than one hundred people near the place where the Andreafsky meets the Yukon. She was a student at the University of Alaska Fairbanks who had made the long journey from the delta to the interior. She was a responsible young woman who, in the words of her friend, was not a partier. She was saving her own money for orthodontic work so that she could go back to school. She was, in the words of the prosecutors who fought for her, a young vibrant woman. She was twenty years old, and she had her whole life in front of her. She was not the woman in the bathtub. She was Sophie. For almost twenty-six years, the evidence of what happened to her sat on a shelf, waiting for the world to learn how to read it. When it finally spoke, it spoke clearly. And it is worth asking one last question. How many other women in Alaska are still waiting for their evidence to speak? If there is one lesson from Sophie's story, it is this. Time does not erase the truth. Evidence that is kept, and people who refuse to forget, can bring an answer even after a quarter of a century.
