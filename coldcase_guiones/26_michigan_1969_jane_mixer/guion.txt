Jane would have been forty-three. Somewhere in those twenty years, the life she had been heading home to announce, a marriage and perhaps a family, simply never happened. In 1999, thirty years. Jane would have been fifty-three. And through all of those decades, the stockings and the other evidence from Denton Cemetery stayed where they had been put, in the care of the police, waiting for something that no one could have promised would ever come. Meanwhile, Jane's family carried her. In Muskegon, the Mixers lived with the loss. Jane's sister had a daughter, Maggie, born a few years after the murder. Maggie grew up knowing that she had an aunt who had been killed, and knowing, as most people did, that the killing was thought to be part of the Michigan Murders. As an adult, she turned that inheritance into writing. She worked for years on a book about her aunt, drawing on Jane's own writings and on the public record of the case. She called it Jane, A Murder. She wrote it as a book about a murder that would never be solved. She finished it believing that the story of who killed Jane Mixer was over, and that it had ended in silence. She was about to be proven wrong in the most dramatic way possible. Outside the family, the story of the Michigan Murders kept being told, and each retelling tended to repeat the same assumption. In 1976 a writer named Edward Keyes published a book called The Michigan Murders, a popular account of the killings that kept the series in the public imagination for a generation. Articles marked the anniversaries. Students at both universities heard the stories from older students, the way every campus passes down its legends. And in nearly every version, Jane Mixer appeared as one of the victims of the same man. The repetition gave the assumption the feel of a fact. A story told often enough stops sounding like a theory. It starts sounding like history. That is one of the quiet dangers of a famous case. The more famous it becomes, the harder it is for anyone to ask whether one of its pieces belongs somewhere else. And yet the evidence did not care about the story. It sat in storage, unchanged, holding exactly what it had held since the morning of March 21, 1969. While the family remembered, the science changed. And it changed in stages, each one making the evidence from Denton Cemetery a little more valuable. In the mid-1980s, a British scientist named Alec Jeffreys discovered that certain regions of human DNA vary so much from person to person that they could be used like a fingerprint. Within a few years, in England, DNA was used for the first time to solve a murder and to clear an innocent suspect. By the end of the 1980s, American courts were beginning to accept DNA evidence. Through the 1990s, the techniques improved dramatically. Scientists learned to copy tiny amounts of DNA millions of times, so that a sample far too small to analyze could be multiplied into one large enough to read. They learned to read a standard set of locations on the human genome, so that profiles from different laboratories could be compared with each other. And then came the step that would matter most of all to Jane Mixer's case. In October 1998, the FBI began operating its national DNA index, a computer system that allowed a DNA profile from a crime scene anywhere in the country to be searched against profiles collected from offenders across the country. Every state contributed. Michigan contributed. A profile that had no name in a local laboratory could now be compared, in seconds, against a growing national list of known individuals. There was one important limitation, and it is the key to everything that happens next. The database could only find people who were already in it. If the man who killed Jane Mixer had never been arrested or convicted of a crime that required a DNA sample, his profile would not be in the system, and the database would stay silent no matter how many times investigators searched it. DNA databases are not magic. They are lists. And a list only contains the names that have been written on it. So let us lay out the ledger, the account of time that this case ran up. Seventeen years passed between Jane's death and the first use of DNA to solve a murder anywhere in the world. Twenty-nine years passed between her death and the start of the FBI's national DNA index. Thirty-two years passed before her case was formally reopened for DNA testing. Thirty-three years passed before that testing produced usable DNA profiles. And thirty-five years passed before the database returned a name and anyone was arrested for her murder. Think about the man whose DNA was on those stockings during those decades. He was in his mid-twenties in 1969. By the time the national database came online in 1998, he was in his fifties. He had built a career in nursing. He had lived his life. And his profile was not in the database, because nothing in his record, at that point, had put it there. Then, at the very start of the new century, two things happened, in two different parts of the system, that no one connected at the time. The first was this. In 2001, according to later reporting, the man who would eventually be convicted of Jane's murder was arrested for forging prescriptions. It was a non-violent offense. It had nothing to do with anyone's death. But that case led to his DNA being collected and entered into the system. A routine step, in a minor case, completed by people who had never heard of Jane Mixer. The second was this. Also around 2001, the Michigan State Police took a fresh look at the Jane Mixer case. A detective re-examined the old evidence and decided to have the stockings from the scene tested for DNA. The investigation that had been effectively closed in the public mind for more than three decades was reopened, by people who did not assume that the old answer was the right one. A forgery case in one file. A murder case in another. Two pieces of paper in two different offices, each with a DNA sample attached. Neither office knew about the other. The database would know.

To understand what happened next, and to understand why this case is still argued about today, you need to understand how the evidence from Denton Cemetery was made to speak. So let us step out of the story for a few minutes and step into the laboratory. Every human cell carries a copy of that person's DNA, a long chemical code that is the same in every cell of the body and different from person to person, except in identical twins. Most of that code is shared by all human beings. That is why we all have hearts and lungs and hands. But scattered through it are short stretches where the code simply repeats itself, like a word typed over and over, and the number of repeats varies from one person to the next. One person might have a certain short sequence repeated nine times at one location and twelve times at another. Someone else might have it eleven times and fourteen times. Forensic scientists look at a standard set of these locations, more than a dozen of them, and write down the numbers. That list of numbers is a DNA profile. The method was proven in a landmark case in England in the 1980s. Two teenage girls had been murdered in villages in Leicestershire, in 1983 and 1986. A young man confessed to one of the killings, but DNA testing showed that he had not committed either of them. Police then asked thousands of local men to give blood samples, and eventually the evidence led them to a baker named Colin Pitchfork, who had persuaded a colleague to give a sample in his place. In 1988 he was convicted. That single investigation showed the world both sides of the new science at once. DNA could convict the guilty, and it could clear the innocent. Two unrelated people sharing the same numbers at every location is so unlikely that, in practice, a full match is treated as identifying a single person. Now think about the problem of a sample from 1969. Clothing kept in storage for more than thirty years does not hold much DNA. What it holds may be degraded, broken into fragments by time, heat and handling. In the 1980s, that would have been the end of the road. But by the late 1990s, laboratories could use a technique that works like a photocopier for DNA. It takes the tiny number of molecules present and copies the targeted regions again and again, doubling them with each cycle, until there is enough to read. A sample too small to see could produce a full profile. That same power is also its greatest weakness, and you need to remember this, because it is at the heart of this story. A process sensitive enough to read a few cells from a stocking is also sensitive enough to read a few stray cells that should not be there. A cell that drifts from one sample to another on a glove, on a tool, on a work surface, can be copied just as faithfully as a cell that was there from the beginning. The scientists call that contamination. It is the reason modern laboratories use protective clothing, separate rooms, separate equipment and strict procedures. It is also the reason that defense lawyers, in cases built on DNA, ask so many questions about what else was in the laboratory on the day the evidence was tested. There is a second idea you need, and it is the difference between kinds of DNA. Some DNA evidence comes from body fluids, such as blood. Some comes from the cells left behind when a person touches something, from skin, from sweat, from saliva. Investigators often call this touch DNA. It is harder to interpret, because people shed skin cells all the time, and because touch DNA can be transferred from one object to another. In Jane Mixer's case, according to reports from the time, the DNA that would become the center of the prosecution was not from blood. It was described as possibly coming from sweat, saliva or skin cells, left on the pantyhose she had been wearing. One analyst reported finding that same male profile in three separate samples taken from the pantyhose. And there was a second sample, from a small spot of blood on Jane's hand. That spot, too, was tested. It, too, produced a male profile. Here is how those profiles become names. Once a laboratory has developed a profile from crime scene evidence and it does not belong to the victim, it can be uploaded to the national DNA index. The system compares it against profiles taken from people who have been convicted of, and in some states arrested for, qualifying crimes. If the numbers line up, the system reports a hit. That hit is not yet proof. It is a lead. Laboratories then confirm it, usually by comparing the evidence to a fresh sample from the person named. But in a cold case with no other suspect, a database hit can turn a decades-old mystery into a single name overnight. So why did it finally work for Jane Mixer? Because three things had to be true at once. The evidence had to have been preserved, and it had been. The science had to be able to read it, and by the early 2000s, it could. And the person who left the DNA had to be in the database. That last condition is the one luck controls. Many cold cases yield a perfectly good DNA profile that sits in the system for years without a match, simply because the person who left it has never been required to give a sample. Jane's case could easily have been one of them. The man whose DNA was on her pantyhose had lived for more than three decades without his profile being in any database. Only his arrest for forging prescriptions in 2001 changed that. Without that minor case, the database might have stayed silent. And there is something else worth knowing about the testing of the Mixer evidence. It was carried out as part of a cold case analysis at a Michigan State Police laboratory in 2002. That same laboratory, in that same period, was also processing evidence from other cases, including recent ones. That is normal. Crime labs handle many cases at once. But it means that the history of every sample includes the history of the laboratory around it, and in Jane's case that history would become a matter of fierce dispute. The 2002 analysis did what everyone had hoped it would do. It produced profiles. And those profiles, searched against the national index, came back with names. Not one name. Two. One profile, the one from the pantyhose, matched a man who had been in his twenties and living in Ann Arbor in 1969. That was the kind of answer investigators had dreamed of. The other profile, the one from the blood on Jane's hand, matched a man who could not possibly have been there. In the spring of 1969, he had been four years old. The science had spoken, and it had said two things at once. One of them was very hard to believe. The question that would follow this case into court, and long after it, was whether you could believe the first without believing the second.

Every solved cold case has someone who decided not to accept the old answer. In Jane Mixer's case, that person was a Michigan State Police detective sergeant named Schroeder, whose name appears in different accounts with slightly different spellings. Around 2001, more than three decades after Denton Cemetery, he re-examined the old evidence in the case. He did not start from the assumption that John Norman Collins had killed Jane. He started from the evidence. And the evidence, he decided, should be tested. It is worth pausing on how unusual that decision was. By 2001, the Mixer case was not a priority for anyone outside her family. It was not new. It did not have a public campaign behind it. It was officially open but widely regarded as solved in all but name. Choosing to spend laboratory time and money on stockings from 1969, for a murder that most people attributed to a man already serving life, was the kind of decision that only gets made when someone believes the victim deserves a real answer rather than a convenient one. So the stockings went to the laboratory. The analysis was carried out in 2002 at a Michigan State Police crime lab. And it worked. Male DNA was found on the pantyhose. A male profile was also developed from the small spot of blood on Jane's hand. The profiles were entered into the national DNA index, and over time, both of them came back with a match. Here is the first name. John Ruelas. In early 2002, John Ruelas had killed his mother. As a result of that crime, his DNA had entered the system. And his profile matched the blood on Jane Mixer's hand. But in March 1969, John Ruelas was four years old. A four-year-old boy did not drive a law student away from a campus ride board. A four-year-old boy did not take her to a cemetery near Ypsilanti. Investigators ruled him out as a suspect, because there was nothing else they could reasonably do. The match was real in the sense that the numbers lined up. It was impossible in every other sense. Hold on to that, because we will return to it. Here is the second name. Gary Earl Leiterman. In 2004, he was sixty-two years old. He was a retired nurse. In 1969, according to reports at the time of his arrest, he had lived in Ann Arbor. He had been in his mid-twenties then. And no public account has ever shown that anyone looked at him in connection with Jane's death before the DNA spoke. Not in 1969. Not in the decades that followed. His profile was in the database for one reason only. In 2001, he had been arrested for forging prescriptions. That minor case, completely unrelated to any violent crime, had led to his DNA being collected. And now his DNA, according to the laboratory, was on the pantyhose of a law student who had been killed thirty-five years earlier. One analyst reported finding his profile in three separate samples from that pantyhose. It was not from blood. It was described as possibly coming from sweat, saliva or skin cells. Think about what investigators now had. A man who had been living in the same city as the victim at the time of her murder. A man of the right age to have answered a ride board notice. A man whose genetic profile, according to the lab, appeared on the very clothing that had been used to strangle her. And no prior link of any kind between him and the case. There was no confession waiting to be extracted, no witness who had kept a secret for decades, no tip from a relative. There was only the science, and the science pointed at him. What it did not do was explain itself. If Leiterman's DNA was on the pantyhose because he had handled them on the night of March 20, 1969, then the blood on Jane's hand, the blood that matched John Ruelas, had to have arrived some other way. If, on the other hand, something had gone wrong in the laboratory, something that had put a four-year-old's DNA onto 1969 evidence, then how could anyone be certain the same thing had not happened twice? Investigators and prosecutors weighed that question and decided to go forward. It is important to be fair to both sides of that dilemma, because reasonable people have landed on each side of it. From the point of view of investigators, the two matches were not equal. The Ruelas match was to a single small spot of blood, and it was flatly impossible as an explanation of the crime. The Leiterman match was found in more than one place on the pantyhose, the very garment at the center of how Jane died, and it belonged to a man who had lived in Ann Arbor at the time. To them, one result looked like an anomaly and the other looked like evidence. From the point of view of a skeptic, the matter was simpler and more troubling. The same testing had produced both results. One of them was known to be wrong about the crime. A laboratory that could put a child's DNA onto a 1969 crime scene, by whatever route, had shown that something in its process could fail. And a hit in a database, by itself, is a lead and not a verdict. That tension never went away. It is the tension at the center of this whole story. In late November 2004, on a Wednesday, Gary Earl Leiterman was arrested. He was charged with one count of murder in the death of Jane Louise Mixer. He was held without bond. The Associated Press carried the story across the country under a headline that captured the astonishing span of time involved: a man charged in the slaying of a woman thirty-five years ago. Consider the then and now. In March 1969, Jane Mixer was twenty-three, and Gary Leiterman was in his mid-twenties, living in Ann Arbor. In November 2004, Jane had been dead for thirty-five years, and Leiterman was sixty-two, a retired nurse. And in between, there was the four-year-old boy who had grown up to become a killer himself, and whose DNA had appeared, impossibly, on the hand of a woman who had died when he was a small child. Leiterman denied any involvement in Jane's death. He has continued to deny it. For the family, the news was overwhelming. For thirty-five years, they had lived with the assumption that Jane had been one of the victims of the Michigan Murders. Now they were being told that the man who killed her might not have been that killer at all, and that the person charged was someone none of them had ever heard of. For Maggie Nelson, who had just written a book about her aunt's murder in the belief that it would never be solved, the arrest turned everything upside down. She would go on to write a second book, this time about what followed. Within months, they would be sitting together in a courtroom in Ann Arbor, the city where Jane's last ride had begun. And the question of the four-year-old boy would be sitting there with them.

The trial of Gary Earl Leiterman began in July 2005, in Ann Arbor, in Washtenaw County, the county where Jane Mixer had lived and studied and where her last ride had begun. It had been thirty-six years since the morning at Denton Cemetery. The defendant was a sixty-two-year-old retired nurse. The victim's family sat in the courtroom. Among them were Jane's sister and her niece, Maggie Nelson, who had moved to Michigan for the summer to attend the trial, staying in a friend's house so they could be there for every day of it. Maggie would later turn that summer into a memoir, published in 2007, called The Red Parts. Much of what the wider world knows about the atmosphere of that courtroom comes from her account and from the news coverage of the time. The charge was first-degree murder. Under Michigan law, that is the most serious charge there is. In plain words, it generally means a killing that was premeditated, one that the killer had time to think about, even briefly, before he acted. To convict, a jury must be convinced beyond a reasonable doubt, which is the highest standard of proof in American law. It does not mean beyond all doubt. It means that the evidence leaves no doubt that a reasonable person would act on in the most important matters of their own life. That standard would matter enormously in this trial, because there was, in the middle of the evidence, a doubt that everyone could see. The only question was whether it was a reasonable one. In Michigan, there is no death penalty, and a conviction for first-degree murder carries a mandatory sentence of life in prison without the possibility of parole. Everyone in the room knew what was at stake. For a man of sixty-two, a guilty verdict meant he would almost certainly die in prison. For the family, a not guilty verdict would mean that Jane's case, after thirty-six years, would end in silence again. The prosecution's case rested on the DNA. The jury heard that a male DNA profile had been found on the pantyhose Jane was wearing, in three separate samples, and that it matched Gary Leiterman. The jury heard that the DNA was not from blood, and that it might have come from sweat, saliva or skin cells. And the jury heard that Leiterman had been living in Ann Arbor at the time of the murder. The prosecution also presented handwriting evidence. A police lieutenant testified about writing in a phone book connected to the case. He told the court that it was, in his words, highly probable that the writing was Leiterman's. But under cross-examination, he admitted that he had examined only photographs of the writing, not the original. And then there was John Ruelas. There was no way to keep the four-year-old out of the courtroom. Leiterman's attorney, Gary Gabry, built his case around a single argument: that the laboratory had made a mistake. If the lab could produce a match to a man who was four years old in 1969, then the lab could not be trusted to have produced the Leiterman match correctly either. The defense pointed out that the Ruelas case and the Mixer evidence had been handled in the same laboratory. A DNA analyst confirmed on the witness stand that the samples from the two cases had been tested on the same day. But the analyst, and other laboratory staff, maintained that the work had been done in separate parts of the lab, and that quality controls were in place that made a mix-up unlikely. The analyst denied that contamination of that kind was possible. The defense wanted to bring an expert to the stand to challenge that. His name was Dan Krane, a biology professor at Wright State University in Ohio, who had studied and written about how DNA evidence is interpreted. But the court allowed Krane only limited testimony, after finding that some of his methods were not generally accepted in the scientific community. It was a significant ruling. It meant that the jury heard less from the defense's chief scientific witness than the defense had wanted. How the blood that matched John Ruelas came to be on Jane Mixer's hand was never explained at the trial. The prosecution acknowledged that it was something that needed explaining. One account of the trial reports that the prosecution itself described it as a circumstance that needed to be explained. But no explanation was offered that showed how it happened. The prosecution's position was, in essence, that whatever had happened with the blood spot, it could not erase the evidence on the pantyhose. Ruelas was not a suspect. He could not have been. His DNA on Jane's hand was a puzzle, but it was a puzzle about a single drop of blood, not about the person who had killed her. The defense's position was the mirror image. The Ruelas result proved that something had gone wrong. And if something had gone wrong once, there was no reason to assume it could not have gone wrong in the testing that named Leiterman. Two ways of reading the same laboratory report. Two very different conclusions. The jury chose. In July 2005, after deliberating for less than three hours, the jury found Gary Earl Leiterman guilty of first-degree murder in the death of Jane Louise Mixer. Less than three hours, after thirty-six years. For the family, it was the end of a road that had begun with a father driving through the night in Muskegon. For Leiterman, it was the beginning of a life sentence. Later in 2005, he was sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole, the sentence Michigan law required. He did not admit to the crime. He has not admitted it since. From the moment of his conviction, he has maintained that he is innocent and that the laboratory got it wrong. It is worth stating plainly what the verdict means and what it does not. Under the law, Gary Earl Leiterman is the man convicted of murdering Jane Mixer. A jury of his peers heard the evidence, including the evidence about John Ruelas, and found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. That verdict stands. At the same time, the trial did not answer the question that had hung over it from the start. No one explained, in that courtroom, how a four-year-old's blood came to be on a dead woman's hand. The jury did not need that explanation to reach its verdict. But many people who followed the case, then and later, believed that it was the most important unanswered question of all. And John Norman Collins, the man so many had blamed for thirty-five years, never stood trial for Jane's death and was never charged with it. The courtroom in Ann Arbor had removed Jane's name from his story. Her death now had a different name attached to it. For Maggie Nelson, sitting in that courtroom through the summer, the experience was not the clean resolution that television promises. Her book about that summer would become a widely read account of what it means for a family to sit through a murder trial decades after the crime. The verdict was in. The doubt was not gone. And the debate about what really happened in that laboratory in 2002 was only beginning.

In November 2005, a few months after the verdict, the CBS News program 48 Hours broadcast the story of Jane Mixer's murder to a national audience under the title Deadly Ride. Correspondent Maureen Maher told the story of a young law student, a ride board, a cemetery and a fresh look at old evidence. For many Americans, it was the first time they had heard Jane's name. For people in Michigan who remembered the Michigan Murders, it was the first time they had heard that one of the victims on that famous list had been killed, according to a jury, by someone else entirely. Two years later, in 2007, Maggie Nelson published The Red Parts, her memoir of the reopened case and the summer of the trial. It was later republished under the title The Red Parts, Autobiography of a Trial. It is not a conventional true crime book. It is a book about grief that skips a generation, about what it means to inherit a murder, and about sitting in a courtroom while strangers discuss the most private facts of your family's worst day. Through it, Jane Mixer became something she had not been for decades. She became a person again, not just a number on a list. And then there is the doubt, which did not go away. Leiterman has continued to say that he is innocent, pointing to what he describes as irregularities at the crime lab where the Mixer evidence was analyzed. In 2016, more than ten years into his life sentence, his case drew attention again. A psychologist at the University of California, San Diego, named John Wixted, who studies how evidence can mislead, consulted six DNA experts about the Mixer case. He argued, in an article published by the Association for Psychological Science, that contaminated DNA evidence may have convicted the wrong man. His reasoning ran along the same line the defense had drawn in 2005. The Ruelas result showed that contamination had happened. A laboratory that had put a four-year-old's DNA on 1969 evidence, he argued, could not be assumed to have kept everything else clean. CBS News reported that Leiterman, by then more than ten years into his sentence, hoped to win a new trial. Here we have to be fair, the way this story deserves. On the other side stands a jury verdict, reached after a full trial in which the defense put the contamination argument squarely in front of the jurors. Leiterman's DNA was reported in three separate samples from the pantyhose, not just one. He was living in Ann Arbor at the time of the murder. A handwriting witness, whatever the limits of his method, testified that it was highly probable that writing connected to the case was his. The laboratory staff testified that the cases were handled in separate areas, under quality controls. The jurors heard all of it and convicted. As of the most recent public reports we were able to find, his conviction has not been overturned. And the question of how John Ruelas's blood reached Jane Mixer's hand has, as far as the public record shows, never been answered. Both of those things are true at the same time. That is the honest shape of this case. It was solved in a courtroom. It was not settled in every mind. And it has become something more than one family's story. Cases like this one are part of the reason that forensic laboratories today talk so much about contamination. Modern labs commonly keep DNA profiles of their own staff, so that a stray cell from an analyst can be recognized for what it is. They separate old evidence from new samples in space and in time. They run blank controls alongside real samples, to catch DNA that appears where none should be. They document every step, so that a defense lawyer, decades later, can see exactly what happened to a sample and when. None of those safeguards can travel back in time to a laboratory bench in 2002. But each of them is an answer to the question that the Ruelas match posed, which is how you can know that what a machine reads on a piece of evidence was really there from the beginning. For the families of victims, those safeguards matter as much as they matter for defendants. A verdict that rests on clean evidence is a verdict that lasts. A verdict that rests on evidence with an unexplained flaw will be argued over for as long as anyone remembers the case. Jane Mixer's family received a conviction. They also received that argument, and they did not ask for it. As for John Norman Collins, the man whose shadow fell across Jane's case for thirty-five years, he was never charged in her death. Reports describe him still serving his life sentence for the murder of Karen Sue Beineman, under the name John Norman Chapman, and still maintaining his innocence in that case. The list of seven that the newspapers kept in 1969 is, in the end, a list of seven young women, and not a list of one man's crimes proven in court. So let us go back to where this story began. Thursday, March 20, 1969. A twenty-three-year-old woman leaves the stone halls of the Law Quadrangle in Ann Arbor. She is a first-year law student at a time when very few women were. She is the daughter of a dentist in Muskegon. She has arranged a ride home through a notice on a campus board, the way students all over America did in those years. She is going home for spring break, and according to her family, she is going home to tell her parents that she is going to be married. In Muskegon, her father is waiting. By eleven o'clock he can wait no longer, and he goes out to look for her in the dark. The next morning, she is found inside a wire fence in a small cemetery two miles east of Ypsilanti. Thirty-five years later, a pair of nylon stockings, kept in police storage the whole time, gives up a name. And the same testing gives up another, the name of a man who was four years old the night she died. This was Jane Louise Mixer. A daughter. A sister. An aunt to a niece she would never meet, who grew up to tell her story to the world. A law student who chose a hard road and walked it on purpose. A young woman in love, carrying good news across the state. Not a number on a list. Not a footnote in someone else's case. A person. If there is a lesson in her story, it is this. Keep the evidence. So many solved cold cases begin with someone who refused to throw something away. But keep the doubt too. Science is the most powerful tool justice has ever had, and it is still run by human beings, in human laboratories, where mistakes can happen. The answer to that is not to trust the science less. It is to demand that every laboratory be careful enough, and open enough, that a verdict never has to stand next to an unexplained question. Jane Mixer deserved an answer without an asterisk. Her family waited thirty-five years for a name. They got one, and with it, a question that is still being asked. If this story stays with you, subscribe and share it.
