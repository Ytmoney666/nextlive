In the early hours of Tuesday, July 20, 1971, a group of young women came home to their apartment on Brookes Avenue in Burlington, Vermont, after an evening out. One of the women who lived there had not gone with them. Her name was Rita Curran. She was 24 years old, and during the school year she taught second grade in the small town of Milton, a short drive north of the city. That summer she was earning extra money as a chambermaid at a Burlington motel, and she was sharing this ground-floor apartment with friends. When her roommates walked into the bedroom, they found Rita on the floor. She was dead. She had been beaten, sexually assaulted and strangled. Beside her right arm, on the floor of that bedroom, lay something small and ordinary. A cigarette butt. A Lark, the kind of thing anyone might drop without a second thought. Rita's family would wait for an answer for more than half a century. Her parents would die without one. Her little brother would grow old. Her little sister would spend decades wondering whether anyone would ever say a name. In those years, a rumor grew around her murder, a rumor so famous that it threatened to swallow her story completely. Ted Bundy, one of the most notorious serial killers in American history, had been born in Burlington. And so, for decades, people whispered that maybe it was him. No evidence ever linked him to Rita Curran. Not a fingerprint. Not a witness. Not a single document placing him in Vermont that summer. But the whisper never quite went away. Meanwhile, the cigarette butt sat in storage. It waited through five decades, through new police chiefs and new detectives, through the invention of DNA testing, through the rise of national databases and then the rise of something stranger still, ordinary people sending their own saliva to genealogy companies to find distant cousins. On February 21, 2023, the Burlington Police Department called a press conference. Every murder investigation begins with a circle. At the center is the victim. Around her are the people closest to her, the people who lived with her, worked with her, loved her, or had reason to be angry with her. Beyond that is a wider ring, the people who were physically near her when she died. And beyond that is the vast, shapeless outer ring of everyone else, strangers, drifters, people passing through. Detectives work from the center outward, because experience teaches them that most victims are killed by someone they know or someone close by. In Rita Curran's case, the circle had a strange shape from the start. She had no known enemies. She was a schoolteacher, a summer chambermaid, an oldest sister. And the place where she died was not a dark road or a lonely park. It was her own bedroom, in a building full of other people. That meant the second ring, the people physically nearby, was unusually important. The roommates had been out together and came home together. Their whereabouts could be checked. That left the other tenants of the building. On the morning of July 20, 1971, police went to the third floor of the building and spoke to a married couple who lived there. The 2023 police report quotes the original 1971 notes. When officers contacted the couple, the woman stated that she did not hear or see a thing. She told the detective, a man identified in the report as Detective Winterbottom, that she did not know anything was going on until she saw the police cars and the ambulance. Her husband told police that he had been in the apartment with his wife all night. And his wife confirmed it. In police language, that is an alibi. It is the simplest kind there is. I was home. My wife was with me. We heard nothing. And it is also one of the hardest kinds to break, because it rests on two people saying the same thing, and one of them is the person who would know best. In 1971, there was no phone record to check, no camera footage to review, no digital trace of a man's movements on the stairs of an old house. There was only what people said. A detective faced with two calm adults telling the same story has very few tools to push against it. Unless something physical places the man at the scene, a fingerprint, a witness, a piece of his clothing, the story stands. A week later, on Wednesday, July 28, 1971, the husband's details appeared in an official police entry. William, age 31, 15 Brookes Avenue, Burlington, date of birth December 14, 1939, born in Chicago, Illinois. His surname is in that entry too, and we will come back to it. For now, it is enough to know that his name, his age and his address were in the Burlington police file from the first eight days of the investigation. And here is the line from the 2023 report that may be the most painful in the entire case. According to that report, the man on the third floor was never investigated as a person of interest or as a suspect. Not in 1971. Not in the years that followed. He was a neighbor with an alibi, and the alibi was accepted. Some reports say he had a criminal record. Even so, he was not looked at closely. Why does that happen? It is not hard to understand, even if it is hard to accept. A detective's time is limited, and every hour spent on one person is an hour not spent on another. When a person has an alibi, especially one confirmed by someone who lives with them, investigators move them to the side and spend their time on people whose stories have holes. That is a reasonable way to work, most of the time. It fails in exactly one situation. It fails when the person giving the alibi is lying. And in a case like Rita's, where there was no way to test the most important piece of evidence, a lie told calmly by two people on the first morning could carry the entire weight of the investigation. So the investigation moved outward. Detectives looked at other people. Over time, as the 2023 report would describe, many names were added to the pool of possible suspects in Rita Curran's murder. The details of those people have not been made public, and it would be wrong to name or describe anyone who was simply looked at and never charged. What is clear is that none of the leads produced an answer. No confession came. No witness stepped forward to say they had seen a man on the stairs. No fingerprint broke the case open. Each lead was followed, and each one, sooner or later, went quiet. Meanwhile, the man on the third floor did something that, in hindsight, says a great deal. He left. According to Burlington police, at some point after the murder he left Burlington and went to Thailand. There, as the acting police chief would put it in 2023, he moved to Thailand and became a Buddhist monk. A man who, according to police, had strangled a young schoolteacher in the building where he lived, became, by the police account, a Buddhist monk on the other side of the world, in a tradition whose very first precept is to refrain from taking life. The contrast is almost too stark to describe, and it is better left to stand on its own. The exact date he left Vermont is not in the public record. What is in the record is that he went, and that his wife's story did not change after he was gone. She kept the alibi. She kept it while he was in Thailand. She kept it for years afterward. Think about the cost of that, measured in time. Every month that the alibi held was a month in which the man who killed Rita was not in any detective's sights. Every year was a year in which Rita's parents grew older without an answer. In those early years, he was alive and within the reach of the law. A detective who had doubted the alibi, a wife who had decided to tell the truth, a witness who had noticed him leaving the apartment that night, any of those things could have changed everything. None of them happened. There is also a quieter cost, one that is easy to forget. Every year that passed made the case harder in ordinary ways too. Neighbors moved away. Roommates married and changed their names. Detectives retired and took their memories with them. Notes that made sense to the person who wrote them became harder to read for the people who came after. Cold cases do not freeze. They erode. By the end of 1971, the murder of Rita Curran was an open case without a suspect. By the middle of the decade, it was becoming something else, the kind of case that is still technically open but that no one is actively working, the kind that new detectives hear about as a story from before their time. A teacher, a bedroom, a cigarette in an evidence bag, and a file full of interviews that led nowhere. And then, as the 1970s went on, something happened far from Vermont that would change how the whole country talked about Rita Curran's murder. A young man born in Burlington in 1946 was about to become the most infamous killer in America. And when the world learned where he had been born, people in Vermont would begin to look at Rita's case in a completely different light. The wrong light.

On November 24, 1946, a baby boy was born in Burlington, Vermont, at the Elizabeth Lund Home for Unwed Mothers. His mother was young and unmarried, and in the America of the 1940s that was a source of deep shame. The boy would be raised for a time believing that his grandparents were his parents and that his mother was his older sister. He would grow up far from Vermont, mostly in Tacoma, Washington. His name was Theodore Robert Bundy. In the summer of 1971, when Rita Curran was killed, Ted Bundy was living in Seattle. He was a student at the University of Washington, and that year he volunteered at a crisis hotline in the city, where one of his fellow volunteers was a former police officer and future true crime writer named Ann Rule. Nobody yet knew what he was. That would change. By the middle of the 1970s, young women had begun disappearing in Washington, in Oregon, in Utah, in Colorado. Bundy was arrested in Utah in 1975, convicted of kidnapping, charged with murder in Colorado, and escaped from custody twice. In January 1978, after his second escape, he attacked women at a sorority house at Florida State University in Tallahassee, killing two of them, and weeks later he abducted and killed a twelve-year-old girl in Lake City, Florida. He was sentenced to death in Florida. Over the following decade, his name became shorthand for a certain kind of horror, the handsome, articulate young man who did not look like a monster. And once the world learned that Ted Bundy had been born in Burlington, Vermont, people in Burlington started to look back at their own unsolved cases. Rita Curran's murder was the most famous of them. The theory was simple, and it was seductive. Bundy was born in Burlington. A young woman had been strangled in Burlington in 1971, the same period in which Bundy was believed by some to have begun killing. According to one later account, the motel where Rita worked that summer stood near the old home for unwed mothers where Bundy had been born. To some writers and investigators, that was too much coincidence to ignore. A retired FBI agent named John Bassett later proposed Bundy as a suspect in Rita's murder, pointing to what he saw as similarities with known Bundy crime scenes. Over the years, the case was discussed in books, articles, podcasts and online forums, often under headlines that put Bundy's name ahead of Rita's. A local news report would later note that Bundy had at one point been looked at as a possible suspect. Here is what the theory never had. It never had evidence. No witness ever placed Ted Bundy in Vermont in July 1971. No fingerprint, no document, no record of travel, no physical evidence of any kind ever connected him to Brookes Avenue. On January 22, 1989, two days before he was executed in Florida, Bundy spoke at length with an FBI agent named William Hagmaier, and in those final conversations, according to published accounts, he denied any involvement in Rita Curran's murder. A Vermont newspaper would later sum up the Bundy angle in two words: a dead end. The rumor itself did a kind of harm that is easy to overlook. It turned Rita into a footnote in someone else's story. Online, the murder of a Vermont second-grade teacher was often listed as a possible Bundy victim, one name in a long list of maybes. People who had never heard of Milton Elementary School, or of the Curran family, had opinions about whether Ted Bundy had been in Burlington that summer. And while all of that attention was pointed at a famous man in a Florida prison, the real answer was still sitting quietly in a Burlington police file, under an ordinary name with an address on Brookes Avenue. Meanwhile, the years went by. Every July, the anniversary came and went. Rita's parents grew older. Her brother and sister built their own lives, with jobs and families and homes, and carried their sister with them. Mary would later describe often wondering whether the person who killed her sister would ever be identified. She spoke about the case over the years, including, according to a Vermont newspaper, with Senator Patrick Leahy, a former Chittenden County state's attorney who had become one of the longest-serving members of the United States Senate. Rita's parents did not live to hear an answer. And then, somewhere in all of those years, without anyone in Burlington knowing it, the clock ran out on any chance of a trial. In 1986, on the other side of the country, the man on the third floor died. We will come to the details. For now it is enough to say that, from that day on, there would never be an arrest, never be a courtroom, never be a jury. Whatever happened next, the law could no longer hold him to account. And no one in Vermont even knew it had happened. Now, consider the ledger, the cold accounting of what this case lost, and when. In July 1971, a man in the building was interviewed on the first morning and given an alibi by his wife. Eight days later, his name, age and birth date were recorded. According to the 2023 report, he was never investigated as a person of interest. Sometime after the murder, he left the country, and his wife kept his secret. In the late 1970s and 1980s, public attention drifted toward Ted Bundy, without evidence. In 1984, a British scientist named Alec Jeffreys discovered DNA fingerprinting. In 1986, the man on the third floor died, 15 years after the murder, and the possibility of a trial died with him. In the 1990s, American police labs began using DNA routinely, and the FBI built its national database, CODIS, which became fully operational in 1998. And still the cigarette in Rita's case sat untested. Not until 2014, according to accounts of the case, did Burlington police send the evidence from Rita's bedroom for DNA analysis, including the cigarette. That was 43 years after the murder. A DNA profile was developed. It was compared against the criminal databases. It matched no one. That result was both a disappointment and a clue. It meant the man who left that cigarette had never been entered into the system, either because he had never been convicted of a qualifying crime in the era of DNA collection, or because he was already gone by the time those databases existed. Investigators in 2014 now had a genetic fingerprint of the killer. They just had no one to compare it to. It was a key without a lock. Think of it like a fingerprint found at a scene in a town where no one has ever been fingerprinted. The print is perfect. It is also useless, until you find a way to compare it to the right person. For everyone still waiting on this case, 2014 meant something strange. The killer's genetic signature now existed, written out as a string of numbers in a laboratory report, and it still could not tell anyone his name. And for a few more years, it would stay that way. But the science was moving faster than anyone in Burlington realized. In California, in Texas, in Virginia, a handful of researchers and genealogists were about to show the world a new way to use a key like that. And the next time the DNA profile from Rita's bedroom left Vermont, it would come back with a family.

To understand how a cigarette from 1971 finally spoke, we have to step away from Burlington for a few minutes and tell a story inside the story. It is the story of how DNA went from a laboratory curiosity to the most powerful witness in American criminal justice, and how, in the space of just a few years, it learned to do something its inventors never imagined. Every person carries a set of instructions inside nearly every cell of their body. That set of instructions is DNA. Half of it comes from your mother and half from your father, and apart from identical twins, no two people carry exactly the same version. When you lick an envelope, or drink from a glass, or hold a cigarette between your lips, you leave behind a few cells from the inside of your mouth, carried in your saliva. Those cells contain your DNA. In 1984, at the University of Leicester in England, a scientist named Alec Jeffreys realized that certain regions of DNA vary so much from person to person that they could be used like a fingerprint. Within a few years, that discovery had been used to solve murders in England, and by the late 1980s and 1990s, American police laboratories were adopting it. The method that became standard compares a small number of those highly variable regions. If the pattern from a crime scene matches the pattern from a suspect, the odds that it came from someone else are astronomically small. But this method has one great weakness. It can only answer a question you already know how to ask. You need a suspect. You need a second sample. To solve that problem, the FBI built a national index of DNA profiles called CODIS, which became fully operational in 1998. Profiles from people convicted of certain crimes, and in many states people arrested for them, are stored in the system, and crime scene profiles are searched against them. CODIS has solved thousands of cases. But if the person who left the evidence has never been entered into that system, if he was never convicted in the DNA era, or if he died before it existed, CODIS will search and search and return nothing. That is exactly what happened in Rita Curran's case in 2014. The profile from the cigarette existed. The database had no one to match it to. For years, that was the end of the road for cases like hers. Then the road got longer. Starting in the 2000s and 2010s, millions of ordinary people began sending their own DNA to consumer companies, out of curiosity about their ancestry, or to find relatives they had never met. Those tests do not look at a handful of regions the way police tests do. They look at hundreds of thousands of tiny points across a person's DNA, called SNPs, a word that sounds technical but simply means single letters in the genetic code that differ from person to person. Comparing those hundreds of thousands of letters makes it possible to tell not just whether two samples come from the same person, but whether two people are related, and roughly how closely. Siblings share about half of their DNA. First cousins share about an eighth. Second and third cousins share less, but still enough to be detected. In 2010, a free website called GEDmatch opened, allowing people to upload the raw data from their consumer tests and compare it with others. It was built by genealogy hobbyists for adoptees and family historians. Nobody built it to catch killers. But in April 2018, investigators in California announced that they had used it to identify the man they called the Golden State Killer, responsible for a string of murders and rapes across California in the 1970s and 1980s. They had uploaded crime scene DNA, found distant cousins of the killer, built family trees back through generations and then forward again to the present, until the trees converged on one man of the right age, in the right places, at the right time. Then they collected a discarded sample from him and confirmed the match. That case changed everything. The method came to be called investigative genetic genealogy. Within months, other cases began to fall. One of the companies at the center of that wave was Parabon NanoLabs, based in Virginia, and its chief genetic genealogist was a woman named CeCe Moore. Moore had spent years helping adoptees find their birth parents using exactly these tools, and she became one of the most prominent figures in the new field, credited with helping to identify suspects in many cold cases across the country. The method has its limits and its controversies. Genealogy websites have changed their rules about what police may see, and many users now have to opt in before their DNA can be compared with crime scene evidence. But for cases like Rita Curran's, where a perfect DNA profile sits in a file with no name attached, investigative genetic genealogy offered something CODIS never could. It did not need the killer to be in any database. It only needed some of his relatives to be. Now think about what that meant for a cigarette left on a bedroom floor in 1971. The saliva on that filter had been drying for decades. DNA breaks down over time, through heat and moisture and simple age. That a usable profile could still be developed from it at all was remarkable. That it might be strong enough to search among the relatives of millions of people was more remarkable still. But it was possible. And there was something else. Even if genealogy could suggest a name, a careful investigation would need more. It would need confirmation that the name was right. In many cases, police do that by collecting something the suspect throws away, a coffee cup or a fork or a cigarette of his own. But a man who died decades ago leaves nothing to collect. In that situation, investigators turn to his relatives again, this time directly. If a living brother or half-brother agrees to give a DNA sample, scientists can calculate how likely it is that he and the person who left the evidence share a parent. It is the same arithmetic families use to confirm a long-lost sibling, turned toward a murder case. In 2019, Burlington police had a new team of detectives working on Rita Curran's murder. They had the 2014 profile. They had the file from 1971, with its interview notes and its list of names. And they had a growing pool of people, in the words of the later report, to compare DNA to, people who had been near the crime, or connected to it, or named in it over the years. The pool was large. What the detectives needed was a way to cut through it. In 2022, according to NPR's reporting, the Burlington Police Department contracted Parabon NanoLabs. The DNA from the cigarette, the same cigarette that had lain beside Rita's arm on July 20, 1971, was about to be compared with the family trees of strangers across the country. And somewhere in those trees, there were people who had no idea that they were distant relatives of a man who had once lived on the third floor of a building on Brookes Avenue.

The heroes of this part of the story are not famous. They are a handful of detectives in the Burlington Police Department, working a case that was older than most of them, and a genealogist in Virginia who had spent years learning to read family trees the way other people read maps. At the head of the Burlington detective bureau was Lieutenant James Trieb, the commander of the department's Detective Services Bureau. He and the detectives who renewed the investigation in 2019 did the slow, unglamorous work that cold cases demand. They went back through the 1971 file, page by page. They read interview notes written half a century earlier by officers who were long retired or gone. They looked for the people from that file who were still alive, people who had been young in 1971 and who were now old. They built a list of names to compare against the DNA. And later, Lieutenant Trieb would put all of it into a final report, 34 pages long, that would be released to the public on the day the case was closed. One sentence in that report explains how the man on the third floor finally came into focus. Because of his proximity to this crime, the report says, he was added to the suspect pool, along with many others, as a person to compare DNA to. Proximity. Not suspicion. Not a hunch. He was on the list because he had lived in the building. Then, in 2022, the DNA from the cigarette went to Parabon NanoLabs, and to CeCe Moore. What happened next happened fast. Moore and her team used what police described as open-source genealogy websites, the databases where ordinary people upload their own DNA and where users have agreed that it may be compared with law enforcement samples. The profile from the cigarette found matches among people who shared small amounts of DNA with the man who had left it. Distant cousins. Strangers to the case. People with no idea that their own curiosity about their ancestry was about to help close a murder from 1971. According to reporting on the police findings, the genealogy showed strong connections to the man's relatives on both sides of his family, his father's side and his mother's side. When you can trace both lines, the family trees begin to close in from two directions at once, like two search parties walking toward each other through a forest. And where the two lines met, there was one man who fit. He had been born in Chicago in December 1939. He would have been 31 in July 1971. And his name was already sitting in the Burlington police file, in an entry dated July 28, 1971, with an address on Brookes Avenue. His name was William R. DeRoos. Fifty-one years after he told police he had been home all night, his name came back from the genealogy as the one man who fit. Later, at the press conference that announced the result, CeCe Moore summed up the strange arithmetic of the whole case in a single sentence. This case is over 50 years old, she said, and it only took a few hours to narrow it down to William DeRoos. More than fifty years of waiting. A few hours of genealogy. That contrast is the heart of this story. The answer had never been far away. It had been two floors up, and then in a filing cabinet, and then in an evidence locker. What had been missing was not information. It was a way to connect the pieces. But a name from a family tree is not proof. It is a lead, a very strong one, and the detectives in Burlington knew they needed more before they could stand in front of Rita's family and say it out loud. The obvious problem was that William DeRoos was dead. He could not be interviewed. He could not be asked for a sample. There was no coffee cup to collect, no discarded fork. So the detectives went back to his family. They found a living half-brother, a man who shared one parent with William DeRoos. He agreed, voluntarily, to give a DNA sample. That decision deserves a moment of recognition. He was asked to help police confirm that his own half-brother had killed a young woman, and he said yes. When the scientists compared his DNA with the DNA from the cigarette, the analysis indicated, according to the police report, a 97 percent probability that the two were half-siblings. In plain words, the man who left the cigarette on Rita's bedroom floor almost certainly shared a parent with the man who had just given his DNA. Then there was the housecoat. Remember the torn garment collected from Rita's bedroom in 1971. Investigators were able to recover DNA from it as well, and according to Burlington police, the DNA on Rita's housecoat matched the DNA on the cigarette. That mattered enormously. A cigarette, on its own, might show that someone had been in a room. DNA on the victim's own torn clothing tied the same person to the violence itself. And then came the last piece, the one no laboratory could provide. William DeRoos's wife in 1971, the woman who had told Detective Winterbottom that she had not heard or seen a thing, was still alive. The detectives went to speak with her again. More than fifty years after she had backed her husband's alibi, she told them something different. According to Burlington police, she now said that on that July night, after the couple argued, her husband had left their apartment for a brief period, to cool down, and that he had been gone during the time when Rita's roommates were out. The acting chief of the Burlington Police Department, Jon Murad, would describe that moment with one of the most striking lines of the entire case. Five decades later, he said, she gave our detectives a different story: the truth. We do not know why she told the truth when she did. We do not know what she had believed, or feared, or told herself in all the years between. The public record does not include her reasons, and this story will not invent them. Police said that in 1971 DeRoos convinced his then-wife to provide the alibi. What we know is that the alibi which had protected William DeRoos from the first morning of the investigation finally collapsed, from the inside, long after the man it protected was gone. The public accounts do not lay out the exact order in which these final steps happened, the half-brother's sample, the housecoat, the interview with the former wife. What is clear is that by early 2023 they were all complete. Now every piece was in place. The cigarette beside Rita's arm. The DNA on her torn housecoat. A genealogy trail that ran through both sides of one man's family. A half-brother's DNA showing a 97 percent probability of shared parentage. And a wife who, after half a century, withdrew the alibi she had given. All of it pointed to the same man, the neighbor two floors above. There was only one thing left for the Burlington police to do. They had to tell the world. And they had to explain why William DeRoos would never stand trial.

To understand why there was no arrest in this story, we have to follow William DeRoos out of Burlington and across the rest of his life, as far as the public record allows. According to Burlington police, at some point after the murder of Rita Curran, DeRoos left Vermont and went to Thailand. There, the acting police chief would later say, he became a Buddhist monk. How long he stayed, and what his life there was like, has not been made public. What police said is that he eventually turned up in San Francisco, and that there he met another woman who became his wife. Some later reporting has described a pattern of violence in his later life, but those accounts have not been laid out in detail in the public record, and they will not be repeated here as fact. What is documented is how his life ended. On August 7, 1986, William DeRoos died in a hotel in San Francisco. The cause, according to reports of the police findings, was acute morphine poisoning, a drug overdose. He was 46 years old. It had been 15 years since the night on Brookes Avenue. He was never arrested for Rita Curran's murder. He was never charged. He never stood trial. He was never convicted. Under American law, a person who has not been convicted is presumed innocent, and a person who dies before any charge is filed can never be tried at all. That is the legal reality, and it is important to say it clearly. What exists in this case is not a verdict. It is the conclusion of a police investigation, reviewed by a prosecutor, built on DNA evidence and a withdrawn alibi. That is why, throughout this story, his role is described in the words the authorities themselves used. According to Burlington police, he was identified by DNA as the man who killed Rita Curran. And for more than 36 years after his death, no one in Burlington knew that the man they would eventually name was already gone. On Tuesday, February 21, 2023, the Burlington Police Department held a press conference. In the room were the acting chief of police, Jon Murad, the detective commander, Lieutenant James Trieb, the genetic genealogist CeCe Moore, and the chief prosecutor for Chittenden County, State's Attorney Sarah George. And in the room, too, were Rita's brother Tom Curran and her sister Mary Curran Campbell. Fifty-one years and seven months had passed since the early hours of July 20, 1971. Chief Murad spoke about Rita. He described her murder in words that stayed with many of the people who heard them. The random violence of her murder, he said, left a stain on our community and it devastated her family. Then the police laid out what the investigation had found. A neighbor. The same building. Two floors above. An alibi given by his wife on the first morning, and kept for five decades, until she gave detectives a different story. The cigarette. The housecoat. The genealogy. The half-brother. And Murad told the room what had become of the man after the murder, that he moved to Thailand and became a Buddhist monk, and that he had died in San Francisco in 1986. Lieutenant Trieb explained the work behind it, and his 34-page final report was released to the public. In news reports from that day, he was quoted as saying that the detective bureau and the chief were confident that William DeRoos was responsible for the aggravated murder of Rita Curran, and that because DeRoos had died of an overdose, he would never be held accountable for his actions in a court of law. The report itself noted that Rita and DeRoos did not appear to have known each other. As far as investigators could tell, she was not someone he had a relationship with, or a grudge against. She was the young woman who lived downstairs. CeCe Moore described the speed of the genealogy work, the few hours it had taken to narrow fifty years down to one name. And then State's Attorney Sarah George spoke about the legal side. Because DeRoos was dead, there would be no charges. But George had reviewed the evidence, and she said that she believed there was probable cause to charge him with aggravated murder in the first degree, had he been alive. In Vermont, aggravated murder is the most serious homicide offense on the books. Probable cause is the standard a prosecutor must meet to bring a charge, the level of evidence that would allow a case to begin. It is not a conviction. It is the threshold of a trial that, in this case, could never happen. After the press conference, George went further when she spoke with reporters. I would have felt perfectly comfortable going to trial, she said. There is something painful in that sentence. It is the voice of a prosecutor telling a family that the evidence was strong enough, and that the courtroom is closed anyway. There would be no jury to hear the case. No witness stand for the former wife. No cross-examination of the DNA analysts. No chance for Rita's brother and sister to stand before a judge and speak to the man who took her from them. No sentence. The only verdict available to the Curran family was the one written in a police report. And so the Burlington Police Department did the one thing left to it. It closed the case. The murder of Rita Curran, which had been open since July 1971, which had outlived the officers who first worked it, which had been tangled up for decades in the legend of Ted Bundy, was officially closed. That day, the story ran in newspapers and on television across the country, and many of those headlines used the word solved. The careful word, the one this story uses, is identified. Rita's killer was identified, according to Burlington police, by DNA. He was never tried, and he never answered for it. Tom Curran also spoke. He had been a young man of about 21 when his sister died. By that February he would have been in his seventies. And in front of the cameras, the police chief, the prosecutor and the reporters, he did not talk about the killer much at all. According to a local television report, he said that he did not think about the killer as much as he thought about Rita and his parents. And that he prays to his parents, and to Rita. Mary Curran Campbell spoke too. For her, as for her brother, the day was not really about William DeRoos. It was about a sister who had been gone for most of her life. And about the question she had carried for half a century, which had finally, and at great cost, been answered. Think about the shape of that answer. It did not come with handcuffs or a perp walk or a courtroom sketch. It came in a police department briefing room, in the form of a report and a series of statements, about a man who had been dead since the year the space shuttle Challenger exploded. It was the truth. It was not justice in the full sense of the word. And everyone in that room knew the difference.

After the cameras left, the case of Rita Curran became something it had never been before. It became a closed file. In Vermont, it had been one of the state's most talked-about unsolved murders for half a century, and it was described in local reporting as Burlington's oldest cold case. Now it had an ending, even if it was not the ending anyone would have chosen. On the Vermont State Police's public page about the case, Rita's murder is now listed as closed, with a neighbor, William DeRoos, identified as responsible, a man who had died before he was ever named. As of October 2026, there have been no further public developments. There will be no trial. There will be no appeal. The legal story is over. But some questions remain, and it is honest to name them. We do not know exactly how William DeRoos got into Rita's apartment that night. We do not know exactly when he left for Thailand, or how long he stayed. We do not know why his wife backed his alibi in 1971, or why, five decades later, she finally decided to tell detectives what had really happened. We do not know whether anyone in the 1970s ever doubted that alibi out loud. Those answers, if they exist, are in the private memories of people who have chosen not to share them, or in parts of the record that have not been made public. And there is one more question that can never be answered at all, the one that every family of every victim in a case like this carries. What would have happened if the truth had come out in 1971? Would a young teacher's family have had a trial, a verdict, a sentence, a sense that the system had worked? Would a man who, according to police, was capable of this have been stopped before he reached Thailand, or San Francisco? No one can know. What we do know is what changed. The Ted Bundy rumor, which had wrapped itself around Rita's murder for decades, finally lost its grip. Bundy had denied any role in the case shortly before his execution. Now the DNA had done what no theory ever could. It showed that the answer had nothing to do with a famous serial killer. It had to do with a neighbor. An ordinary man with an ordinary address, two floors up, whom almost nobody outside the original file had ever thought about. If there is a lesson in that, it is a hard one. The most dramatic explanation is not always the true one. The man who killed Rita Curran was not a legend. He was, according to Burlington police, the person who lived upstairs. The case also stands as a lesson about evidence, and about the people who keep it. In July 1971, someone looked at a cigarette butt on a bedroom floor and decided it mattered. For decades, the Burlington Police Department kept it, through years when nobody could do anything with it, through police chiefs and detectives who came and went. If that cigarette had been thrown away, or lost, or allowed to degrade beyond use, Rita's case would still be open today, and the Bundy rumor would still be the loudest voice in it. Instead, a small object did what fifty years of human memory could not. It told the truth. And the case is a lesson about alibis. A wife's word, on the first morning of a murder investigation, carried more weight than it deserved. It carried that weight for 51 years. In the end, that same woman was the one who took it back. A big sister to Tom and to Mary. A student at a small women's college in Burlington.
