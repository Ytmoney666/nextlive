And the judge said something at sentencing that, in hindsight, reads like a warning. Had Mr. Tapp not confessed, the judge said, there would have been no conviction in this case. Let that sentence sit for a moment. The court itself acknowledged that the entire conviction rested on the confession. Not on fingerprints. Not on DNA. Not on an eyewitness. On words, spoken by a twenty-year-old after weeks of questioning and five polygraph examinations, words he would spend the next two decades insisting were false. For the Dodge family, the verdict was supposed to be an ending. For Carol Dodge, it was the answer she had been waiting for since the day her family was called to the police station. She believed the police. She believed the jury. Why would she not? They were the professionals. They had a confession. A court had reviewed the evidence and found the young man guilty. And so the case of Angie Dodge was, officially, solved. Chris Tapp never accepted that. He later professed his innocence, and he never stopped. He said his confession had been false. He said he had never been in that apartment. For years, almost no one outside his own circle believed him. Prisons are full of people who say they did not do it, and courts are designed to treat a jury's verdict as final. A confession, once it is played for a jury, is one of the most powerful pieces of evidence in the legal system. Studies of wrongful convictions have shown again and again that jurors find it almost impossible to believe that anyone would admit to a murder they did not commit. That is precisely why a false confession is so dangerous. It does not just mislead the police. It can overpower everything else in the courtroom, including the science. Chris Tapp went to prison. He was in his early twenties. Outside, life moved on. The 1990s turned into the 2000s. Phones got smaller and then started fitting in pockets. The internet became faster. And in police laboratories, DNA science kept advancing. But in Idaho Falls, the official story did not change. The case was closed. And yet the evidence was still saying what it had always said. Somewhere out there was a man whose DNA had been in that apartment, a man who had never been named, never been charged, never been asked. If the police theory was correct, he was an accomplice who had escaped justice. If the police theory was wrong, he was the killer, and he was free. Either way, he was out there. And no one was looking for him with any urgency, because, officially, the killer was already behind bars. For years, the only people pushing against that version of events were Chris Tapp, his family and his lawyers. Then, in 2007, the Idaho Innocence Project at Boise State University agreed to take on his case. Innocence projects are organizations, often based at universities and law schools, that review cases where there is reason to believe an innocent person has been convicted. They often focus on cases where DNA evidence might prove innocence. Tapp's case seemed made for them. The physical evidence had never matched him. In 2008, the Idaho Innocence Project requested DNA testing on hairs recovered from Angie. The idea behind a request like that is simple. If the hairs and other biological evidence from the scene could be tested with more modern methods, the results might show conclusively that Tapp and Hobbs were not the men who were in that apartment. In 2012, Idaho passed a new law that gave defendants greater access to DNA testing after conviction, a law the Idaho Innocence Project and its allies had supported. That same year, on February 29, 2012, a court held an evidentiary hearing focused on Tapp's confession, and specifically on that January 29, 1997 interrogation. Among the people who took the stand was Tapp's former attorney. And among the others was Jared Fuhriman. By 2012, Jared Fuhriman was no longer a police officer. He was the mayor of Idaho Falls. Imagine that scene for a moment, using only the facts. A man who had spent fifteen years in prison, insisting his confession was false. And on the witness stand, defending the investigation that put him there, the elected mayor of the city. Despite the hearing, the courts did not set the conviction aside. In August 2012, a judge turned down the request for additional DNA testing. Later that year, limited testing was done anyway, and it excluded both Tapp and Hobbs as contributors to the DNA evidence. Once again, the DNA did not match. Here is the ledger, as it stood at the end of 2012. Sixteen years since Angie's murder. Fifteen years since the interrogations. Five polygraph tests before a confession. Three men named in the police theory, and none of them matched the DNA. One man in prison. One unknown man, the true source of the evidence, never identified. And a court system that, for now, was not willing to let the evidence speak. But outside the courtroom, someone had started to listen. And it was the last person anyone would have expected. Because the doubts that would finally break this case open did not begin with a detective or a judge. They began with the one person who had more reason than anyone to want the conviction to stand.

For years after the trial, Carol Dodge believed that the man who killed her daughter was in prison. That was what the police had told her. That was what a jury had decided. But Carol was not the kind of person who could leave a question alone, and the case had one question that never went away. If Chris Tapp was guilty, whose DNA was in Angie's apartment? The police answer was that it belonged to an accomplice who had never been identified. For a while, perhaps, that answer was enough. But as the years passed and no accomplice was ever found, it began to sound less like an explanation and more like an excuse. The public record does not pin down the exact moment when Carol's doubts turned into a conviction of her own. What it does show is where she ended up. She came to believe that Chris Tapp was not the man who killed her daughter. And eventually she stood alongside the Idaho Innocence Project and the New York based Innocence Project in supporting him. Think about how rare that is. A mother whose child was murdered, publicly siding with the man a court had convicted of the murder. It meant accepting that the answer she had lived with for years was wrong. It meant accepting that her daughter's killer was still free. And it meant going back to the beginning, alone if necessary, to find him. Meanwhile, the police had not entirely stopped looking for the source of the DNA either. Despite Tapp's conviction, Idaho Falls police continued to search for the unidentified man, the supposed accomplice. And in 2014, that search produced one of the most notorious false leads in the early history of genetic genealogy. Here is how it happened. There is a part of human DNA called the Y chromosome that passes, almost unchanged, from father to son, generation after generation. That means men in the same paternal line share very similar Y chromosome markers, often along with the same surname. Years earlier, a genealogy project sponsored by the Mormon church had collected DNA samples from volunteers through the Sorenson Molecular Genealogy Foundation, so that people could research their family histories. That database later came under the control of Ancestry. In 2014, investigators searched the Y chromosome profile from Angie's case against it, and found a close match. Thirty four out of thirty five markers matched a man named Michael Usry Senior, who lived outside Jackson, Mississippi. Ancestry was served with a court order to reveal his name. The father himself was excluded. But the near match suggested that a male relative might be the man police were looking for. Investigators turned to his son, Michael Usry Junior, a filmmaker in New Orleans, whose age and connections to Idaho seemed to fit. He was questioned, and he willingly gave detectives a sample of his DNA. About a month later, he was cleared. The DNA did not match. The Usry episode became a national cautionary tale about familial DNA searching. It showed how a near match on the paternal line could point at an entire family, and how innocent people could end up under suspicion simply because a relative once took a genealogy test for fun. It raised serious questions about privacy, and about what genealogy companies would do when police came asking. The same year, an independent review by the nonprofit group Judges for Justice concluded that Tapp's confession had been coerced and did not match the physical evidence at the crime scene. The pressure on the official story was building. In July 2016, the Bonneville County prosecutor, Daniel Clark, hired a private firm to investigate the Tapp case. In December of that year, he announced his conclusion. He had found no evidence that would reverse Tapp's conviction. The official story still stood. Then, in March 2017, something changed for Chris Tapp. Under an agreement negotiated by his lawyer, John Thomas, with the prosecution, his rape conviction was vacated. He was released on time already served for the murder conviction, which remained on his record, and as part of the deal he could no longer appeal. On Wednesday, March 22, 2017, at 11 in the morning, Christopher Tapp walked out of prison. He had been locked up for about twenty years. He had been questioned at twenty. He came out at about forty. And he came out still, in the eyes of the law, a convicted murderer. He was free. But he was not cleared. He could rent an apartment, find work, see his family. But every background check, every form that asked about criminal history, would still tell the world that he had been convicted of murdering Angie Dodge. The only way to change that was to find the man who actually did it. That same year, Idaho Falls police turned to a company called Parabon NanoLabs. Parabon offered something called DNA phenotyping. In simple terms, it uses DNA to predict what a person might look like. Ancestry. Eye color. Hair color. Skin color. Freckling. Even the general shape of the face. Parabon produced what it calls a Snapshot composite, an image of what the unknown man might have looked like at twenty five years old with an average build. Those two settings, the age and the body weight, were simply defaults, because DNA cannot tell you how old someone is or how much they weigh. On July 12, 2017, Idaho Falls police announced the results of another Parabon analysis. Using kinship testing that looked at both sides of the Usry family, not just the paternal line, the company reported that it was 87.63 percent confident that the unknown DNA from the crime scene did not belong to anyone in the Usry family. The family that had lived under suspicion for three years was formally cleared. And the man behind the DNA was still a stranger. But by then, a new kind of science was about to change everything. It worked with a different type of DNA marker, called SNPs, hundreds of thousands of tiny single-letter variations scattered across a person's genetic code. Consumer genealogy companies read these markers to tell their customers who their relatives are. The more markers two people share, the more closely related they are. And on a free website called GEDmatch, which had launched in 2010, people could upload their genealogy results and look for relatives across companies. In April 2018, investigators in California arrested a man they had identified as the Golden State Killer, after using GEDmatch to find his distant relatives and then building their family trees until the branches pointed to one man. The case made headlines around the world. It proved that a killer who had never been in any police database could be found through the DNA of cousins he might never have met. The question was whether it could work in Idaho Falls. And there was a serious problem. The DNA from Angie's apartment was more than twenty years old. It had been tested again and again. Whatever was left was degraded. To a genealogist, it was like trying to read a book with a third of the pages torn out. Most experts would have said it was not good enough to try. Most experts had not met Carol Dodge.

In November 2018, Carol Dodge reached out to a genetic genealogist named CeCe Moore. Moore worked with Parabon NanoLabs, and she had become one of the best known figures in the new field of investigative genetic genealogy. According to reports, Carol did not simply ask. She urged Moore to take the case. And she kept pushing. The problem was the sample. According to The Marshall Project, the DNA profile Parabon could extract had only about sixty one percent of the information analysts typically need. One account puts it in starker terms. Of the roughly eight hundred and fifty thousand markers that are typically read, about three hundred and twenty nine thousand were missing. Moore later said that genetic genealogy had never been done in a suspect case with a sample that degraded. Analysts at Parabon were hesitant. But at Carol Dodge's urging, Moore tried anyway. This is the part of the story that deserves to be told slowly, because it is where an ordinary laboratory file began to turn into a name. The team uploaded the profile they had to GEDmatch. As in almost every case of this kind, the man himself was not there. What they found were partial matches, people who shared small stretches of DNA with the unknown man. Distant cousins. People who had uploaded their own DNA to learn about their ancestors, with no idea that their results would one day help solve a murder in Idaho. From those partial matches, Moore and her team began to build family trees, working backward through birth records, marriage records, census entries and obituaries, generation by generation, looking for the place where the trees of different matches came together. They drew up three family trees. And those three trees converged on a single couple, a husband and wife who appeared to be the great grandparents of the man they were looking for. That was the breakthrough, but it was only the beginning. A couple who lived generations ago can have dozens or even hundreds of descendants. The team had to follow every branch forward in time, through children and grandchildren and great grandchildren, looking for men who were the right age, who had been in the right place, and who fit what the DNA predicted about the man's appearance. They narrowed the list down to about six men. Then the police went to work. Investigators followed a handful of those men and collected items they threw away in public, the kind of everyday objects that carry traces of a person's DNA. Each one was tested. None of them matched. For a moment it looked as if even this new science might fail. And then CeCe Moore noticed something in the family tree that changed everything. One woman in the family had conceived a son shortly before she divorced her husband. The boy was raised with the surname of her new husband. On paper, he did not look like part of the family at all. This is one of the hidden traps of genealogy. Family trees are built from records, and records follow names. A child raised under a different surname can vanish from the obvious branches of a tree even though his DNA belongs to them completely. The genealogists had followed the names. The DNA was following blood. His surname did not match any of the lines the genealogists were following. That is why he had never appeared on anyone's radar. Accounts differ slightly on exactly how the final link was confirmed. Some describe an obituary that provided the missing name. Others describe the obituary differently. But they all agree on where the trail ended. There were six men on the list. There turned out to be a seventh. His name was Brian Leigh Dripps. And when investigators looked at where he had been living in 1996, they found something remarkable. Brian Dripps had lived across the street from Angie Dodge. Directly across I Street. The man whose DNA had been sitting in an evidence file for more than twenty years had been her neighbor. During the original investigation, the police had built a theory around a group of young men from around town and an accomplice no one could find. And according to the reporting, Dripps had never been asked to give a DNA sample. One account notes that Parabon's prediction of the unknown man's appearance, Northern European ancestry, fair skin, brown eyes, brown hair, fit him. But a prediction is not proof. A family tree can say where to look. It cannot convict anyone. Before the police could act, they needed something far more certain than a genealogist's reasoning. They needed his DNA, collected legally, and compared directly against the evidence from 1996. In the United States, police can generally collect an object that a person has thrown away in public, because the law treats it as abandoned. A coffee cup left on a table. A napkin in a trash can. A cigarette butt on the pavement. That is the method investigators had already used on the six men before him, and it was the method they would use now. By 2019, Brian Dripps was living in Caldwell, Idaho, in Canyon County in the southwestern part of the state. Detectives began following him. And within the first hour of surveillance, he did something countless smokers do every day without thinking. He flicked a cigarette butt out of the window of his vehicle. It was exactly what investigators needed. And they lost it. In traffic, the butt was gone before anyone could collect it. So they kept watching. Hour after hour. Every stop, every turn, every time a window rolled down, someone had to be ready. One lost cigarette had already shown how easily the chance could slip away. So they kept watching. According to the Post Register, more than twenty hours of constant surveillance passed before Dripps did it again. Another cigarette butt, tossed out of the window of his vehicle. This time, investigators recovered it. The cigarette was sent to the Idaho State Police forensic laboratory, where the DNA on it was compared with the profile from the evidence collected in Angie's apartment in 1996. Pause here for a moment, because this is the place where twenty three years of this story come together. A teenager tutoring children in Idaho Falls. A first apartment on I Street. A Thursday morning when she did not come to work. A young man told he might face execution. A jury deliberating for thirteen hours. A filmmaker in New Orleans questioned for a crime he knew nothing about. A mother who changed her mind and refused to stop. A sample most experts considered too damaged to use. Three family trees. One divorce. A different surname. And now, a cigarette butt tossed from a car window. The laboratory ran the comparison. The DNA matched. For the first time since June 13, 1996, the DNA from that apartment had a name. It had taken the persistence of a mother, the skill of a genealogist, the patience of detectives in a surveillance car, and a sample most people had written off. The question now was what Brian Dripps would say when the police knocked on his door.

On May 15, 2019, police arrested Brian Leigh Dripps in Caldwell, Idaho. He was 53 years old. By the ages reported in court, he would have been about thirty on the night he crossed I Street. Angie had been eighteen. Nearly 23 years had passed, almost to the month. In that time, Angie's family had buried her, sat through a trial, watched a young man go to prison, begun to doubt that verdict, and fought to have it overturned. Brian Dripps, meanwhile, had gone on living. By 2019 he was living on the other side of the state, in Caldwell. He had a family. His own lawyer would soon describe him to a judge as a kind, loving father. And until that spring, according to the reporting, no one in law enforcement had ever asked him for a DNA sample. Detectives sat down with him. According to court documents, Dripps at first denied ever being in Angie's apartment. He denied having anything to do with her. Then the detectives showed him the test results. His DNA, from the cigarette he had thrown from his car, matched the DNA left at the scene in 1996. Confronted with that, his account changed. According to a probable cause affidavit written by Idaho Falls Detective Sage Albright, Dripps admitted that he had killed Angie Dodge. He said that he had entered her apartment by himself, carrying a knife, with the intent to rape her. He said that he did not mean to kill her. He said he had worn gloves. And he said he believed she was still alive when he left. It is important to be careful with those words. They are his account, given to detectives after he had been confronted with scientific proof. Prosecutors would later make clear that they did not accept his attempt to minimize what he had done. But one part of what he said mattered enormously to another man. Dripps said he had acted alone. There was no group of young men. There was no accomplice. There was no Ben Hobbs, no Jeremy Sargis, no unidentified third man, and there was no Chris Tapp. For twenty two years, the police theory had required all of those people to explain a single DNA profile. Now the owner of that profile was saying what the evidence had said from the beginning. One man had done this. In the days after the arrest, Idaho Falls police were not ready to comment on what it meant for Christopher Tapp's conviction. But the facts were moving faster than any official statement. Dripps was charged with first degree murder and rape. He was arraigned by video and denied bail. Prosecutors announced that they would seek the death penalty, the same punishment that had been used, more than two decades earlier, to frighten a twenty-year-old into confessing to a crime he did not commit. And then, two months after the arrest, came the day Chris Tapp had waited for since 1997. On July 16, 2019, The Marshall Project reported that in what appeared to be a first, genetic genealogy had helped clear a wrongful conviction. The next day, July 17, 2019, the Bonneville County prosecutor, Daniel Clark, the same prosecutor who had reviewed the case in 2016 and found no reason to reverse it, filed court documents to clear Christopher Tapp of the 1996 rape and murder of Angie Dodge. In that filing, he wrote that there was clear and convincing evidence that Tapp had been convicted of a crime he did not commit. A judge agreed. That same day, Christopher Tapp was officially exonerated. The headline in the local newspaper said it simply. After 23 years, Christopher Tapp cleared of all charges. The Innocence Project would later describe him as the first person in the world to be proven innocent with genetic genealogy. Think about how that day must have looked from the outside. The man who had been convicted of killing Angie Dodge, standing in court to be declared innocent. And among the people who had fought for that moment was Angie's own mother. The legal case against Brian Dripps moved more slowly. In November 2019, his trial was scheduled for 2021, and his defense said it intended to ask for a change of venue, to move the trial away from eastern Idaho, where the case was so well known. In August 2020, during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, his defense asked that he be released from jail because of the virus. He remained in custody. Then, on February 9, 2021, Brian Dripps stood before District Judge Joel Tingey in Idaho Falls and pleaded guilty to first degree murder and rape. Under the plea agreement, he would serve a fixed term of twenty years in prison, with an indeterminate sentence of up to life. The deal took the death penalty off the table. In court, Dripps spoke. When I went over, he said, I went with the intent to just rape her. I didn't mean to murder her. When the judge asked whether he remembered cutting Angie's throat, he answered, No sir. But he did not deny that he had done it. The prosecutor at the hearing, Deputy Attorney General Jessica Kuehn, pointed out that Dripps had lived 23 years knowing he had killed Angie Dodge, and had never come forward. Twenty three years in which he could have spoken. Twenty three years in which an innocent man sat in prison, then lived as a convicted murderer outside it. The local newspaper noted something else that was hard to ignore. The sentence Dripps had agreed to was lower than the one Chris Tapp had received in 1998 for a crime he did not commit. On June 8, 2021, the family gathered in the Bonneville County courthouse in Idaho Falls for the sentencing. Not everyone in the family supported the plea deal. Roger and Todd Dodge asked Judge Tingey to reject it. They said they wanted Dripps to face a jury. The judge accepted the agreement. Brian Leigh Dripps, now 55, was sentenced to a minimum of twenty years and up to life in prison. He received credit for about two years already served. He was ordered to register as a sex offender and to pay a total of ten thousand dollars in fines. Both the prosecution and the defense told the court that because of Dripps's poor health, twenty years was effectively a life sentence, and that he would likely die before he was ever eligible for parole. When Dripps addressed the family, he said, I know you'll never forgive me, but I'm sorry. He said he wished he could have a do-over of that night. Judge Tingey spoke directly about the time it had taken to reach that courtroom. Twenty five years, he said, is a long time to wait for some type of closure on such a brutal crime. He said it was impossible to quantify how much damage had been caused, and that it had spread wide. And then he turned to the other victim of this case. A young man, the judge told Dripps, spent a significant portion of his life in prison for no good reason. He was innocent. That falls on you. But the words most people remember from that day came from Angie's family.

Carol Dodge addressed that courtroom twenty five years after the morning her daughter did not come to work. She had waited for this moment through one wrong conviction, a false familial match, a release without exoneration, and a genealogy search that almost no one thought could work. We had to go through 25 years of hell, she said. Whatever you give this man is not enough. You have shattered our family. And, according to the Post Register, she told him that he deserved eternal hell. KTVB reported her telling him, I can't forgive you, ever. Angie's brother Todd had counted the time in a different way. Because of Mr. Dripps' premeditated actions, he said, he sentenced me to a lifetime of hell. And so far, I have served 9,126 days. Look at that number closely. From June 13, 1996, to June 8, 2021, the day of the sentencing, is exactly 9,126 days. Todd Dodge had not rounded. He had counted every single one. Afterward, his brother Brent was asked whether the sentence brought closure. I wouldn't say it's closure, he said, because as Todd described there is still a lot of chaos. And there was. Because a sentence for Brian Dripps did not undo what had happened to everyone else in this story. In October 2020, Chris Tapp had sued the city of Idaho Falls and several former officers of its police department. On June 9, 2022, the Idaho Falls City Council unanimously approved a settlement. The city, through its insurer, would pay Christopher Tapp eleven point seven million dollars. It agreed to send him an official letter of apology. It agreed to host a public discussion with experts on interrogation techniques. And the mayor apologized. Twenty five years after a twenty-year-old was told in an Idaho Falls interrogation room that he might face the death penalty, the city of Idaho Falls apologized to him. Chris Tapp did not simply take the money and disappear. According to the Innocence Project, he joined forces with its policy team, the Idaho Innocence Project and other advocates to lobby for a law to compensate the wrongfully convicted in Idaho, and that law passed. He testified before legislative committees in several states against deceptive police interrogation tactics. The young man who had once been pushed into a false confession spent his freedom trying to make sure it would not happen to anyone else. And then the story took one last turn that no one could have imagined. On October 29, 2023, Chris Tapp was injured during a party in a suite at the Resorts World hotel in Las Vegas. At first, it was described as an accident. He was taken to a hospital, and on November 5, 2023, Christopher Tapp died. He was 47 years old. The Clark County coroner later ruled his death a homicide, caused by blunt force trauma to the head. In March 2024, Las Vegas police obtained a warrant charging a former professional wrestler and former Nevada congressional candidate, Daniel Rodimer, then 45, with murder in Tapp's death. Rodimer has pleaded not guilty. His attorneys have argued that the coroner's findings show Tapp's injuries resulted from drug and alcohol use and were not suspicious. In July 2026, a judge denied his motion to dismiss the indictment, and his trial is reported to be set for February 1, 2027. He has not been convicted, and he is presumed innocent. Tapp's mother and his estate have also filed a civil wrongful death lawsuit. The man who was the first person in the world to be exonerated with genetic genealogy lived as a fully free man for just over four years. Jared Fuhriman, the officer who became mayor, later died at 60 after a long battle with Alzheimer's disease. Brian Dripps remains in prison. As of the most recent reports available, no appeal of his conviction has been reported. And Carol Dodge kept going. She said Dripps would never have been arrested without the advances in DNA technology and genetic genealogy, and with her son Brent she began asking people to give to 5 for HOPE, to help cold case foundations and under-funded police departments find answers for other families. Her story has been told on Dateline, on ABC's 20/20, and in podcasts, and through all of it she has kept her daughter at the centre of the story. Some questions in this case may never be fully answered. Why did a DNA result that excluded every suspect not stop the case against Chris Tapp in 1998? Why did the police theory keep adding new, unnamed men instead of going back to the evidence? Why was the man living directly across the street from the victim never asked for a sample during the original investigation? The public record does not give a complete answer to any of those questions, and this channel will not invent one. What the record does show is the cost. Ben Hobbs and Jeremy Sargis lived for years with their names attached to a murder they were never charged with. And a family waited a quarter of a century. Consider the then and now of it. When Angie died, she was eighteen. On the day her killer was sentenced, she would have been forty three. When he was finally arrested, he was 53. Those numbers are the real ledger of this case. And through all of it, the evidence never changed. It said the same thing on June 13, 1996, that it said on May 15, 2019. One man. One profile. The DNA did not match anyone else, because there was never anyone else. So let's go back to the beginning. A Thursday morning in June 1996. A quarter past eleven. A small apartment on I Street in Idaho Falls. Coworkers who noticed that a reliable young woman had not come in, and who went to check on her.
