On Saturday, January 4, 1964, a nineteen-year-old named Mary Sullivan was inside a small apartment on Charles Street, on Beacon Hill, in Boston, Massachusetts. She had grown up on Cape Cod. She had only just arrived in the city. The apartment was new to her, the street was new to her, and the year was only four days old. By the end of that day, Mary was dead. She had been sexually assaulted and strangled inside the apartment she had barely begun to call home. Her death would be counted as the last in a series of killings that had held Boston in fear for a year and a half, a series the newspapers had already given a name. The Boston Strangler. Whoever killed Mary left something behind. Not a name. Not a witness. Not a fingerprint that anyone could use. Biological evidence, on her body and on a blanket in the room. In 1964, that evidence could not speak. Within two years, a man would confess, in long and vivid detail, to being the Boston Strangler. He would confess to killing Mary Sullivan. Nine years after Mary died, he was stabbed to death in a prison cell block, and the question of whether he had told the truth was buried with him. For decades, people argued. Detectives argued. In late October 1964, a young woman in the Boston area was attacked in her home by an intruder. She was sexually assaulted. On paper, he was not the kind of man people imagined when they pictured the Boston Strangler. Albert DeSalvo was born on September 3, 1931, in Chelsea, Massachusetts, a working-class city just across the harbor from Boston. He had served in the United States Army. He was married. He had two children. But DeSalvo already had a police record, and it was a disturbing one. In the early 1960s, before the strangling series began, he had been arrested in connection with a string of incidents in which a man went door to door, claiming to be from a modeling agency, and talked his way into women's apartments with the excuse of taking their measurements. The press called that man the Measuring Man. DeSalvo was convicted on lesser charges connected to those incidents and served a short sentence. He was out again by the time the Boston Strangler killings were under way. After his arrest in the autumn of 1964, investigators came to believe that DeSalvo was responsible for a much larger series of sexual attacks across New England. In those attacks, according to later accounts, the man wore green work clothes, the kind a handyman or a maintenance worker might wear, and the newspapers called him the Green Man. He would be charged in connection with those attacks. At that point, he was not charged with any murder. And then the story took a turn that still shapes everything about this case. DeSalvo was sent for psychiatric observation to Bridgewater State Hospital, a state institution south of Boston that held men who had been accused or convicted of crimes and whose mental condition was in question. There he met another inmate, a man named George Nassar, who was himself facing a murder charge in a separate case. According to accounts of what happened next, DeSalvo told Nassar that he was the Boston Strangler. Nassar told his own lawyer. His lawyer was a young, aggressive and increasingly famous defense attorney named F. Lee Bailey. Bailey went to see DeSalvo, and he agreed to represent him. In 1965, Albert DeSalvo confessed. He confessed to being the Boston Strangler. He confessed, according to accounts, in detail to each of the eleven killings on the official list, and to two more that were not on it. He confessed to the killing of Mary Sullivan. His statements were recorded over long sessions with his lawyer and with investigators from the Attorney General's office. Some of the people who heard them were convinced. They said he knew things about the crime scenes that had never been published, details that only someone who had been there could know. For many in law enforcement, and for most of the public, that settled it. The Boston Strangler had a name. But not everyone was convinced. Critics pointed out that DeSalvo's confessions also contained mistakes. Some of the details he gave did not fit the evidence. Mary's nephew would later describe him bluntly as a con man and a sexual predator. And there was a much bigger problem. There was no physical evidence connecting Albert DeSalvo to any of the killings. No fingerprint. No eyewitness who could place him at any of the scenes. The only thing linking DeSalvo to the Boston Strangler murders was what came out of his own mouth. Then came the question of what to do with the confession. The answer is one of the most unusual outcomes in American criminal history. DeSalvo was never charged with any of the Strangler killings. Under an arrangement with prosecutors, he was instead tried on the separate Green Man charges, the sexual assaults and robberies from the series of attacks that had led to his arrest. His defense, led by Bailey, centered on his mental state. In January 1967, he was convicted. According to one account, the date was January 18, 1967. He was sentenced to life in prison. In other words, the man who had told his lawyer and investigators that he was the most feared killer in Massachusetts went to prison for life for crimes that did not include a single murder. His confession to killing Mary Sullivan was never presented to a jury. It was never cross-examined. It was never tested against the evidence in a courtroom. No judge ever ruled on whether it was true. For the Sullivan family, that meant something very specific. They had been told, in effect, that the man who killed Mary had been found. Eleven women on the official list, thirteen by DeSalvo's own count. Two nicknames from his earlier crimes, the Measuring Man and the Green Man. One long confession. Zero murder charges. The Strangler had a face and a name, and he was behind bars. For the families of the women who had died, it was not that simple. None of them had ever seen a trial for the murder of the woman they loved. None of them had heard a jury say the word guilty. And as the years passed, the man at the center of it all remained, in the eyes of the law, a man who had never been charged with a single killing. Then, on November 26, 1973, Albert DeSalvo was found stabbed to death in his cell at Walpole State Prison, the maximum security prison in Massachusetts. He was forty-two years old. The person who killed him was never identified. Years later, reports would claim that DeSalvo had been about to recant his confession around the time he was killed. That claim has been repeated many times, but it has never been proven, and it should be treated as exactly what it is, an allegation. What is certain is this. On the day he died, DeSalvo had never been charged with any murder. He had never been tried for any murder. He had never been convicted of any murder. A man who died before trial, or in this case was never tried at all, was never convicted. That is not a technicality. It is the law. And with his death, the one person who could have been questioned under oath about Mary Sullivan's last afternoon was gone. Now the cold years began in earnest. The 1970s. The 1980s. The 1990s. Detectives who had worked the Strangler cases retired. Some died. Reporters who had covered the killings moved on. The building at 44A Charles Street went on being an ordinary building on an ordinary street. And in police storage, the evidence from Mary's case stayed where it was. On Cape Cod, Mary's family carried on. Her sister Diane grew up, married, and became a mother. Her son Casey grew up with the portrait on the mantel and with a story that never quite made sense to him. He went to Boston University to study journalism. He became a reporter. And slowly, the question of who really killed his aunt became the central question of his working life. Casey Sherman was not alone in his doubts. Over the decades, a number of writers and investigators argued that the Boston Strangler killings might have been the work of more than one man. They pointed to the differences between the cases. Some of the women were elderly and some were young. The killings happened in different cities and in different ways. One book even put the idea in its title, calling the case The Boston Stranglers, in the plural. To these critics, the story of one man going door to door for a year and a half looked less like a fact and more like a narrative that had been convenient for everyone involved. A confession that solved thirteen cases at once, without a single trial, was very attractive to a public that wanted to feel safe again. Around the turn of the century, the Sullivan family and the family of Albert DeSalvo found themselves, unexpectedly, on the same side. DeSalvo's relatives believed he had been wrongly labeled as the Strangler. Mary's family believed that the real killer of their daughter and sister had never been identified. Both families wanted the same thing. They wanted a scientific test. And by then, the science existed. DNA identification had been developed in the mid-1980s, and by the late 1990s it was being used in courtrooms all over the United States. On October 14, 2000, Mary Sullivan's remains were exhumed so that scientists could look for DNA evidence. Think about what that meant for her family. Thirty-six years after they had buried her, they agreed to open her grave, because they believed the truth was worth it. Days later, on October 20, 2000, the Attorney General of Massachusetts, Thomas Reilly, announced that his office would carry out new DNA testing of its own on the evidence from Mary's case. The official process and the private process were now running in parallel. A year later, on October 26, 2001, DeSalvo's body was exhumed from its grave in Peabody, north of Boston, at the request of his family. The work was led by James Starrs, a professor of forensic science at George Washington University in Washington, D.C., who had built a career on re-examining famous cases with modern science. Starrs and his team took samples from DeSalvo's remains and compared them with material they had recovered from Mary's remains and her clothing. In December 2001, Starrs announced the results. His words were blunt. We have found evidence, he said, and the evidence does not and cannot be associated with Albert DeSalvo. According to reports of the findings, the team had found genetic material from two different people, and neither of them was DeSalvo. For Casey Sherman, it was a vindication. He told reporters, in substance, that if DeSalvo did not kill Mary Sullivan, and yet he had confessed to her murder in great detail, then there was reason to doubt that he had killed any of the women at all. If the most detailed confession was wrong about the last victim, why trust it about the others? In 2003, Sherman published his first book, A Rose for Mary, subtitled The Hunt for the Real Boston Strangler. It laid out his case that DeSalvo was not his aunt's killer, and it pointed toward other possible suspects. There was one cautious voice in all of this. Even Starrs had noted the limits of what his team had found. Their evidence, he said, cleared DeSalvo of the sexual assault based on the material they tested. It did not, by itself, say who the killer was. And later, when the official investigation produced its own answer, authorities would say that the evidence used in the private testing was questionable. Their own evidence, they said, had been preserved in a lab, collected at the original scene. Hold on to that difference, because it will matter. There was another obstacle that no amount of determination could fix. The national DNA database run by the FBI, the system that lets a crime scene profile be compared with the profiles of convicted offenders, did not open nationwide until 1998. Albert DeSalvo had died in 1973, twenty-five years earlier. No DNA profile of him had ever been made while he was alive. There was no profile of him in any database anywhere. To test him, someone would have to find his DNA another way. Count what had happened by the end of 2001. Thirty-seven years since Mary's death. Twenty-eight years since DeSalvo's. Two graves opened. One confession never tested in court. One scientist saying the DNA was not his. And in a Boston police evidence room, a blanket and biological samples from the original crime scene, collected in January 1964, still waiting for the right test.

To understand how this case was finally answered, we need to step away from Boston for a few minutes and talk about the science. Not in the language of a laboratory, but in plain words. Every person carries DNA in almost every cell of the body. Most of it comes in pairs, half from the mother and half from the father, shuffled together in a new combination for every child. That is why a standard DNA profile is so powerful. It is almost unique. Apart from identical twins, no two people share the same full profile. When a crime lab compares a standard profile from a crime scene with a standard profile from a suspect, it can say, with astonishing confidence, whether they came from the same person. But there is one piece of human DNA that behaves differently. It is the Y chromosome. Only males carry it. And a father passes his Y chromosome to his son almost unchanged. That son passes it to his son. And so on, down the generations. Think of it as a genetic surname. Just as a family name passes from father to son, the Y chromosome passes along the male line. A man shares essentially the same Y chromosome with his father, his brothers, his sons, his paternal uncles, and the sons of his brothers, his nephews. Scientists can read a set of markers on the Y chromosome called short tandem repeats. The abbreviation is Y-STR. A Y-STR profile is like reading the family name written in a man's cells. This kind of test has two qualities that matter enormously for a case like Mary's. The first is a strength. In a sexual assault case, biological evidence is often a mixture. Material from the victim and material from the attacker are tangled together, and the victim's DNA can overwhelm everything else. A Y-STR test ignores the female DNA completely, because women do not have a Y chromosome. It reads only the male material. For evidence that had sat for decades, mixed and degraded, that is a real advantage. The second quality is a limitation. Because all the men in a paternal line share the same Y-STR profile, a match does not point to one man. It points to a family. The National Institute of Justice, which helped fund the work in this case, explained it clearly. Because a Y-STR profile is common to a group of male family members, it does not give the precise match to one individual that other DNA tests can provide. In plain words, a Y-STR match is not a name. It is a family tree with one branch lit up. Scientists had already shown the world what this kind of test could do. In 1998, researchers used the Y chromosome to study the descendants of the family of Thomas Jefferson and the descendants of Sally Hemings, a woman enslaved by Jefferson, and found a genetic link between the two male lines. That study did not name an individual. It could not. But it showed that a man's family line could be traced through his descendants, generations after his death. That is exactly the kind of reasoning that would be applied to Albert DeSalvo. Now, back to Boston. In the years after 2000, the federal government, through the National Institute of Justice, was giving grants to police departments to re-examine cold cases with modern DNA testing. Boston received cold case grants from the Institute in 2009 and again in 2012. The Boston Police Department had a cold case squad, a small team of investigators whose job was to go back through unsolved homicides and look for anything that new science might be able to answer. According to the Institute, the squad decided to use some of that money on the case of Mary Sullivan. Why her case? Because the evidence was there. The biological material recovered in January 1964, seminal fluid from Mary's body and from the blanket in the apartment, had been kept. With modern methods, analysts were able to develop a Y-STR profile from it. For the first time since 1964, the evidence from that room could say something specific. It could say which family line the man came from. But a profile on its own does not mean much. It has to be compared with something. And here the investigators faced the problem we left at the end of the last chapter. Albert DeSalvo had been dead since 1973. There was no profile of him in any database. And they did not need to test every man in Boston. They needed to test one family. Here is where the genetic surname becomes useful. If the man who left that evidence was Albert DeSalvo, then any male relative in his paternal line should carry the same Y-STR profile. A son. A brother. A brother's son. If a DeSalvo nephew on the male line did not match, then DeSalvo was excluded, and the confession was false, at least for Mary. If a nephew did match, then the man in that room in 1964 belonged to the DeSalvo family line, and the circle around the confessed killer would close tighter than ever before. That is a clean scientific question. But it raised a delicate problem. The relatives of Albert DeSalvo had done nothing wrong. Many of them had spent years trying to clear his name. They had their own attorney. They had already supported one exhumation in 2001 in the hope of proving his innocence. Investigators could not assume that a member of the family would volunteer a sample to help prosecutors prove the opposite. So investigators turned to a method that American courts have generally accepted, and that has been used in many cold cases since. The discarded sample. When a person throws something away in a public place, a cup, a napkin, a cigarette butt, a water bottle, the law in most of the United States treats it as abandoned property. Police can pick it up without a warrant. And anything a person has drunk from or touched with their mouth can carry their DNA. It is not glamorous. It is often a single officer in plain clothes, watching, waiting, and moving quickly once the object hits the trash can. Critics argue that this method raises serious privacy questions, especially when the person being followed is not a suspect at all, but a relative of one. Supporters argue that it is legal, that it harms no one, and that it can bring answers to families who have waited for decades. That debate would come up in this case almost immediately. It is worth noticing how different this approach was from the tests of 2001. In 2001, the private team had worked with material recovered from Mary's remains and clothing, decades after her burial. The new test used the evidence the police had collected from the scene in January 1964. The question was the same. The starting material was not. So in the early 2010s, everything was in place. A preserved piece of evidence from 1964. A Y-STR profile developed from it. Federal funding to pay for the work. A confessed killer who had never been tested. And a family line that could be traced through the living. What investigators needed now was one moment. One man from the DeSalvo line, one ordinary day, one object thrown away. They found it.

The hero of this part of the story does not have a famous name. The public record does not tell us who he or she was. It was an investigator on the team working the case for the authorities in Boston, and the job was simple to describe and difficult to do. Follow a man who had done nothing wrong. Wait. Watch. And collect what he threw away. The man was a nephew of Albert DeSalvo, a relative on the male line, which meant that, if the science was right, he should carry the same Y chromosome as his uncle. Authorities have never publicly described the moment in detail, and we will not invent it. What they did confirm is this. Investigators secretly followed the nephew, and when he discarded a water bottle, they picked it up. A plastic water bottle. The kind of object millions of people throw away every day without a second thought. The nephew had no idea that his bottle would end up in a police lab, or that it would carry the weight of a forty-nine-year-old murder. The bottle went to the lab. Analysts developed a Y-STR profile from the DNA left on it. Then they compared that profile with the Y-STR profile developed from the evidence collected in Mary Sullivan's apartment in January 1964. They matched. Stop for a moment and think about what that meant. The evidence in Mary's room had been left by a man whose paternal line was the same as that of Albert DeSalvo's nephew. According to officials, the result excluded about 99.9 percent of the male population as possible sources of the evidence. In other words, out of every thousand men, about nine hundred and ninety-nine could not have left it. The man who did was almost certainly from the DeSalvo family line. That was not yet proof that Albert DeSalvo himself was the source. A brother, a father, a son, or another male relative on the same line would carry the same Y chromosome. But the man who had confessed in 1965, and whose confession had been doubted for decades, now sat at the center of a very small circle. On July 11, 2013, officials in Boston called a news conference. Standing together were the District Attorney of Suffolk County, Daniel Conley, the Attorney General of Massachusetts, Martha Coakley, and the Commissioner of the Boston Police Department, Edward Davis. They announced that testing of DNA from the scene of Mary Sullivan's rape and murder had produced what Conley called a familial match with Albert DeSalvo. Conley said he expected investigators to find an exact match once the crime scene evidence could be compared directly with DeSalvo's own DNA. And he was careful about one thing in particular. The new evidence, he said, applied only to the killing of Mary Sullivan. It did not apply to the other homicides attributed to the Boston Strangler. To get that direct comparison, they would need DNA from DeSalvo himself. And the only DNA of Albert DeSalvo left in the world was in his grave. So the authorities asked a court for permission to exhume his remains, and the court approved. For the second time in twelve years, the grave of Albert DeSalvo would be opened. The first time, in 2001, it had been opened by people who hoped to prove him innocent. This time, it would be opened by prosecutors who believed the science pointed the other way. The reaction was immediate. DeSalvo's family was furious about how the sample had been taken. Their attorney, Elaine Whitfield Sharp, objected to the idea that police would, in her words, sneakily, creepily, take his water bottle and take his DNA. She argued that the family had been treated with disrespect, and she said there was still reasonable doubt about whether DeSalvo had killed Mary Sullivan, even if further tests produced a complete match. It is worth hearing that objection fairly. The nephew had not committed any crime. He had not agreed to give his DNA. He had not even known that he was being followed. His genetic information had been used to build a case against a member of his family. Many people find that troubling, and it is a debate that continues in courtrooms and legislatures across America today. But it is also worth remembering what was at stake on the other side. A nineteen-year-old woman. A family on Cape Cod. Forty-nine years without an answer that could be tested. As news reports noted at the time, experts still disagreed about whether one person had committed all the killings, and the familial match did not change that. It did not tell anyone who had killed the other women. It did not settle whether the Boston Strangler had been one man or several. It spoke to one case, one room, one afternoon in January 1964. Look at the two objects at the heart of this case. A blanket from a Beacon Hill apartment, collected in January 1964. A plastic water bottle thrown away by a relative of the man who confessed, picked up decades later. Nearly half a century separates them. One was kept by a police department that could not read it. The other was discarded by a man who had no idea it mattered. Neither one, on its own, could have answered the question. Together, they changed the case. Look at the numbers too. In July 2013, if she had lived, Mary Sullivan would have been in her late sixties. Her younger sister Diane was in her sixties. Albert DeSalvo, had he lived, would have been eighty-one. Forty-nine years had passed between the afternoon on Charles Street and the news conference in Boston. On Cape Cod and in Boston, one person was watching all of this more closely than anyone. Casey Sherman had spent years, and an entire book, arguing that Albert DeSalvo had not killed his aunt. In 2001 he had welcomed the private findings that said the DNA was not DeSalvo's. Now, the official evidence was pointing straight at the man he had spent so long trying to rule out. What would he say? For a writer who had built part of his career on a single conclusion, the easiest response would have been to dig in. To question the lab, to question the police, to question the method. When the final answer came, he would respond in a way that surprised many people. But first, a crew was preparing to go to a cemetery north of Boston. Puritan Lawn Memorial Park, in the city of Peabody. Forty years earlier, in 1973, Albert DeSalvo had been buried there. Since then, his grave had been opened once already. On Friday, July 12, 2013, the day after the announcement, it would be opened again. The remains of the man who confessed to being the Boston Strangler were about to be taken from the earth, driven into Boston, and laid on a table in the office of the medical examiner. There, scientists would take what they needed. Bone. And teeth. Some of the hardest and longest-lasting parts of the human body, and some of the best places to find DNA that has survived for decades. The answer was now only days away. And it would be stronger than anyone had expected.

On Friday, July 12, 2013, a court-approved exhumation took place at Puritan Lawn Memorial Park in Peabody, Massachusetts. Television cameras gathered outside the cemetery. Reporters described the scene for viewers across New England. The remains of Albert DeSalvo were removed from the grave where they had been placed in 1973, and taken to the Office of the Chief Medical Examiner in Boston. There, according to the plan announced by the authorities, scientists would take samples of bone and tissue for DNA testing. They took what they needed. According to the National Institute of Justice, DNA was extracted from a femur, the thigh bone, and from three teeth. Bone and teeth are among the most durable parts of the human body. The hard outer layers protect the DNA inside from the moisture, bacteria and time that destroy softer tissue. Forty years after his burial, they still held a readable genetic record of the man. Now the scientists could do what no one had been able to do since 1964. They could compare the crime scene evidence from Mary Sullivan's apartment not with a relative, not with a family line, but with Albert DeSalvo himself. This time, the comparison would be direct, man to evidence, with no relative standing in between. One week after the exhumation, on Friday, July 19, 2013, District Attorney Conley, Attorney General Coakley and Commissioner Davis made a joint announcement. They said that a nationally recognized laboratory had compared the DNA from DeSalvo's remains with the seminal fluid recovered nearly fifty years earlier from Mary Sullivan's body and from the blanket in her apartment. The result was a match. According to the officials, DNA specialists calculated the odds that a white male other than Albert DeSalvo was the source of the crime scene evidence at one in two hundred and twenty billion. Pause on that number. One in two hundred and twenty billion. There are about eight billion people on Earth. The number is larger than most estimates of the total number of human beings who have ever lived. Attorney General Martha Coakley chose her words carefully, and they were strong. The finding, she said, leaves no doubt that Albert DeSalvo was responsible for the brutal murder of Mary Sullivan. The National Institute of Justice, summarizing the case later, wrote that the result left no doubt that DeSalvo had raped and murdered Mary. Now listen closely to what the authorities did not say. They did not say that the DNA proved Albert DeSalvo had killed the other women. Coakley said that DeSalvo was, in her words, most likely responsible for the other Boston Strangler killings as well. Most likely. That is an opinion, not a scientific result. The District Attorney had already stressed that the evidence applied only to the Sullivan case. And officials explained why. In the other cases, they had not been able to find enough testable DNA. For the other women, there was no blanket in a lab, no sample that could be read. Count the years one more time, because they explain why this result could never be tested in court. Albert DeSalvo was thirty-two when Mary Sullivan died. He was thirty-three when he was arrested as the Green Man, and still in his early thirties when he confessed in 1965. He was thirty-five when he was sentenced to life in January 1967. He was forty-two when he was killed at Walpole in 1973. He had been dead for twenty-eight years when his own family had his grave opened in 2001 in the hope of clearing his name, and for almost forty years when prosecutors had it opened again in 2013 to confirm the opposite. Forty-eight years passed between his confession and the DNA result. By the time science could check what he had said, there was no one left to cross-examine. Then there is the legal question. And here, the language matters, because this channel is careful about it, and you should be too. Albert DeSalvo was never charged with the murder of Mary Sullivan. He was never tried for it. He was never convicted of it. He died in 1973, and a dead man cannot be charged, tried or convicted. There will never be a trial. There will never be a jury verdict. What exists instead is a conclusion by prosecutors and police, based on DNA evidence, that DeSalvo was the source of the biological evidence found on Mary's body and in her room, and their statement that this leaves no doubt he was responsible for her murder. That is a powerful conclusion. But it is a conclusion reached outside a courtroom. Not everyone accepted it. Elaine Whitfield Sharp, the attorney for the DeSalvo family, disputed the result. She argued that there was no level of what she called unprecedented certainty, as the government now claimed. She also pointed out that the private tests in 2001 had found DNA from other men. The authorities' answer was that their evidence had been preserved in a lab, while the evidence used in the private testing was questionable. Two sets of tests. Two different starting points. Two different conclusions. And then came the voice that many people were waiting for. Casey Sherman, the nephew who had grown up with Mary's portrait on the mantel, the journalist who had written a book arguing that DeSalvo was not her killer, accepted the result. He said the DNA evidence appeared overwhelming. He explained his change of mind in one short sentence. I only go where the evidence leads. He thanked the police and praised them for what he called their incredible persistence. And then he said the words that, for a moment, seemed to close a circle that had been open since 1964. This is now full justice for my aunt, Mary Sullivan. He also said something that reminded everyone what this had always been about. I've lived with Mary's memory every day, my whole life. Think about the courage that took. For more than a decade, his public position, his book and his reputation had been built on the idea that DeSalvo did not kill his aunt. And when the strongest evidence in the case pointed the other way, he said so, publicly, in front of the cameras. That is rare. Many people, faced with evidence that contradicts years of their own work, look for reasons to reject it. On that day, Sherman did the opposite. For the Sullivan family, July 19, 2013, became a kind of verdict day that never came in a courtroom. Mary had died on January 4, 1964. She had been nineteen. Her sister had been seventeen. Forty-nine years, six months and fifteen days later, the evidence from her room had finally spoken, and it had spoken a name. It was the same name the public had heard in 1965. The same man who had confessed. The same man whose confession had been doubted by so many, including Mary's own family. In the end, on the question of Mary's murder, the confession and the science pointed in the same direction. But the story of the Boston Strangler did not end with that news conference. Because a DNA match in one case is not a DNA match in thirteen. And the questions that had haunted Boston since 1962 were about to return, and they have never fully gone away.

So where does that leave the story of the Boston Strangler today? To answer honestly, we have to separate two things that people have been mixing together for more than sixty years. The first is the murder of Mary Sullivan. The second is the series of killings that the newspapers bundled together under one name. Start with what the DNA proves. Biological evidence collected from Mary's body and from a blanket in her apartment on January 4, 1964, was preserved for nearly half a century. In 2013, a Y-STR test linked that evidence to a male relative of Albert DeSalvo, through a water bottle the relative threw away. Then DNA taken directly from DeSalvo's exhumed remains, from a femur and three teeth, was compared with the crime scene evidence, and according to the authorities it matched, with odds of one in two hundred and twenty billion that another white male was the source. On that basis, the Attorney General of Massachusetts said the finding left no doubt that DeSalvo was responsible for Mary's murder. He was never charged, never tried and never convicted, because he had been dead since 1973. But for the authorities who examined the evidence, the case of Mary Sullivan has an answer. Now, what remains debated. Everything else. The DNA says nothing about the other women. Anna Slesers. Mary Mullen. Nina Nichols. Helen Blake. Ida Irga. Jane Sullivan. Sophie Clark. Patricia Bissette. Mary Brown. Beverly Samans. Evelyn Corbin. Joann Graff. For them, officials said, there was not enough testable DNA. Their cases rest where they rested in 1967, on a confession that no court ever tested. The Attorney General said that DeSalvo was most likely responsible for those killings as well. Others have never agreed. Writers have argued for decades that the killings may have involved more than one man. And the debate has not stopped. In November 2025, more than twelve years after the DNA announcement, the television network Oxygen aired a documentary called The Boston Strangler, Unheard Confession, featuring audio recordings of DeSalvo that, according to the network, had never been heard by the public. Its host was Casey Sherman. In an interview published by Fox News Digital on November 15, 2025, Sherman said he does not believe DeSalvo committed the Boston Strangler murders. He described DeSalvo as a con man and a sexual predator, but said he did not believe DeSalvo murdered these women. He pointed to the opinion of DeSalvo's psychologist at Bridgewater, Dr. Ames Robey, who, according to Sherman, told him he did not think DeSalvo was capable of homicide. According to a press release quoted by the network, the recordings show DeSalvo getting key details wrong and, at times, appearing to be coached toward specific answers. Those are the claims of a documentary and of the man who hosted it. They are not new findings by police or prosecutors. And the public reports we found do not explain in detail how Sherman now reconciles his doubts with the 2013 DNA result in his own aunt's case. As of October 2026, we found no public statement from Boston police or Massachusetts prosecutors changing the conclusion they announced in July 2013. So the honest summary is this. In Mary Sullivan's case, the science and the confession point to the same man, and the authorities say there is no doubt. In the other cases, there is no DNA answer, only a confession, and reasonable people still disagree. A confession is not proof. That may be the most important lesson of this entire story. For almost fifty years, Albert DeSalvo's words were treated by some as the final truth and by others as a lie, and no court ever weighed them. In the end, for one victim, it took a blanket on a shelf and a bottle in a trash can to settle what the words could not. The method used in this case has also outlived it. Since 2013, investigators across the United States have used discarded objects, cups, straws, cigarette butts and bottles, to collect DNA from suspects and their relatives in old cases, and genetic genealogy has made family-line searches far more powerful than a single Y-STR comparison. The privacy questions that the DeSalvo family's attorney raised in 2013 have not gone away either. They are still argued in courts and legislatures today. Think about what would have happened if that blanket had been thrown away. If the evidence from Mary's room had been lost in one of the moves, floods or clean-outs that destroyed so many old case files across America. There would be no answer for Mary at all. There would only be a confession, a doubting family, a scientist saying the DNA was not his, and a grave opened twice. The most important decision in this case may have been made by someone whose name we will never know, who looked at a box of old evidence and decided to keep it. Now, let's go back to where we started. It is Saturday, January 4, 1964. Beacon Hill, Boston. Charles Street, at the foot of the hill, between the Common and the river. A nineteen-year-old named Mary Sullivan, from Cape Cod, is in the apartment at 44A, where she has lived for only a few days. The year is four days old. She does not know that she will be the last name on a list that the whole country will learn. She does not know that her little sister will carry her memory for the rest of her life, or that a nephew who has not yet been born will spend his career trying to find out what happened in that room. She does not know that the evidence from that afternoon will sit on a shelf for forty-nine years, waiting for a science that has not been invented. She knows only that she is nineteen, and that she has just arrived. So let's remember her not as the last victim, but as the person she was. Mary Sullivan was a daughter of Cape Cod. She was the joy of her Irish Catholic family. She was her sister Diane's best friend. She and Diane had planned to live their lives together. She was the face in a portrait on a mantel, looking out over a living room where a boy grew up and decided to become a journalist because of her. She was nineteen years old, and she had just moved to the city to start her own life. And she was given only a few days of it. Her name deserves to be remembered beside the names of all the women who died in that year and a half, the seamstress, the widows, the young women with roommates and plans, every one of them a person, not a headline. If there is one thing to take from her story, it is this. Keep the evidence. Test the confession. And never confuse the story everyone believes with the facts that have actually been proven. Sometimes the answer arrives decades late. Sometimes it arrives for only one family out of thirteen. But when it arrives, it arrives because someone refused to stop asking.
