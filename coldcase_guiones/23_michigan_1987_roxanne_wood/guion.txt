The national DNA index is not a list of everyone. If the person who left the DNA had never been entered, the database had nothing to compare it to. A search could run a thousand times and return silence every time. In the same decade, states also began building sex offender registries, Indiana in 1994 and Michigan in 1995, lists of people convicted of sexual crimes who were required to report where they lived. Those registries were built for the future. They could not reach back to a February night in 1987, and each one was kept by its own state. A local investigator in Michigan looking through local names would not automatically see a man who had been convicted, and paroled, on the other side of the state line. And there was a second catch, specific to this case. The amount of DNA that survived from the scene was tiny. Years later, Colleen Fitzpatrick, the president of a company called Identifinders International, would describe it to CBS News as a gnat's eyebrow of DNA left, about three percent of what we normally use. Three percent. Imagine trying to read a book when ninety-seven pages out of every hundred have been torn out. A laboratory can do remarkable things, but every test consumes some of the sample, and when there is almost nothing to begin with, every decision about how to use it becomes a gamble. Use it on the wrong test, and it may be gone forever. So the DNA from Roxanne Wood's murder waited, while the rest of the country moved on. Ten years passed. Then twenty. The children of the 1980s grew up. The people who had bowled in those leagues grew older. And at some point in those decades, according to later reporting, the DNA from the scene was found not to match Terry Wood. Think about what that should have meant. The physical evidence from the murder pointed away from the husband. And yet, as far as the public record shows, it did not end the suspicion. That is the most striking line in this story's ledger of missed chances, and it is worth laying the ledger out plainly. One. On the night of the murder, witnesses placed Terry at the bowling alley at the time Roxanne was killed. He was suspected anyway. Two. Prosecutors decided there was not enough evidence to charge him. He was suspected anyway. Three. The DNA left at the scene, according to later reporting, never matched him. Yet in 2013 he was still, in the words of a detective, the main person of interest. Four. Through all of those years, as far as any public report shows, the man who had actually done it was never questioned about Roxanne Wood at all. In 2013, twenty-six years after the murder, the South Bend television station WSBT revisited the case in a series called Cold Case Files. The headline of that report tells you everything about where things stood. Police have a suspect in a twenty-six-year-old murder case, it said, but did he do it? The suspect was Terry. In that report, a Michigan State Police detective named Suarez said that Roxanne's husband had always been the main suspect, the main person of interest in the investigation. He said Terry's story never made sense to investigators, and he described how the 1987 interview had been interrupted when Terry's family hired an attorney. Twenty-six years later, a request for a lawyer was still being described as part of the case against him. WSBT went to Terry. He declined to go on camera. He said that his attorney had advised him not to talk. But he gave the station one sentence, and it may be the most important thing Terry Wood has ever said in public. I didn't murder Roxanne. Four words. After twenty-six years. It is a sentence that has been said by guilty men and innocent men alike, which is exactly why it carried so little weight at the time. In 2013, nobody outside Terry's own circle had any reason to believe it more than they believed the detective. Now listen to it knowing what we know today. It was the truth. It had always been the truth. And for another nine years, it would be treated as just one more thing a suspect would say. At another point in those years, a regional station, WWMT in Kalamazoo, also examined the killing in an investigative report titled Murder Mystery. The case had become exactly that, a local mystery, the kind that comes up in conversation and on anniversaries and then fades again. Meanwhile, in a different part of the country, a quiet revolution in genetics was underway. In 2010, a free website called GEDmatch went online, allowing people who had taken consumer DNA tests to upload their results and find relatives. Millions of people were sending saliva samples to genealogy companies to learn where their great-grandparents came from. None of them were thinking about crime. But a handful of genealogists and forensic scientists realized that those same databases could be used in a completely different way. If a killer's DNA could be turned into the same kind of profile that hobby genealogists used, it could be compared not against criminals, but against ordinary people, and the killer's cousins might be among them. In April 2018, that idea was used to identify the man known as the Golden State Killer in California, a case that had been unsolved for more than forty years. Almost overnight, police departments across America began to look at their own shelves differently. Cases that had been written off as impossible were suddenly worth another look. The years had cost Roxanne's family three decades without an answer. They had cost Terry three decades of being looked at sideways. But they had also built, piece by piece, the tools that could finally read what had been left in that kitchen. By 2020, thirty-three years after the murder, two very different groups of people were about to pick up the case of Roxanne Wood. One was a private laboratory that specialized in the hardest DNA in the country. The other was a classroom of university students in Kalamazoo.

The first group came from Western Michigan University, in Kalamazoo, about an hour's drive northeast of Niles. The university runs a Cold Case Program in which students work alongside law enforcement agencies on unsolved cases. They are not detectives. They do not carry badges. What they bring is something that overworked police departments rarely have, which is time, patience and fresh eyes. When the Michigan State Police brought the Roxanne Wood file to the program, what they handed over was not a neat folder. It was thirty-three years of paper. Reports, interview notes, lab results, tips, memos, typed pages and handwritten ones, the accumulated record of an investigation that had passed through many hands. According to the reporting on the case, the file ran to somewhere between three thousand and three thousand five hundred pages, and it lived in boxes. The students' job was to turn those boxes into something a detective could actually use. They read every page. They catalogued it. And they built a searchable database, so that any name, any address, any date, any detail in the entire history of the case could be found in seconds rather than days. According to the university, the students dedicated several thousand hours to organizing the material in the file. Michigan State Police later said the students had played a key organizational role, cataloguing the file and, in their words, thinking out loud alongside the investigators. Think about why that matters. In a case that is decades old, the answer is often already in the file somewhere, buried in a tip that nobody followed up, or a name that appears once on page two thousand and never again. When the file lives in boxes, a detective can only check what he remembers. When the file becomes a database, a detective can ask it a question, and it answers. Soon, the investigators would have a very specific question to ask it. The second group was a company called Identifinders International. According to the timeline later published by CBS News, in 2020 Identifinders began looking into the unidentified DNA from Roxanne's case. Its president, Colleen Fitzpatrick, is one of the best-known forensic genealogists in the country. And it was Fitzpatrick who would describe the challenge in the phrase that has stuck to this case ever since. A gnat's eyebrow of DNA left, about three percent of what we normally use. Another account of the case quoted the team calling it the lowest amount of DNA they had ever had to work with to solve a case. To understand why that was such a problem, and why it eventually did not matter, it helps to understand how genetic genealogy actually works. So let's take a moment, and let's keep it simple. The DNA profile that police have used since the 1990s is a little like a short barcode. It looks at a small number of specific places in a person's genetic code, around twenty in the modern American system, and it records the pattern there. That barcode is excellent for one thing, which is comparing one sample directly to another. If the crime scene barcode and the suspect's barcode match, the chances that they came from two different people are astronomically small. But the barcode tells you almost nothing about who a person's relatives are. Genetic genealogy uses a completely different kind of profile. Instead of twenty places in the genetic code, it looks at hundreds of thousands. These are the same kinds of markers that consumer DNA companies read when someone sends in a saliva sample to learn about their ancestry. And because we inherit our DNA from our parents, and they from theirs, those hundreds of thousands of markers are partly shared with every one of our relatives, close and distant. A sibling shares a lot. A first cousin shares less. A first cousin shares, on average, around twelve percent. A third cousin shares less than one percent. By measuring how much DNA an unknown person shares with each relative in a database, a genealogist can estimate how closely they are related. The process, in plain words, goes like this. First, the laboratory has to extract enough DNA from the old evidence to build that larger profile. With a sample as small as the one from Tam-O-Shanter Lane, that alone is a serious technical challenge. Second, the profile is uploaded to the genealogy databases that allow it to be used in law enforcement cases. This matters, because these are private citizens' DNA results. Since 2019, the best known of the public sites, GEDmatch, has required its users to opt in before their results can be compared in police cases, and other services set their own rules about whether and how police may use them. The people whose results make a case like this possible are, almost always, people who simply wanted to know where their family came from. Third, the database returns a list of people who share some DNA with the unknown person. These are usually not close relatives. They might be third or fourth cousins, people who share a set of great-great-grandparents with the person the police are looking for, and who have no idea that this person even exists. Then the real work begins. The genealogist takes those distant cousins and builds their family trees, backward through birth records, marriage records, obituaries, census pages and old newspapers, until the trees meet at a shared ancestor. Then the genealogist builds forward again, down through all of that ancestor's descendants, generation by generation, looking for a person who fits. The right age. The right sex. The right place at the right time. The family tree does not prove anything on its own. It produces a name, or a short list of names. The proof has to come afterward, the old-fashioned way. Police must obtain a sample of DNA from the person on that list, legally, and compare it directly with the crime scene profile. Only that direct comparison, the barcode against the barcode, can confirm an identification. That is how the Golden State Killer was found in 2018. That is how hundreds of other cases have been solved since. And it explains why a case once considered impossible was suddenly back on the table. The trace from Roxanne's kitchen did not need to match anyone in a police database. It only needed to share a little DNA with someone, somewhere, who had once been curious about their own family history. But the process is not magic, and it is not quick. According to CBS News, Identifinders spent about ten months working on the case without results. Ten months of testing, uploading, building and searching, and the answer did not come. The DNA was too limited. The matches were too distant. The trees refused to meet. The case that had defeated investigators for thirty-three years now seemed to be defeating the science as well. At around the same time, the Michigan State Police had assigned the case to two detective sergeants from the Niles post, John Moore and Jason Bailey. They would spend roughly a year and a half working it. And they were about to get help from someone who would later say, in a sentence that is almost a summary of this entire story, that the case had been deemed unsolvable before she became involved.

Her name is Gabriella Vargas. She is an investigative genetic genealogist, and she worked as a consultant with Identifinders. According to CBS News, after ten months of the company coming up empty-handed on Roxanne's case, Vargas offered to help. It was April 2021. Roxanne Wood had been dead for thirty-four years. Terry Wood had been a suspect for thirty-four years. And the DNA from the kitchen on Tam-O-Shanter Lane had been sitting in storage for all of that time, waiting for someone who could read it. Vargas later said something about the case that sums up where it stood when it reached her. It was deemed unsolvable, she said, prior to my involvement. Unsolvable. That was the word. Not difficult. Not slow. Unsolvable. A sample that was only about three percent of what a lab would normally want. Ten months of work by an experienced team without a result. A case file thirty-four years deep. A family that had heard promises before. What happened next is the reason news outlets would later run headlines about a woman who cracked a thirty-five-year-old Michigan murder in four days. That is the number. Four days. Ten months had produced nothing. Four days produced a family, and that family would produce a short list of names. Working from the genetic profile developed from the killer's trace DNA, and from the distant relatives who appeared in the public genealogy databases, Vargas built the family tree. Branch after branch. Record after record. Great-grandparents, grandparents, their children, their children's children. She was looking for the place where the unknown man's DNA fit into a real family, and she was looking for the man inside that family who was the right age, in the right place, at the right time. And she found the family. According to the reporting on the case, the tree narrowed the search to three brothers. Three men from one family, any one of whom, on paper, could have been the source of the DNA. The genealogy could take investigators to that door. It could not tell them which brother had walked into Roxanne's kitchen. That is the moment when genetic genealogy hands the case back to traditional police work, and to the two detective sergeants from the Niles post, John Moore and Jason Bailey. One of the three brothers was a man named Patrick Wayne Gilham. He lived in South Bend, Indiana. His home was about ten miles south of the house where Roxanne Wood had been killed. Ten miles. In 1987, Roxanne herself had worked in South Bend and come home to Niles at the end of each day. And when investigators began to look at Gilham's past, they found something that changed everything. According to Indiana court records, as reported by WOOD-TV, Patrick Gilham had pleaded guilty in 1980 to burglary and to criminal deviate conduct, an Indiana offence that, at the time, covered forcible sexual crimes. He was sentenced to fourteen years in prison. He was released after about six. And at the time of Roxanne's murder, he was on parole for those crimes. Reports differ slightly on exactly how long he had been free, but they agree that he had been released only months before that night in February 1987. Let that settle for a moment. A man convicted of a burglary and a sexual offence was out on parole, living in the same small region, at the very moment when a woman was sexually assaulted and killed in her own home, with no sign of forced entry. And for thirty-four years, as far as any public report shows, nobody ever connected his name to Roxanne Wood. In February 1987, he was about thirty-two years old. In 2021, he was in his mid-sixties. Still a name on a family tree, not yet a suspect who had been confirmed. The family tree was a lead, not proof. To turn it into proof, investigators needed a sample of Gilham's DNA, and they needed to get it legally, without tipping him off. They could not simply knock on his door and ask. If he was the man they were looking for, a request like that could send him running, or prompt him to destroy anything that connected him to the crime. So they did what detectives in genealogy cases across the country have learned to do. They watched him. According to the Michigan State Police, Gilham was surveilled extensively by undercover state police units. Surveillance of that kind is slow and tedious work, hours spent watching a man move through the ordinary errands of his day, waiting for him to do something completely unremarkable. The investigators were waiting for one ordinary moment, the moment when he would leave a piece of his own DNA somewhere public, on something he no longer wanted. A coffee cup. A straw. A napkin. It came on June 3, 2021. According to the police report later described by the Niles newspaper Leader Publications, investigators collected a publicly discarded cigarette butt. In the United States, courts have generally held that once a person throws something away in a public place, they have given up any reasonable expectation of privacy in it. It is no longer theirs. It is trash. And on that piece of trash was saliva, and in that saliva was DNA. The cigarette went to the laboratory. The DNA from the cigarette was compared directly with the DNA from the scene, the old-fashioned barcode against barcode test that genealogy cannot replace. According to the police report, the result was a match. The odds were reported as one in 958.8 trillion. That is a number far larger than the number of people who have ever lived on Earth. The trace of DNA that had waited in storage since February 1987 now had a name. It was not Terry Wood. It had never been Terry Wood. Then the detectives did something that every investigator in a case like this must do. They went to talk to the man. On July 29, 2021, Michigan State Police detectives interviewed Patrick Gilham at the South Bend Police Department. According to the police report, he denied ever going to Michigan except for work in 1987, at a time when he was on parole in Indiana. He denied having friends in Niles. He denied knowing the street where Roxanne and Terry Wood lived. He denied dating any women in Niles. Every denial was recorded. And every denial would be weighed against a laboratory result with odds of nearly a quadrillion to one. Investigators would interview Gilham on two separate occasions in all, with detective sergeants Moore, Bailey and a third detective, Kyle Gorham. And still, for months, they did not arrest him. In a case thirty-four years old, there was no room for error. The state police have not described every step of those months in public. What is known is that the file, those thousands of pages the students had turned into a database, was now searchable, and that by February 2022 the Berrien County prosecutor was ready to file charges. Somewhere in the Niles area, as far as the public record shows, Terry Wood still did not know any of this. He was still, as far as the public knew, the man who had come home at one in the morning and found his wife. He was still the person of interest. In just a few months, that would end, and it would end with news he had been waiting thirty-five years to hear.

On Thursday, February 17, 2022, officers arrested Patrick Wayne Gilham at his home in South Bend, Indiana. He was sixty-seven years old. In three days, it would be exactly thirty-five years since Roxanne Wood had been killed. He was held in the St. Joseph County jail in Indiana, awaiting extradition across the state line to Michigan, the same state line that had separated Roxanne's home in Niles from her workplace in South Bend. The Berrien County Prosecutor, Steve Pierangeli, announced the charges. Open murder, and breaking and entering. Under Michigan law, open murder is a charge that leaves the degree of the killing to be decided later, by a judge or jury, so that the case can end as first-degree murder, second-degree murder, or a lesser offence depending on the evidence. The breaking and entering charge spoke directly to the question that had shaped the entire investigation in 1987. There had been no sign of forced entry. Now the state of Michigan was alleging that a stranger had entered the house anyway. At first, the police said very little about how they had found him. Detective First Lieutenant Chuck Christensen of the Michigan State Police spoke to reporters after the arrest. There's relief that we have an individual arrested for this, he said, but I can tell you there's a lot of investigative work that will continue from this point. The details, the genealogy, the four days, the cigarette butt, the one in 958.8 trillion, would come out over the following weeks, in police reports and press releases and, eventually, in a national television documentary. But one detail mattered more than any other to one man. Terry Wood was told. According to Lieutenant Christensen, when Terry learned of the arrest he was very emotional. He started crying. He was, in the lieutenant's description, bewildered, and in shock. Police said he was pleased and relieved. One headline summed it up in a single word, vindicated. Think about what those thirty-five years had been. A wife lost on a February night. A detective who, according to Roxanne's sister, told him he would not rest until Terry was behind bars. A request for a lawyer that was treated as evidence. Twenty-six years later, a television report still asking whether he did it. Thirty-five years of being the first name in every article, the name that came up whenever anyone talked about the case, the man who could never fully prove that he had not done something terrible. And now, in a single conversation with the state police, it was over. The DNA in that kitchen had never been his, and now there was a name to prove it. It is worth noticing what Terry did not do. He did not give a press conference. He did not do a round of interviews. Later in 2022, when the CBS program 48 Hours prepared a full documentary on the case, Terry declined its request for an interview. After thirty-five years in other people's stories, he chose to stay out of this one. On March 16, 2022, the Niles newspaper Leader Publications reported details from the police report, the cigarette collected on June 3, 2021, the laboratory match, and the interview in which Gilham had denied any connection to Niles. For the first time, the public could see how the case against him had been built. Two days later, on March 18, 2022, less than a month after his arrest, Patrick Gilham appeared in a Berrien County courtroom and pleaded no contest to second-degree murder. A no contest plea is an unusual thing, and it is worth explaining. The defendant does not say I did it. He does not say I am innocent either. He says, in effect, that he will not fight the charge. The court then finds him guilty and he is sentenced exactly as if he had been convicted. In Michigan, a no contest plea requires the court's consent, and it is sometimes accepted when a defendant says he cannot remember the events. That is what Gilham's lawyer would say. Second-degree murder, under Michigan law, is a killing committed with malice but without the premeditation required for first-degree murder, and it can be punished by any term of years, up to life in prison. The plea was part of an agreement with the prosecutor's office that included a minimum sentence of twenty-three years in prison. According to the Associated Press, the prosecutor said the deal was offered with the input of Roxanne's family, and that included her husband, Terry Wood. The prosecutor's office said the agreement effectively keeps Gilham in prison for the rest of his life. Why accept a plea at all, in a case with a DNA match at odds of nearly a quadrillion to one? Because a trial, even a strong one, would have meant months more of waiting, a jury, an appeal, and Roxanne's family reliving that February night in public, day after day. A plea brought certainty. It brought a conviction. And it brought it in weeks, not years. On April 25, 2022, the case came to its final hearing, in the Berrien County courthouse in Niles, the city where Roxanne had been born, where she had gone to school, and where she had died. The judge sentenced Patrick Wayne Gilham to a minimum of twenty-three years and a maximum of fifty years in prison. In the courtroom were Roxanne's brother and sister, Brad and Janet, who gave victim impact statements. And in the courtroom, too, were students from the Western Michigan University Cold Case Program, the young people who had spent thousands of hours turning boxes of paper into a searchable record of Roxanne's case. They heard the family speak. Gilham spoke as well. According to reports from the hearing, he said he was very sorry, and that he hoped that, with God's help, the family could one day forgive him. His lawyer, Scott Sanford, told the court that because of his use of illegal drugs, Gilham did not recall the events of that night three and a half decades earlier. He can't recall the events, which is no excuse, Sanford said, but he is genuinely remorseful. Roxanne's family was not persuaded. Her brother Brad responded to the apology. It seems people like him tend to find Jesus in prison, he said. It is a hard line, and it came from thirty-five years of waiting.
