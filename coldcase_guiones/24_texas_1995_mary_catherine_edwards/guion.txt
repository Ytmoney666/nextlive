Every homicide investigation begins with the same set of questions. Who was close to her. Who had a reason. Who had access. Who was seen nearby. In the case of Mary Catherine Edwards, the scene itself seemed to answer one of those questions before detectives had asked it. No forced entry meant access. Access meant, most likely, someone she knew or someone she was willing to open the door for. So the investigation turned toward the people in her life. That is standard practice in any case like this one, and it is not a criticism of anyone to say so. Detectives look first at the inner circle because, in the great majority of killings of women in their own homes, the person responsible is someone the victim knew. The Beaumont Police Department and the Texas Rangers would later be described, by the lab that eventually helped solve the case, as having conducted an exhaustive investigation. They talked to people. They followed tips. They worked through the names that came up. And at the end of all that work, the case remained unsolved. Then there were the handcuffs. Of all the physical evidence in the townhome, the handcuffs seemed to offer the most direct path to a name. They were police-grade, made by Smith and Wesson, and they had a serial number. In theory, a serial number is a thread you can pull. You contact the manufacturer and ask where that batch was shipped. You contact the distributor and ask which store received it. You contact the store and ask whether anyone remembers selling it, and whether there is a receipt, a purchase order, a department invoice. If the handcuffs had been issued to an officer, the trail might lead straight to a locker room. If they had been bought by a private citizen, it might lead to a name on a mail-order slip. A cold case does not become cold on a particular day. There is no announcement, no letter to the family saying that the search has stopped. It happens slowly, the way a room cools after the heat is turned off. Calls to the detective's desk are returned a little later. The newspaper stories move from the front page to the inside pages, then to the anniversary pieces once a year, then to nothing. The file is moved from an active drawer to a shelf. And the family, who never stopped thinking about the case for a single day, slowly realizes that the rest of the world has. For the family of Mary Catherine Edwards, that cooling began in 1995 and lasted for more than a quarter of a century. Consider how much changed in the world while her case sat waiting. In October 1998, the FBI's National DNA Index System began operating nationally, linking state and local databases into one searchable system known by its acronym, CODIS. For the first time, a DNA profile from a crime scene in one state could be compared automatically against profiles of convicted offenders and against evidence from other unsolved crimes across the country. The profile used in that system is built from a small number of locations in the human genome, places where a short stretch of genetic code repeats itself a different number of times from person to person. Forensic scientists call them short tandem repeats. Think of them like the digits of a very long phone number. If two samples share the same digits at every location, they almost certainly came from the same person. At some point, the DNA from Mary Catherine's case was developed into exactly that kind of profile and searched in CODIS. The result was the result that every family of an unsolved case dreads. No match. There was a reason for that, and it is a reason that runs through dozens of cold cases in America. CODIS can only find people who are already in it. Those people are mostly offenders whose DNA was collected after a conviction or, in some states, after an arrest, under laws that were passed in the 1990s and 2000s and expanded over time. A man whose only violent offense had been resolved in 1981, long before those laws existed, with a guilty plea to aggravated assault and three years of deferred adjudication, was not going to be in that system unless something new put him there. Nothing did. So the profile sat in the database, and it waited, and every time it was compared, it came back empty. The new millennium arrived. Cell phones moved into every pocket. The internet stopped hissing and became something you carried with you. Beaumont lived through hurricanes, through boom and bust in the oil and chemical industries, through the ordinary turnover of a mid-sized American city. Students who had sat in classrooms at Price Elementary in 1995 graduated from high school, then from college, then started families. Over 26 years, the people assigned to any case change. Detectives are promoted, transferred, retire. Chiefs come and go. District attorneys are elected and replaced. Each new investigator who opened the Edwards file inherited the same evidence, the same photographs, the same dead ends with the handcuffs, the same empty CODIS result. And each one faced the same wall. Good police work had been done. The answer was simply not in any place that police work could reach. Allison, Mary Catherine's identical twin, lived through all of it. She grew older year by year, decade by decade. Every birthday was a birthday the twins should have shared. Every year on January 14, the calendar circled back to the day her family had gone up those stairs. Every year, the people who loved Mary Catherine had to decide again how to hold on to hope without letting it consume them. When the case was finally solved, a family spokesperson would describe the experience as a long, emotional journey. It is hard to imagine a more restrained way to describe 26 years of not knowing. Let us lay out the ledger, the account of what the system had and what it missed, in plain numbers. One sexual assault kit, properly collected and preserved. One pair of police-grade Smith and Wesson handcuffs, with serial numbers that led nowhere. One profile in CODIS, with zero matches. One man who had never been a person of interest. One earlier attack, in 1981, on a woman of nineteen whose hands had also been bound behind her back, resolved with a plea to aggravated assault and three years of deferred adjudication. Twelve similarities between those two crimes, which a court would one day list one by one. And zero links made between them for 26 years. Then, in the late 2010s, something began to shift, and it began not in a laboratory, but in an elected office. In January 2019, Bob Wortham took office as the Criminal District Attorney of Jefferson County. Wortham later recalled that on his very first day as district attorney, he called Beaumont Police Chief Jim Singletary and asked him to put people on the Mary Catherine Edwards case. It was a small act, a phone call. But in the life of a cold case, a phone call from the person who will one day have to prosecute it can change everything. It meant the case had a champion with the power to move resources. It meant someone in authority wanted an answer and was willing to say so. Among the Beaumont investigators who would carry the case in this new phase was a detective named Aaron Lewallen. He would come to know it as well as anyone. And he would be working it at a moment when, for the first time, there was a real chance that the DNA in that 1995 kit could say something new. In 2020, the Texas Rangers, through their program for unsolved crimes, and the Beaumont Police Department renewed their efforts publicly. A reward of 6,000 dollars was offered for information. The case went back into the news. The Texas Department of Public Safety keeps a public online list of cases worked by the Rangers' unsolved crimes program, each one a photograph, a date and a short summary asking anyone who knows something to come forward. Mary Catherine's case had its own page on that list. For years, it was one name among many. It is worth remembering what 2020 was like. That spring, the world was shutting down because of the coronavirus pandemic. Schools closed. Offices emptied. People stayed home. And in the middle of all that, in April 2020, Mary Catherine Edwards' DNA evidence was sent to a private laboratory in The Woodlands, a planned community north of Houston, less than a two-hour drive from Beaumont. The lab was called Othram. Its scientists did not do the kind of DNA testing that had been done in 1995, or the kind that had been searched in CODIS. They did something that, until just a couple of years earlier, almost no police department in America had believed was possible. And the reason they could do it at all goes back to a case on the other side of the country, a case that had nothing to do with Beaumont, and a decision by investigators in California in 2018 that would change cold case work forever.

To understand how the Mary Catherine Edwards case was solved, you have to understand a quiet revolution that began not in a police lab, but in the hobby of family history. For decades, millions of Americans had been fascinated by their own family trees. They searched old census records, ship manifests, church registries and newspaper obituaries, trying to find out where their great-grandparents had come from. Then, in the 2000s and 2010s, consumer DNA testing made that hobby explode. For less than the price of a nice dinner, you could spit into a tube, mail it to a company, and receive a list of people around the world who shared some of your DNA, people who might be your second cousins, your third cousins, or relatives you never knew existed. Some of those people chose to upload their genetic data to free public websites, so they could compare their results with people who had tested at other companies. The best known of these sites was GEDmatch, launched in 2010 by two volunteer genealogists. Nobody who uploaded their DNA to GEDmatch in its early years was thinking about murder investigations. They were thinking about adoption searches, lost relatives, and the family stories their grandparents used to tell. Then came April 2018. In California, investigators had spent decades hunting a man known as the Golden State Killer, responsible for a series of murders and dozens of sexual assaults across the state in the 1970s and 1980s. They had his DNA. It had never matched anyone in CODIS. So the investigators tried something new. They took a profile from the crime scene evidence and compared it with the profiles of people on a genealogy website. They did not find the killer. They found his distant relatives. From those relatives, genealogists built family trees backward to shared ancestors, generations back, and then forward again, through every branch, every marriage, every child, until the trees narrowed to a small number of men who were the right age and had lived in the right places. One of them was a former police officer named Joseph James DeAngelo. Investigators collected his DNA from items he had discarded, confirmed the match, and arrested him on April 24, 2018. He later pleaded guilty and was sentenced to life in prison. The arrest stunned the country, and it changed cold case work almost overnight. Within months, police departments across the United States were sending old evidence to genealogists. The technique got a name: forensic genetic genealogy. Here is how it works, in plain words. The DNA test used in CODIS looks at a small number of locations in your genome, around twenty, where a short piece of code repeats. That is enough to tell whether two samples came from the same person. But it is not nearly enough to find cousins. To find cousins, you need a much bigger picture. Genealogy tests look at hundreds of thousands of single points in the genome, places where one letter of the genetic code varies from person to person. Scientists call them single nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs, pronounced snips. Imagine the difference between knowing someone's phone number and having their entire family photo album. The phone number tells you if you have the right person. The photo album lets you see who they resemble, and who they are related to. When two people share long, identical stretches of those SNPs, it means they inherited those stretches from a common ancestor. The more they share, the closer the relationship. Siblings share about half. First cousins share about an eighth. Second cousins, people who share a set of great-grandparents, typically share around three percent. Genealogists measure those shared stretches in a unit called the centimorgan, and from the total, they can estimate how two people are related. The hard part is turning a crime scene sample into the kind of SNP profile that a genealogy database can read. Consumer companies work from fresh saliva. Police work from evidence that may be decades old, degraded, contaminated or tiny. And that is where a company in Texas came in. Othram was founded in 2018 in The Woodlands, north of Houston, the same year the Golden State Killer arrest made forensic genetic genealogy famous. Its scientists specialized in sequencing DNA from forensic evidence, the old, damaged, difficult samples that ordinary labs could not use. Instead of looking at twenty locations, they could read hundreds of thousands. In 2020, Othram had already helped solve one of the most famous cold cases in Texas. In Fort Worth, a seventeen-year-old named Carla Walker had been abducted and murdered in February 1974. Using a tiny amount of DNA from her clothing, Othram built a genealogy profile, and investigators followed the family trees to a man named Glen Samuel McCurley. He was arrested in September 2020, 46 years after the crime, and the following year he pleaded guilty to capital murder and was sentenced to life. That case was being worked in the same months that Mary Catherine Edwards' evidence arrived at the same lab. In April 2020, Othram received the DNA from Mary Catherine's case, the semen collected from her body in January 1995 and preserved ever since. This was, in one sense, an ideal kind of sample. It came from one man. It had been collected by professionals and stored with care. And it had been waiting for 25 years for a technology capable of reading it in a new way. Othram's scientists went to work. According to the forensic science publication The ISHI Report, the profile they built from Mary Catherine's case included more than half a million markers. Half a million. Compare that with the roughly twenty markers that had sat in CODIS for years, coming back empty every time. It was as if investigators had spent two decades trying to identify a stranger from a single blurry photograph, and were suddenly handed his family album. The next step was to compare that profile against genealogy databases where users had agreed to let their DNA be used to help identify the perpetrators of violent crimes and unidentified remains. That consent matters. After the Golden State Killer arrest, the rules changed. In 2019, GEDmatch began requiring its users to opt in before their data could be compared in law enforcement cases, and other services set out their own policies. The relatives found in cases like this one are people whose data was available for exactly this purpose under those rules. When the comparison ran, the database did not return the killer. It never does. It returns relatives, a list of strangers ranked by how much DNA they share with the unknown man, most of them distant, some closer. In Mary Catherine's case, according to Texas Monthly, the closest match was a woman living in Louisiana. Based on the amount of DNA she shared with the profile from the townhome, she appeared to be a second cousin of the man who had killed Mary Catherine Edwards. She had nothing to do with the crime. She may never have met him. But somewhere in the past, generations back, she and he shared a set of great-grandparents. And from that single shared couple, a team of investigators and genealogists was about to begin building a family tree, branch by branch, toward a name that had been hiding in plain sight since 1995.

The heroes of a genetic genealogy case rarely look like the heroes of a television drama. There is no car chase, no dramatic interrogation in the rain. There is a detective at a desk and a genealogist at a computer, and a screen full of names, dates, marriage records and obituaries. The work is slow. It is often frustrating. And in the Mary Catherine Edwards case, it was carried by people who simply refused to stop. The lead from Othram, the woman in Louisiana who appeared to be a second cousin of the unknown man, was passed to Beaumont police detective Aaron Lewallen. Working alongside him was a genealogist named Shera LaPoint, whose role was described in The ISHI Report, a publication that follows forensic DNA work. Together with the Texas Rangers, they began the work that every genetic genealogy case requires. Here is what that work looks like. If a woman is your second cousin, it means that one of her great-grandparents is also one of yours. The first task is to build her family tree backward, generation by generation, until you find her four pairs of great-grandparents. One of those couples is the link. But you do not yet know which one. So you build forward. You take each couple and trace all of their descendants: every child, every grandchild, every great-grandchild, every marriage, every move, every birth announcement and every obituary. Old newspapers help. Public records help. Social media helps. Other DNA matches, more distant ones, help confirm which branch is the right one. Slowly, out of hundreds of names, a few branches begin to stand out, families that lived in the right part of the country, with sons who were the right age to have been an adult man in Beaumont in 1995. It is like assembling a puzzle without the picture on the box. One piece at a time, you narrow it down. The Beaumont Enterprise later reported that the work of DNA testing and genealogy in this case went on for years. In Mary Catherine's case, the branches eventually led to one family, and inside that family, to one man. His name was Clayton Bernard Foreman. He was born around 1960, which made him about 35 years old in January 1995, and 61 by the spring of 2021. And when investigators began to look into who he was, the story they found was almost impossible to believe. Clayton Foreman had grown up in Beaumont. He had graduated from Forest Park High School in 1978. He had gone to the same high school as Mary Catherine Edwards, and her twin sister Allison, and their close friend Dianna Coe. And in 1982, Clayton Foreman had married Dianna Coe. Remember that wedding. At that ceremony, Mary Catherine and Allison Edwards were Dianna's bridesmaids. The man whose DNA had been sealed in a sexual assault kit for 26 years was the groom. The bride's two friends, the twins, had stood beside her at the ceremony. Thirteen years after that day, one of those bridesmaids was dead, and the man she had seen exchange vows with her friend had never once been named as a person of interest. Investigators kept digging, and they found something else. Before that wedding, in 1981, Clayton Foreman had been accused of attacking a young woman in Beaumont, a former high school classmate who was nineteen years old at the time. He had pleaded guilty to aggravated assault. He had received three years of deferred adjudication, a form of probation. Years later, that woman would describe what happened to her in a courtroom, and the details would sound terribly familiar. Her hands, she said, had been bound behind her back. By the time investigators found him, Clayton Foreman no longer lived in Texas. He was living in Reynoldsburg, a suburb on the east side of Columbus, in Franklin County, Ohio. According to one local report, he was working as a rideshare driver, picking up passengers in his own car. He had built a life far from Beaumont, roughly a thousand miles from the townhome where Mary Catherine had died. His first wife, Dianna Coe, would later tell the CBS program 48 Hours about things she remembered from their marriage, things that took on a different meaning once she learned what investigators had found. She recalled that he kept a billy club by the bed, which he said was for protection. And she remembered that he had ordered handcuffs, and that he had hung them over the rearview mirror of his car. She also remembered the moment she heard about Mary Catherine's death. She called him, she said, crying, and told him that somebody had murdered Catherine. According to her account to 48 Hours, he answered with two words, and no emotion at all. Oh, really? Consider the two lives side by side. In January 1995, Mary Catherine Edwards was 31 years old, a teacher in the middle of a school year. Clayton Foreman was about 35. In the spring of 2021, Mary Catherine was still 31, as she would be forever, frozen in the photographs her family kept. Had she lived, she would have been in her late fifties, perhaps still teaching, perhaps retired, perhaps with a classroom of former students who now had children of their own. Clayton Foreman was 61. He had lived all of those years. He had driven strangers around a city in Ohio. He had grown older, the way Mary Catherine never could. And through all that time, as a jury would later find, he was the man who had walked out of that townhome in Beaumont and gone on with his life. For anyone who had followed the case, the identification answered one question and opened a harder one. The fear that had shadowed the case since 1995, that a police officer might be responsible, could finally be set aside. The truth was in some ways harder to hear. It had been someone from inside her own circle of memories. But a family tree, however convincing, is not proof. Forensic genetic genealogy produces a lead, not a conviction. To make a case that could stand up in court, investigators needed Clayton Foreman's DNA, and they needed to compare it directly with the sample from 1995. They could not simply ask him for it. That would warn him. So they did what investigators in the Golden State Killer case had done, and what detectives in Fort Worth had done in the Carla Walker case. They waited for him to throw something away. In the United States, courts have generally held that once you put your garbage out at the curb for collection, you no longer have a reasonable expectation of privacy in it. Investigators collected discarded items from the trash outside Foreman's home in Ohio and sent them for testing. The result was recorded in the arrest affidavit, in the flat language of police paperwork. DNA collected from the trash run of Clayton Foreman's residence is a match to the DNA extracted from the semen collected from Edwards' body in 1995. After 26 years, the DNA in that sealed kit finally had a name. Not a stranger. Not a police officer. A man from Mary Catherine's own high school. Her friend's husband. The groom at a wedding where she had been a bridesmaid. All that remained was to go and get him.

In late April 2021, investigators from Texas went to Ohio. The Jefferson County Sheriff's Office aviation unit flew them to Reynoldsburg. According to the Texas Department of Public Safety, Clayton Bernard Foreman was arrested on April 26, 2021, in Franklin County, Ohio. Some news reports gave the date as April 29. Either way, it had been more than 26 years since the Saturday when Mary Catherine's family climbed the stairs of her townhome. By the calendar, the arrest came 9,599 days after that Saturday. Foreman was 61 years old. He was charged with capital murder. Around the time of his arrest, Foreman sat down with two investigators: Texas Ranger Brandon Bess and Beaumont police detective Aaron Lewallen. The conversation was recorded on video, and it lasted about 50 minutes. According to coverage of the trial, it began as a conversation, not as a formal interrogation. In that interview, Foreman acknowledged that Mary Catherine and her twin sister had been bridesmaids at his 1982 wedding. But he told investigators that, beyond the wedding, he had not had any interactions with them. Later, his defense attorney would argue that the interview had violated Foreman's constitutional rights and had been conducted under false pretenses. The judge would disagree, and the jury would watch it. In Beaumont, the news broke quickly. The case that had been featured on the Rangers' unsolved list, the case that some people in the city had nearly forgotten and others had never stopped thinking about, finally had a name attached to it. And the detail that every news outlet reached for was the same one. The victim had been a bridesmaid at his wedding. National outlets picked up the story. Fox News ran the headline that DNA linked an Ohio man to the 1995 murder of a bridesmaid in his wedding. For Mary Catherine's family, the news meant that the question they had carried since 1995 had an answer. It also meant that the answer was someone they knew. Foreman did not come back to Texas right away. According to reporting at the time, he appeared to contest extradition, the legal process by which one state hands over a person charged with a crime to another state. An Ohio judge ordered that he be returned to Jefferson County by July 6, or be released. According to one report, he was transported to the Jefferson County jail on June 15, 2021. On September 22, 2021, a Jefferson County grand jury indicted Clayton Bernard Foreman for capital murder. District Attorney Bob Wortham credited the Beaumont Police Department and Chief Jim Singletary for the breakthrough, and the Beaumont Enterprise reported that his office was weighing whether to seek the death penalty. In Texas, capital murder is the most serious charge in the criminal code. It applies to certain kinds of murder, including a murder committed in the course of another serious felony such as aggravated sexual assault. For an adult, it carries only two possible punishments: death, or life in prison. In the end, the case went to trial with life in prison as the result of a conviction. Then came the wait. Cases like this one take time to prepare, especially when the evidence is nearly three decades old and every piece of forensic work can be challenged. Almost three more years passed. On Monday, March 11, 2024, jury selection began in the Criminal District Court of Jefferson County, before Judge John Stevens. Foreman was 63 years old. His defense attorney was Tom Burbank. Testimony began the next day, Tuesday, March 12, and the very first thing the jury heard was that 911 call from January 14, 1995. My sister's dead. Allison Edwards Brocato took the stand. She told the jurors how close she and her twin had been. Through tears, she described the day her sister died. She told them that Mary Catherine had loved her students dearly, that she called them all her kids. The prosecution showed the jury the evidence that had been stored since 1995: the police-grade handcuffs, the T-shirt, the sexual assault kit. Forensic witnesses walked the jurors through the DNA evidence, the science that had carried a sealed sample from 1995 all the way to a name. KFDM's coverage put the spotlight squarely on that DNA testimony. Dianna Coe, Foreman's first wife, was part of the story the jury heard, as she was in the reporting that followed. And then the prosecution called a witness from 1981. The woman who had been nineteen that year took the stand. She testified that Clayton Foreman had bound her hands behind her back with a belt, and had sexually assaulted her at knifepoint in his car before driving her home. She also told the jury that, at the time, she had agreed to allow him to plead guilty to aggravated assault, the plea that ended with three years of deferred adjudication. Under Texas law, evidence of another crime is generally not allowed simply to show that a defendant is a bad person. But it can be admitted for other purposes, such as proving identity, intent or motive, especially when the earlier act shares distinctive features with the crime on trial. The judge allowed the jury to hear her. Later, an appeals court would explain why, listing twelve specific similarities between what happened to her in 1981 and what happened to Mary Catherine Edwards in 1995. Then there was the recorded interview from 2021. The defense had fought to keep it out. Under the rule that comes from the Supreme Court's 1966 decision in Miranda versus Arizona, a person who is in custody must be warned of the right to remain silent and the right to a lawyer before police question them, and statements taken in violation of that rule can be excluded. The defense argued that Foreman's rights had been violated and that he had been drawn into the conversation under false pretenses. Judge Stevens ruled that the video could be shown. The jury watched it. They heard Foreman say that the twins had been bridesmaids at his wedding, and that there had been nothing more. The state rested. The defense called no witnesses. Clayton Foreman did not testify. On Wednesday, March 20, 2024, the case went to the jury. Local station 12News summed up that day in three numbers. Twenty-nine years of waiting. Seven days of testimony. About fifty minutes of deliberation. Another report put the deliberation at 52 minutes. Either way, it took the jury less than an hour. Guilty of capital murder. One of the jurors later spoke to 12News about how the panel had reached its verdict so quickly. Because the state had not sought the death penalty, the sentence was automatic. Judge John Stevens sentenced Clayton Bernard Foreman to life in prison, in the custody of the Texas Department of Criminal Justice. Under the Texas law that applied to capital murders committed in the mid-1990s, a person sentenced to life must serve forty calendar years before becoming eligible for parole. Local reporting put Foreman's earliest parole eligibility in 2061, when he would be about a hundred years old, though some coverage described it as thirty years. For the man who had been in his twenties in 1981, in his thirties in 1995, and in his sixties at last in a courtroom, the verdict closed one chapter. But for the family who had waited 29 years to hear that word spoken aloud, the story was not quite over. There was an appeal still to come.

After the verdict, the Edwards family spoke briefly, through a family spokesperson, as many families of murder victims do after decades of waiting, and the words they chose were careful ones. The spokesperson told 12News that it had been a long, emotional journey to finally see justice and closure. A long, emotional journey. That was how the family summed up 29 years. It is worth sitting with those words for a moment, because they leave out so much. They leave out the Saturday in 1995, the stairs, the bathroom, the call to 911. They leave out the anniversaries, the years when the case seemed to have been forgotten by everyone except the people who could never forget it. They leave out a twin sister who lived more than half her life without the person who had shared her face. And they leave out the strange, painful knowledge that the man responsible was not a stranger, but someone who had once been part of their world, a friend's husband from a wedding long ago. That is the restraint of people who have been through something unbearable and have decided that they will not let it define them in public. Later that year, in November 2024, the CBS program 48 Hours broadcast an hour on the case, reported by correspondent Natalie Morales, under the title Tracking the Killer of Mary Catherine Edwards. In it, Dianna Coe described the billy club by the bed and the handcuffs hanging from the rearview mirror, and detective Aaron Lewallen walked viewers through what the investigators had found. CBS headlined one of its articles with the twist that had stunned so many people: the victim had been a bridesmaid at her killer's wedding. The episode aired again in January 2026. And a national audience, most of whom had never heard of Beaumont, Texas, learned the name Mary Catherine Edwards. Clayton Foreman appealed. His lawyers raised several arguments before the Ninth Court of Appeals, which sits in Beaumont. They argued that the jury should never have heard about the attack in 1981, that his rights under Miranda had been violated during the interview in 2021, and that the trial court had allowed hearsay evidence that should have been kept out. The appeals court rejected all of those claims. In a decision reported by 12News in early 2026, the court upheld his conviction. It found that the evidence of the 1981 assault had been properly admitted, not to show that Foreman was a bad person, but because it was relevant to identity, intent and motive. And the court laid out its reasoning in a way that echoed everything this story has been about. It listed twelve specific similarities between what happened to a nineteen-year-old woman in 1981 and what happened to Mary Catherine Edwards in 1995. As of October 2026, Clayton Bernard Foreman is serving a sentence of life in prison in the custody of the Texas Department of Criminal Justice. His conviction for capital murder has been affirmed on appeal. According to local reporting, he will not be eligible for parole until 2061. So what changed because of this case? Some of the answer is in the science. Forensic genetic genealogy has gone from an experimental technique in 2018 to one of the most powerful tools in American cold case work. The same lab that worked Mary Catherine's case in The Woodlands has since been credited with helping to solve hundreds of cases, enough that Texas Monthly wrote a long feature about it, built around her story. In June 2026, the United States Senate passed a bill named after Carla Walker, the Fort Worth teenager whose 1974 murder Othram also helped solve, to create a pilot program funding forensic genetic genealogy for investigators. As of October 2026, it had not been voted on by the House of Representatives. And some of the answer is in the lesson that the Edwards case teaches about the past. The man who killed Mary Catherine had already been in the criminal justice system once, in 1981, for an attack with striking similarities. He pleaded guilty to aggravated assault, received three years of deferred adjudication, and walked out into the rest of his life. There was no DNA database to hold his profile. There was no system to connect a pair of handcuffs in a townhome to a belt in a car fourteen years earlier. Today, many states collect DNA from people convicted of serious crimes, and some from people arrested for them. Cases from before those laws remain the hardest to solve, because the people responsible were never entered into any database. That is why genealogy matters. It reaches the people that the system missed. And some of the answer is in people. A district attorney who, on his first day in office, picked up the phone and asked for the case to be worked again. A Beaumont detective, Aaron Lewallen, and a Texas Ranger, Brandon Bess, who followed a genealogy lead across state lines and sat down face to face with the man it pointed to. A genealogist, Shera LaPoint, who helped build the family trees out of a single match in Louisiana. The scientists at Othram, who read half a million markers from a sample that had waited a quarter of a century. The officers who, in January 1995, collected and sealed the evidence correctly. And a woman who had been nineteen in 1981, who walked into a courtroom more than four decades later and told a jury what had been done to her, so that the jury could understand what had been done to Mary Catherine. Every one of them mattered. There are also questions this documentary cannot answer, and will not pretend to. We do not know exactly what happened in Mary Catherine's townhome on the night of January 13, 1995, beyond what the evidence and the verdict established. We do not know what passed between her and the man who came to her door. Clayton Foreman did not testify, and he has never given a public account. Some silences are permanent. So let us go back to where this story began. Saturday, January 14, 1995. A family in Beaumont, Texas, keeps calling a phone that nobody answers. They do not yet know what they are about to find. They do not know that the answer will take 26 years, a lab north of Houston, a second cousin in Louisiana, and a bag of trash at a curb in Ohio. They do not know that the man responsible was in their daughter's past, at a wedding where she stood beside a close friend in 1982. They only know that Mary Catherine is not picking up. Mary Catherine Edwards was not a case number. She was a daughter. She was a twin. She was a friend who stood up for her friend at the altar. She was a teacher at Price Elementary who loved her students and called them all her kids. She was 31 years old. She deserved a long life, a full classroom every fall, and the chance to grow old beside the sister who shared her face. Instead, she was taken by someone she knew, and it took more than a quarter of a century for anyone to say his name. If there is one lesson in her story, it is this. Evidence that is kept with care can outlast silence. A sealed kit, a pair of handcuffs, a recording of a family's worst moment, all of it waited, and when the science was finally ready, it spoke.
