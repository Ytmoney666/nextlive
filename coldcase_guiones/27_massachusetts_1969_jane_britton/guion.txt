Jane was lying face down on her bed. Her body was partly covered by blankets and by a fur coat. She was not moving. The two young men who found her were not police officers or doctors. The facts that were established in the following days can be said simply, and they should be said simply. Jane Britton had been sexually assaulted. And there was the powder. Investigators found red ochre sprinkled on Jane's body and around the apartment. To most people in 1969, the words red ochre meant nothing at all. To the people in Jane's world, they meant a great deal. Red ochre is a natural earth pigment, a clay colored by iron oxide, the same mineral that gives rust its color. Human beings have used it for an extraordinarily long time, to paint cave walls, to decorate their bodies and their tools, and in some ancient cultures to sprinkle on the dead as part of a burial. Archaeologists have found red ochre in graves tens of thousands of years old, from Europe to Africa to the Americas. Any student of archaeology at Harvard would have known that. Any professor certainly did. So the moment the detail became public, it lit a fuse. A murdered archaeology student. A red powder used in ancient burials. A famous university. The newspapers did not need much more than that. Within days there was talk in the press of a primitive rite, of some kind of ritual killing, as if the crime had been staged by someone who wanted to recreate an ancient burial in a modern bedroom. It was a sensational idea, and like many sensational ideas, it travelled much faster than the facts. The police and anthropologists at Harvard rejected the ritual theory. But it never went away. Once it had been printed, it was out in the world. It would be repeated in conversations and articles and student lore for the next half century. Many years later, accounts of the case would suggest a far more ordinary possibility. Jane painted. And according to later reporting, the red powder in her apartment may simply have come from her own art materials. That explanation has never been confirmed in a court or in an official statement, and it should be treated with care. But it is a reminder of how easily an ordinary object can become a sinister symbol when people want a story. Inside the apartment, the investigators did what investigators did in 1969. They photographed the scene. They looked for fingerprints. They noted the windows, one of which, according to later accounts, opened onto a fire escape. They talked to the neighbors, to the boyfriend, to the friends who had been with Jane the evening before. And at the autopsy, they collected biological evidence on swabs, the ordinary, routine samples that were taken in sexual assault cases at the time. That routine step is the most important thing that happened in this case for the next forty-eight years. It is worth understanding what those swabs could and could not do in 1969. Forensic science at the time could look at blood types. It could sometimes say that a sample came from a person with type A blood, or type O, which might narrow a list of suspects or exclude someone, but could never point to a single individual. There was no DNA testing. The word DNA was known to scientists, the structure of the molecule had been described in 1953, but the idea of reading a person's genetic identity from a stain on a swab was science fiction. The first DNA fingerprinting technique would not be developed until 1984, in England. The first time DNA would be used to solve a murder was still nearly two decades away. So the swabs from Jane's autopsy were collected, labeled, and stored. And then they waited. Not for weeks or months. For decades. Remember those swabs. Small pieces of cotton, carrying a trace too small for anyone in 1969 to read, sitting in evidence storage while presidents came and went, while the Cold War ended, while the computers that would one day run the national DNA databases were still being invented. Half a century later, the Massachusetts State Police Crime Lab would confirm that the profile which finally broke this case came from the remaining DNA on those original swabs. Not from a new witness. Not from a confession. From the routine samples collected at the autopsy in January 1969, by people who could never have known what they were saving. Meanwhile, the scene itself told the investigators very little in a way they could use. There were many people who had been inside that apartment, students and friends and neighbors, and any of their traces could have been there innocently. There were open windows. There was a door that was found unlocked. There was a red powder that seemed to mean everything and might have meant nothing. There was no weapon. In the newspapers of January 1969, the story had everything that sells a newspaper. The victim was young, educated and from a respected family, the daughter of a Radcliffe vice president. The setting was Harvard. The detail was exotic. Reporters went looking for anyone who knew her, and the anthropology department, which until that week had been a small and private world, suddenly found itself on the front pages. One thing about the scene would, over time, grow into a legend of its own. That version of the story is simply false. Jane was found in her own bed, in her own apartment on University Road, by her boyfriend and her neighbor. She was not found in a museum. The red ochre was real, but the ritual was invented. It is one example of how, in the years that followed, the true story and the told story drifted further and further apart. For Jane's parents, none of that mattered. Their daughter was dead. Her father, a senior administrator at the women's college she had graduated from, now had to face colleagues and students who were reading about his child in the newspapers. Her younger brother, Boyd, had lost his sister. Whatever the police found or did not find, whatever the newspapers said or did not say, nothing would bring her back. And for the investigators, the first and most urgent question was the one that every detective asks in a case like this. Who had a reason. Who had access. Who was close to her. Those were reasonable questions. But in this case, the answers they pointed to were all inside the small, closed world of Jane's department. And that is where the investigation turned its eyes.

Every murder investigation begins with circles. In the centre is the victim. In the first circle are the people closest to her, family, partner, friends, the people who saw her last. In the next circle are the people she worked or studied with, the neighbors, the acquaintances. Further out are the strangers. Detectives work from the centre outwards, because statistically that is where the answer most often lies. In 1969, in Cambridge, the circles around Jane Britton were crowded, and they were crowded with anthropologists. Her boyfriend was an anthropology student. Her neighbor across the hall was an anthropology student. The friends she had dinner with on her last evening were anthropology students. The people she had spent the previous summer with, in Iran, were anthropologists. The building where she was supposed to be sitting an exam that morning was the anthropology museum. And the strangest physical clue in the room, a red powder used in ancient burials, seemed to speak the language of anthropology. So it is not surprising that the investigation turned inward, towards the department. What is surprising is how long it stayed there, in the minds of the public, after the official investigation ran out of road. As in any case of this kind, the people closest to Jane were the first to be asked hard questions. That is standard, and it is not an accusation. It is what investigators do. Her boyfriend, the friends who had been with her that evening, the neighbors who had seen her last, the students and teachers who knew her, all of them became part of the file. Accounts of the case written decades later describe an investigation that looked closely at people inside her academic world, at fellow students and at members of the faculty. Nobody was ever charged. No one in that world was ever proven to have had anything to do with her death. In 2018, the District Attorney's office would say that DNA testing had allowed investigators to exclude other potential persons of interest before they ever reached the man they finally identified. But in 1969, there was no DNA test to exclude anyone. There was only suspicion, and suspicion, once it attaches itself to a person, is very hard to remove. Think about what that meant for the people in that department in the weeks after the murder. They were grieving a friend. They were also, in a sense, under a cloud. Reporters wanted to talk to them. Investigators wanted to talk to them. And among themselves, they began to talk, too, about who might have done it, about what the red powder meant, about what they had noticed or thought they had noticed about Jane's life in the weeks before. That kind of talk is natural. It is also how rumors are made. And the rumor that grew out of this case would attach itself, over the years, to one man in particular. It said that Jane had been involved with a professor in her department, and that he had killed her, and that the university had protected him. In time, the rumor named Karl Lamberg-Karlovsky, the archaeology professor who had led the dig in Iran where Jane had worked the summer before. It is important to be precise here, because this is exactly where a story like this can do real harm. Karl Lamberg-Karlovsky was never charged with any crime in connection with Jane Britton's death. The rumor was a rumor. It was passed from student to student over decades, and it was believed by many people who had no evidence for it at all. And when, in 2018, science finally produced an answer, the DNA did not point to him. It did not point to anyone in the department. It pointed to a stranger. Reviewers of the book that later investigated the rumor in depth were clear that, by the end of it, the rumor proves false. It is one thing to know that now. It was another thing to live under that shadow for half a century. Why did the investigation not move outwards, towards strangers. Part of the answer is simply the science of the time. Without DNA, without a national database, without computers to connect cases across jurisdictions, a stranger who left no fingerprint and no witness was almost impossible to find. Detectives could only search for a stranger if they already had a name, and there was no name. Part of the answer was the physical evidence, which seemed to point inwards. An unlocked door, a red pigment from the world of archaeology, a victim surrounded by colleagues. And part of the answer was the story itself. A murder with a ritual flavor, at Harvard, in an anthropology department, was simply more interesting to the public than the possibility that a young woman had been attacked in her bed by a man who had climbed in through a window. Becky Cooper, the writer who later spent years on this case, would say in an interview that whenever she followed the theories about particular suspects, the satisfying narrative tended to fall apart once it was examined closely. That observation could stand as a summary of the first decades of this case. Theories were easy. Proof was impossible. And every year that a theory went unproven, it did not fade. It hardened. A guess told once becomes a possibility. Told a hundred times, it starts to sound like history. So the months passed, and then the years. The newspapers moved on to other stories. The students who had sat the exams that January took their degrees and scattered across the world, to universities and museums and excavations of their own. Some of them would go on to distinguished careers. Some of them carried the memory of that week for the rest of their lives, along with the uncomfortable knowledge that people had once wondered about them. The Cambridge police did not solve the case. No grand jury indictment was ever announced. No trial was ever held. The file went into a cabinet, the swabs went into storage, and Jane Britton's name became, in the public mind, the name of an unsolved Harvard murder. And all the while, the circles that investigators had drawn around her stopped at the edge of her department. Outside those circles, beyond the students and the professors and the neighbors, Cambridge was full of people who had nothing to do with anthropology. Some of them had grown up in the city. Some of them worked near Harvard Square. Some of them knew the streets and the back alleys and the fire escapes as well as any student knew the stacks of the library. None of them was in Jane's file. And in the years after her death, a series of violent crimes against women would take place in Cambridge and Boston that, at the time, no one thought to connect to University Road. One of them involved a woman who was attacked by a man she had met at the Harvard Square subway station, only a few blocks from Jane's apartment. The man was arrested. He was convicted. And his name went into the records of the criminal justice system, where it would sit, unnoticed by anyone working on Jane's case, for decades. He was not a scholar. He had never set foot in a trench in Iran. And he was exactly the kind of person no one in 1969 was looking for.

The years that followed Jane's death are the years in which nothing happened, at least on paper. That is what a cold case is. Not a dramatic chase, but a long silence in which life goes on for everyone except the person at the centre of the file. In the 1970s, the students who had known Jane finished their degrees. The excavation in Iran that she had helped to dig continued, season after season, until 1975. Harvard changed. Radcliffe changed. The country changed. And the case of the murdered anthropology student slowly became something people talked about rather than something anyone was actively solving. But this case is not only about what did not happen. It is also about what was happening, at the very same time, in the streets around Harvard and Boston, and that no one connected to Jane. In January 1972, almost exactly three years after Jane's death, a 23-year-old woman named Ellen Rutchick was raped and killed in her apartment on Beacon Street in Boston. She was the same age Jane had been. Her case, too, went cold. In December 1973, on the twelfth of the month, a 24-year-old woman named Mary Lee McClain was sexually assaulted and killed in her apartment on Beacon Hill. Her case went cold as well. Two young women, killed in their own homes, in neighborhoods across the river from Cambridge. In the same years, a man was arrested for attacking a woman he had met at the Harvard Square subway station. And in 1975, a woman was raped in Boston, and this time the attacker was caught, convicted, and sentenced to fifteen to twenty years in prison. These events lived in different files, in different police departments, in different counties. Cambridge is in Middlesex County. Boston is in Suffolk County. The detectives on one side of the river had no reason, and no tool, to see the cases on the other side as part of a single pattern. Nobody, in the 1970s, could take a trace of biological evidence from a crime scene in Boston and compare it to a trace from a crime scene in Cambridge and say that the same man had been in both rooms. Remember that. The ledger of this case is not a ledger of laziness. It is a ledger of blindness, the kind of blindness that comes from having the evidence in your hands and no way to read it. Here is that ledger, in plain numbers. Jane was killed in January 1969. The first DNA fingerprinting method was developed in 1984, fifteen years later. The FBI's national DNA index, the system known as CODIS, began operating nationwide in 1998, twenty-nine years after Jane's death. In the 1990s, states including Massachusetts began collecting DNA from people convicted of serious crimes, which meant that a man serving a long prison sentence for rape would eventually have his genetic profile stored in a database. Each of those steps brought the answer closer. None of them, on its own, was enough. Because the other half of the equation was the evidence from Jane's case, and that evidence was old, small, and degraded. Meanwhile, the family waited. Jane's parents grew old without an answer. By the time the case was finally closed, in 2018, the person who spoke for the family was her brother Boyd. Reports at the time described him as Jane's surviving brother. Whatever her parents had hoped for, they did not live to hear the name. That is one of the quiet cruelties of a case that stays cold for half a century. The people who most needed the answer are often the ones who never receive it. And inside Harvard, the rumor kept going. Year after year, new students arrived, and some of them heard the story of the anthropology graduate student who had been murdered in 1969. In the version that circulated, she had been involved with a professor, she had been killed by him, her body had been found in the museum covered with red ochre, and the university had covered it all up. Some parts of that version were simply wrong. She was not found in the museum. Other parts were never supported by any evidence. But a story does not need evidence to survive. It only needs to be retold. In 2009, an undergraduate named Becky Cooper heard the story for the first time. She could not let it go. A few years later, in 2012, she came back to Harvard and, as part of trying to understand the world Jane had lived in, she audited a class taught by the very professor the rumor had named. She later wrote that she felt as if she were living inside a giant spider web, trying to learn the truth without alarming the people she was asking about. Over the following years, she interviewed Jane's friends, her family, her classmates, her former boyfriend, and every law enforcement officer still living who had worked on the case. She went looking for the files. And that is where she ran into a wall. The Middlesex District Attorney's office, which had authority over the case, refused to release much more than old newspaper clippings. The reason it gave was that the investigation into Jane's murder was still active. In June 2017, the Boston Globe reported on the fight. The official in charge of administering the state's public records law had repeatedly ordered the District Attorney's office either to release portions of the documents or to give a better explanation of why they had to be withheld. Cooper, along with a man named Michael Widmer, kept pressing. Cooper's view, as she later described it, was that to argue the case was still active, the District Attorney's office needed to actually examine whatever evidence it had that could still identify a culprit. Whether or not that pressure was the reason, it is a fact that the evidence in Jane's case was tested again in this period. And the timing matters. By then, other cold cases in Boston had already moved. In 2005, the family of Ellen Rutchick had asked the Boston Police Cold Case Squad to look again at her murder. Investigators had a laboratory isolate a genetic profile from old microscope slides, and in July 2009 that profile hit on a profile already stored in the national database. The Suffolk District Attorney's office was told of the match in 2010. In 2012, a grant-funded cold case review produced a second hit in the murder of Mary Lee McClain, and on October 18, 2012, the Suffolk District Attorney and the Boston Police Commissioner announced the name of the man they said had been responsible. Both times, it was the same name. Both times, the man was already dead. And both times, the announcements came from the other side of the river, about cases that no one publicly connected to a murder in Cambridge in 1969. So by 2012, a name sat in the files of the Suffolk County District Attorney, linked by DNA to two murders of young women in their own apartments in the early 1970s. Across the river, Jane's swabs sat in storage. The science to read them was getting closer every year. It was only a matter of whether there was anything left on those old pieces of cotton to read.

To understand how this case was finally broken, we need to step away from Cambridge for a few minutes and talk about the science, because the science is the quiet hero of this story. Every human being carries a genetic code inside nearly every cell of the body. Most of that code is the same from one person to the next. But there are small regions where people differ, places where a short sequence of genetic letters repeats itself a different number of times in different people. Scientists call these short tandem repeats, or STRs. Think of them as a row of beads on a string, where one person has seven red beads in a row and another person has eleven. Look at enough of those places at once, and the combination becomes almost unique to a single person. That is a modern DNA profile. In 1984, a British scientist named Alec Jeffreys discovered a way to read differences like these and realized they could identify individuals. Within a few years, his discovery was used in a double murder investigation in the English county of Leicestershire. It cleared an innocent young man who had confessed, and it led police to the real killer. The world of criminal investigation was never the same again. In the decades that followed, DNA would free innocent people from prison, identify killers in cases thought hopeless, and give names to the unnamed dead. But each of those successes depended on the same simple condition. Somebody, at some point, had to have kept the evidence. In the United States, the FBI built a system called CODIS, the Combined DNA Index System, to store profiles from crime scenes and from people convicted of crimes, so that a profile found at one scene could be searched against thousands, and eventually millions, of others. The national level of that index began operating in 1998. From then on, if a man convicted of a serious crime had his DNA taken in prison, and if that same man had left his DNA at an old crime scene, the database could, in principle, connect the two. In principle. Because there is a problem with very old evidence in sexual assault cases, and it is a problem that kept Jane's case cold long after DNA testing became routine. A swab taken from a victim of a sexual assault almost always contains a mixture. Most of the DNA on it comes from the victim herself. Only a small amount, sometimes a tiny amount, comes from the attacker. In fresh samples, laboratories have ways to separate the two. But in a sample that is decades old, that has dried and aged and broken down over time, the attacker's small contribution can be completely swamped by the victim's own DNA. The laboratory tries to read the profile and sees mostly her. That is where a different kind of test comes in. It is called Y-STR testing. The Y chromosome is the one that only males carry. It is passed from father to son, almost unchanged, generation after generation. A Y-STR test reads only the markers on that chromosome. And because the victim, a woman, has no Y chromosome at all, her DNA is simply invisible to the test. It is like turning off all the lights in a room except the one you are looking for. Whatever male DNA is on the swab, however small, suddenly stands out. There is a catch, and it is an important one, and it is part of why this case was handled the way it was. Because the Y chromosome is passed down the father's line almost unchanged, a Y-STR profile is not unique to one man. A man, his brothers, his father, his sons and his paternal cousins can all share the same Y-STR profile. It works a little like a family surname. It can tell you which family line a man belongs to, but on its own it cannot always tell you which man in that line it was. So a Y-STR result is a powerful lead, but it usually needs to be confirmed. Investigators call a match of this kind a soft hit, a strong indication that has to be tested further before anyone can call it an identification. Now come back to Cambridge. For almost half a century, the swabs taken at Jane's autopsy in January 1969 had been preserved. They had survived the retirement of detectives and the passing of almost everyone who had first handled them. They had survived the decades when nobody was working on the case at all. Whatever attempts had been made over the years to read them had not produced a profile that could identify anyone. And then, in October 2017, the Massachusetts State Police Crime Lab tried again, using the Y-STR method. For the first time in the history of the case, the lab obtained a male-specific DNA profile from the remaining material on the original swabs. Forty-eight years and nine months after Jane's death, the trace left behind in her room finally said something. That success came at a cost. The sample was tiny. Becky Cooper, who had followed every step of the case, later said that this last round of testing used up the remaining crime-scene DNA. There would be no second chance. If the profile was wrong, or if it led nowhere, there was nothing left on those swabs to try again with. It was the last reading of the last trace of the only physical witness in the case. So the profile existed. The next question was who it belonged to. Before a profile can name anyone, it has to be compared with something. A crime lab can compare a Y-STR profile from a crime scene with known samples, such as samples taken from suspects, or samples taken from convicted offenders and stored by the state. And there is something else Y-STR testing can do, which mattered here. It can exclude. If a man's Y-STR profile is different from the crime scene profile, he cannot be the source. According to reporting on the case, DNA testing allowed investigators to exclude other potential persons of interest before they reached the man they eventually identified. Think about what that means for the people who had lived under the shadow of this case for decades, the students, the neighbors, the professor named in the rumor. For almost fifty years, there had been no way to clear them. Suspicion could not be disproven. Now, the same science that could point to a killer could also, finally, point away from the innocent. So, through the end of 2017 and into 2018, the State Police lab worked with the profile from Jane's swabs. It compared that profile against samples it held from convicted offenders. And in July 2018, the lab notified investigators that it had found a match. The profile from the swabs taken in Cambridge in January 1969 matched the stored sample of a man who had spent years in the Massachusetts prison system. He was not an anthropologist. He was not a student, a professor, a neighbor or a friend. He was a man whose name had already appeared, years earlier, in two cold case announcements on the Boston side of the river. And the investigators now had to find out if they could prove it.

Every cold case that is finally broken has people behind it whose names rarely make the headlines. In this case, they included the scientists of the Massachusetts State Police Crime Lab, who coaxed a readable profile out of swabs that were nearly half a century old, and the investigators assigned to the Middlesex District Attorney's office who refused to treat Jane's file as history. One of them was a State Police sergeant named Peter Sennott. When the case was finally closed, it was Sennott whom Jane's brother Boyd singled out by name to thank. The family's statement also thanked the friends, the public officials and the members of the press who had persevered in keeping the investigation active. Those words matter. They are a reminder that a case like this is not solved by one flash of genius, but by a long chain of people who each refused to let it drop. And in this case, part of that chain was a writer who had first heard a rumor as an undergraduate, and who spent years pushing for the files, asking questions, and refusing to accept that Jane would be remembered only as a ghost story. In July 2018, the lab's notification arrived. The male DNA profile from Jane's swabs matched the stored sample of a man named Michael Sumpter. Here is who he was, according to the records released by the Middlesex District Attorney's office and the reporting that followed. Michael Eugene Sumpter was born in Boston on September 26, 1947. In January 1969, when Jane was killed, he was 21 years old, two years younger than she was. He was not a stranger to Cambridge. According to the District Attorney's office, he had lived in Cambridge as a young child and had attended first grade in the Cambridge public schools. As a juvenile, he had come into contact with the Cambridge police. In the late 1960s, he had a girlfriend who lived in Cambridge. And in 1967, less than two years before Jane's murder, he had worked on Arrow Street in Cambridge, approximately a mile from Jane's apartment on University Road. Arrow Street is a short street on the edge of Harvard Square. It is the kind of place a person could walk from to University Road without a second thought. He knew the neighborhood. He knew the streets. He was, in the language the District Attorney would later use, a career criminal with ties to Cambridge. And there was more. In 1972, three years after Jane's death, he was arrested for assaulting a woman he had met at the Harvard Square subway station, only blocks from her apartment, and he was later convicted. In 1975, he raped a woman in Boston, and for that crime he was sentenced to fifteen to twenty years in prison. That conviction is why his DNA was on file. And then there were the two names we heard earlier. Ellen Rutchick, 23, killed in her Beacon Street apartment in January 1972. Mary Lee McClain, 24, killed in her Beacon Hill apartment in December 1973. In 2010 and in 2012, the Suffolk County District Attorney's office had announced that DNA evidence linked Michael Sumpter to both of those killings. Reporting on the Britton announcement in 2018 said that, in total, Sumpter had been linked by DNA to four other sexual assaults, two of which ended in the deaths of the women. Like those cases, the case of Jane Britton would never come to trial. Because Michael Sumpter was already dead. He had died of cancer in 2001, on August 10, at the age of 54. Accounts differ on the exact circumstances. Some reports say he had been released on parole into hospice care at the end of his prison sentence for the 1975 rape. Others place his death in the correctional unit of a Boston hospital. Either way, the man whose stored sample matched the profile from Jane's swabs had been dead for seventeen years by the time the lab connected him to her. Consider the contrast. In January 1969, Jane was 23 and Sumpter was 21. Had they both lived, by the autumn of 2018 Jane would have been in her seventies, a retired scholar perhaps, with decades of fieldwork behind her. Sumpter would have been 71. Instead, one of them had been gone for almost half a century, and the other had died in 2001 without his name ever being publicly connected to University Road. He served his time for other crimes. For this one, he never answered at all, and now he never would. So the investigators faced a problem. A Y-STR match, as we saw, is powerful but not unique. It points to a family line, not necessarily to one man. To call this an identification, they needed to confirm it. And with Sumpter dead, they could not simply ask him for a sample. This is where the newest tool in the story appears. Investigators searched for Sumpter's relatives, and through the genealogy website Ancestry.com they located his brother. It is important to describe this accurately. This was not the long, branch-by-branch genetic genealogy search that has solved other famous cold cases. Investigators already had a name. What they needed was a living male relative from the same paternal line, someone whose DNA could be compared against the crime scene profile to strengthen the conclusion. Sumpter's brother cooperated with investigators. He provided a fresh DNA sample. According to the District Attorney's office and the reporting that followed, that sample further confirmed the match, and at the same time the brother himself was excluded as a possible contributor of the DNA found on Jane's swabs. In other words, the profile belonged to Sumpter's family line, and investigators were satisfied that the man in that line who had left it was Michael Sumpter. It is worth pausing on the brother's role. He had done nothing wrong. He was asked, decades later, to help confirm that his own brother had committed a terrible crime, and he agreed. Cases like this one depend on that kind of quiet cooperation from families who never asked to be part of the story. So now the picture was complete, as complete as it would ever be. A 21-year-old man with roots in Cambridge, who had worked about a mile from Jane's door, whose DNA, according to the District Attorney, matched the profile from the swabs taken at her autopsy. A man later convicted of an assault that began a few blocks from her apartment and of a rape in Boston. A man whom DNA had already linked, years earlier, to the deaths of two other young women in their own apartments. And a man who had never, as far as anyone has ever shown, met Jane Britton in his life. For almost fifty years, people had looked at the circle of anthropologists around Jane and asked which of them could have done it. The answer, according to the District Attorney, had been outside that circle all along, a stranger who knew the neighborhood, who came in through a window, and who left nothing behind that 1969 could read. What remained was to tell the world. And to say it in a way that the law would allow, because Michael Sumpter had never been charged with Jane's murder, and never would be.

On Tuesday, November 20, 2018, the Middlesex District Attorney, Marian Ryan, stood before reporters and announced the end of the oldest cold case her office had ever solved. It had been 49 years, ten months and thirteen days since Jane Britton was found on University Road. Jane had been found on a Tuesday. The answer came on a Tuesday too. In between lay almost the entire adult life of everyone who had known her. The young man who knocked on her door that afternoon was now almost half a century older, and he had lived all of those years with the memory of what he found behind it. The students who had sat the exams that morning had built whole careers and retired from them. The Radcliffe that her father had helped to run had, by then, been fully merged into Harvard. A whole world had passed. Ryan laid out the story the way prosecutors lay out a case. The swabs from the 1969 autopsy. The Y-STR profile obtained by the State Police Crime Lab in October 2017, for the first time in the history of the case. The notification in July 2018 that the profile matched the stored sample of Michael Sumpter. The brother located through Ancestry.com, his cooperation, his fresh sample, the further confirmation, and his own exclusion as a possible contributor. Then she described what her office believed had happened that night. According to the District Attorney, Sumpter entered Jane's apartment through a window, assaulted her, and murdered her in her bed before fleeing the building. She described his ties to Cambridge, his childhood in the city, his job on Arrow Street about a mile away, his girlfriend in Cambridge in the late 1960s, his later conviction for an assault that began at the Harvard Square subway station. And she said, in words that were quoted by news organizations across the country, quote, As a result of their perseverance and the utilization of the latest advances in forensic technology by the Massachusetts State Police Crime Lab, today I am confident that we are able to say that the mystery of who killed Jane Britton has finally been solved, end quote. Reports that day described it as the oldest case her office had been able to solve. Now we need to be very careful, because this is the point where many true crime stories stop being careful. Michael Sumpter was never charged with the murder of Jane Britton. He was never arrested for it. He was never tried. No jury ever heard the evidence. No defense lawyer ever cross-examined the scientists or challenged the methods. No judge ever ruled on whether the DNA could be admitted. He died in 2001, seventeen years before his name was connected to her, and the law does not put the dead on trial. In the eyes of the law, a person who has not been convicted of a crime is presumed innocent of it. That principle does not disappear because a person is dead, or because a prosecutor is confident, or because the evidence seems strong. So the accurate way to describe this case is the way we have described it. Michael Sumpter was identified by DNA as the man responsible for Jane Britton's murder, according to the Middlesex District Attorney. The District Attorney's office closed the case on that basis. That is a serious and significant conclusion, reached by the public officials responsible for the case, and supported by the State Police Crime Lab. But it is an identification, not a conviction. In American policing, there is a long-standing category for exactly this situation. When investigators have identified the person they believe committed a crime, have enough evidence to support that conclusion, but cannot bring that person to court because of something outside their control, such as the death of the offender, a case can be closed by what are called exceptional means. Many of the cold cases broken by DNA in recent years have ended this way, with a name announced at a press conference rather than a verdict read in a courtroom. Sumpter's other cases ended the same way. The Suffolk County District Attorney's office had named him in the deaths of Ellen Rutchick and Mary Lee McClain, but he was never tried for those killings either. Not one of the women DNA linked him to in these murders ever saw him answer for what happened to her in court. There is something deeply unsatisfying about that for the families, and something important about it for everyone else. A trial is not just a way to punish. It is a way to test. It is the place where the evidence is challenged, where the other side gets to speak, and where a community decides together what it accepts as true. When a case ends without a trial, that test never happens. What we are left with is the word of the investigators and the strength of the science. In this case, the science is strong, and the District Attorney was confident. But the honest way to tell this story is to say exactly that, and no more. There was one more thing that the announcement did, and for some people it was almost as important as naming Sumpter. It ended, at least officially, half a century of suspicion inside Harvard. For decades, the rumor had pointed at a professor, at the department, at the university. Now the District Attorney was saying that the man responsible was a stranger to all of them, a career criminal from outside that world, with no connection that anyone has ever shown to anthropology, to the dig in Iran, or to the people the rumors had followed for so long. The DNA had not just named a man. It had pointed away from everyone else. For the professor named in the rumor, the announcement came after a lifetime of whispers. He had never been charged with anything. And now the evidence pointed somewhere else entirely. For the classmates and friends who had sat the exams that January morning, it meant that the question many of them had carried for half a century, whether someone they knew had done this, finally had an answer, and the answer was no. And for Jane's family, there was the statement from her brother Boyd. He thanked Sergeant Sennott. He thanked the friends, the officials and the reporters who had refused to let the case go. He said that the DNA match might be all the family would ever get as a conclusion. And he said that learning to understand and to forgive remained a challenge. Read those words again in your mind. Not a celebration. Not a victory. An acceptance that this, a name from a laboratory, announced long after the man was dead, was as close to justice as the family was ever going to get. And an admission that, nearly fifty years later, the hardest part was still not over. The cameras were packed up. The headlines ran for a few days. The phrase solved after nearly fifty years travelled around the world. But the woman at the centre of it all was still, for most people who read those headlines, a name and a red powder. The real story of Jane Britton, the person, had still not been told.

Two years after the press conference, in November 2020, Becky Cooper published the book she had spent a decade researching. It was called We Keep the Dead Close: A Murder at Harvard and a Half Century of Silence. It had begun, years earlier, with the rumor she heard as an undergraduate, the story of a graduate student murdered by a professor and a university that kept it quiet. By the time it was finished, it had become something different. Reviewers described it as part true crime, part memoir, and part history of academia. They noted that the rumor that had started it all proves false. And many of them said that its real subject was not only the murder, but the silence around it, and the way a powerful institution and a small, competitive academic world could shape what people believed about a young woman, about the men around her, and about who gets to tell a story. National Public Radio framed its review around the idea of a cautionary tale. Others praised the way Cooper made Jane matter as a person rather than as a victim. That last point is the heart of this story. Because for fifty years, Jane Britton was mostly remembered for the way she died. A red powder. A rumor. A Harvard mystery. The facts that the District Attorney announced in 2018 answered the question of who, according to the investigators. But they did not, on their own, give Jane her life back in the public memory. That took a writer who refused to stop asking, a brother who carried her name for half a century, and friends who kept talking about her long after the newspapers had stopped. So what changed? Some things changed because of science. A swab collected in 1969 produced an answer in 2017, and that is a lesson every police department in America has learned again and again in the past twenty years. Keep the evidence. Store it properly. Do not throw away the old boxes. The technology to read them may not exist yet. One day it will. The same lesson ran through the cases on the other side of the river. In the Ellen Rutchick case, it was old microscope slides, kept for decades, that produced the profile that matched in 2009. In Jane's case, it was swabs from 1969. In both, the answer existed only because someone, long ago, had put the evidence away carefully and no one, in all the years since, had thrown it out. Some things changed because of persistence. The fight over Jane's records, the orders from the state's public records office, the pressure from a writer and from people who had known her, all of that kept the case visible at a time when it could easily have been forgotten. And some things did not change at all. There was still no trial. There was still no moment when the man the District Attorney identified had to stand up and answer for what he had done. There was no testimony, no cross-examination, no verdict. Jane's parents never heard the name. Ellen Rutchick's family and Mary Lee McClain's family received their answers the same way, from a press conference, about a man who was already dead. There are still questions that may never be answered. Did Jane and Sumpter ever cross paths before that night? No connection between them has ever been publicly shown. Where did the red ochre really come from, and how did it end up where it was found? Later accounts suggest it may have come from her own painting materials, but that has never been officially confirmed. What exactly happened in the hour after she said goodnight to her neighbors? Only one person knew, and he died in 2001. The case is closed. As of October 2026, there have been no new developments reported, and the Middlesex District Attorney's conclusion stands. Michael Sumpter was identified by DNA, according to the District Attorney's office, and he was never charged, never tried, and never convicted. And the professor whom the rumor named for decades was never charged with anything, while the DNA pointed to someone else entirely. That, too, is part of the lesson. A rumor can feel like knowledge. It can be repeated by intelligent people in famous places for half a century. It can attach itself to a real person and follow him for decades. And it can be wrong. In this case, the evidence that finally spoke did not come from gossip in a museum hallway. It came from a few pieces of cotton in an evidence locker. Now come back with us, one last time, to where this story began. It is Tuesday, January 7, 1969, just after noon. A young man climbs the stairs of an apartment building at 6 University Road, a few minutes' walk from Harvard Square. He has just finished one of the most important exams of his life. His girlfriend did not come. He knocks on her door on the fourth floor. She does not answer. Behind that door is Jane Sanders Britton. She is 23 years old. She grew up in Needham. She was born in Boston at the end of a world war. She went to Radcliffe and graduated in 1967. She chose a field where women had to fight for a place, and she took her place. She crossed the world to dig in the ground of an ancient mound in Iran. She was outspoken and independent and hard to sum up in a sentence. She painted. She went ice skating on the Common on the last night of her life. She had a glass of sherry with her neighbors after midnight on the night before her exam. She was a daughter, and a sister, and a friend, and a scholar at the very beginning of her work. She was not a legend. She was not a ritual. She was not a ghost story to be told to new students. She was a young woman who should have walked into the Peabody Museum that morning, sat down, and passed her exam. For 49 years, the people who loved her waited for someone to say what happened to her. When the answer came, it came from a laboratory, not a courtroom, and it named a man who could no longer be held to account. It was not the justice anyone would have chosen. But it was the truth, as far as the evidence can tell it, and it was enough to lift a shadow that had fallen across innocent people for half a century. If there is one thing to take from Jane's story, it is this. Look for the person, not the legend. Ask for the evidence, not the rumor. And never assume that because a case is old, it is finished. Somewhere, right now, in a box on a shelf in an evidence room, there is a swab or a slide or a scrap of fabric that is waiting, the way Jane's swabs waited, for someone to try one more time.
