From the Domingo and Sanchez scene near Goleta, DNA linked that double murder to other killings in the south. Two legends became one offender. Investigators who had spent decades in separate rooms now knew they were looking for the same person. It was a breakthrough, and it was also a new kind of torment, because it meant that a single man had done all of this and had still never been identified. Among the people who refused to forget was Bruce Harrington. His brother Keith and his sister-in-law Patty had been killed in 1980. When DNA technology became available in the mid-1990s, Bruce understood something that many lawmakers did not yet understand. A DNA profile from a crime scene is only useful if there is something to compare it to. The larger the state's database of known offenders, the better the chance of a match. So Bruce began pushing for a law. He researched it, he helped shape it, and he put his own money into it. He ran into significant opposition in Sacramento in the early 2000s. He kept going. The result was Proposition 69, which California voters passed in 2004 with more than 62 percent of the vote. It required DNA samples from everyone arrested for a felony in the state, and from some people arrested for certain misdemeanors. In 2004, Bruce said, the only realistic way to identify who had killed Keith and Patti was through DNA technology. He was right about that, although the path that finally led to the killer would turn out to be one that no lawmaker in 2004 could have imagined. It is worth understanding why that law mattered so much to a man like Bruce. A DNA profile from a crime scene is like a key found on the floor of a locked room. On its own, it opens nothing. You have to go door to door, trying it in every lock, and the database is the hallway full of doors. Before laws like Proposition 69, that hallway was short. After it, the hallway became enormous. Every felony arrest in California now added another door. Bruce Harrington did not know whether his brother's killer would ever walk through one of those doors. He only knew that without them, the key would never turn. He had lost Keith and Patty in 1980. He spent the following decades making sure that the state would at least have the tools to find the people who did such things. In the end, the man he was searching for never did walk through one of those doors. He stayed outside them. But the principle Bruce fought for, that evidence must be compared, preserved and taken seriously, is the principle that solved the case. Michelle Cruz refused to forget her sister. For years after Janelle's murder, she did not speak about it much in public. Then she began to, hoping, as she said, to spread awareness and solve the case. She spent countless hours raising attention and helping with investigation efforts. A sister who had been 17 when Janelle died became one of the most persistent voices in the case. Jennifer Carole refused to forget her father and Charlene. She became a writer and an advocate, and she would later write publicly about what the crime had done to her family and about how victims prepare to face a defendant in court. In Visalia, Elizabeth carried her father's memory and her own fear. In Sacramento, the families of Brian and Katie Maggiore waited for someone to tell them officially what they already suspected. And in Southern California, the families of the Offermans and Mannings, the Smiths, the Harringtons, the Witthuhns, the Domingos and Sanchezes, and the Cruzes lived inside the same unanswered question. And then there was a woman who had never met any of them. Her name was Michelle McNamara. She was a writer in Los Angeles, married to the comedian Patton Oswalt. She became fascinated, and then consumed, by this case. She spent years researching it, trying to see the whole of something that had always been studied in pieces. In March 2013, Los Angeles Magazine published her long article about the case, titled In the Footsteps of a Killer. In that article, she gave the man a new name, one that finally captured the size of what he had done. She called him the Golden State Killer. The article made a splash. It brought the case to readers who had never heard of the Visalia Ransacker or the East Area Rapist. And it led to a contract for a book. Michelle kept working. She kept digging through files, building timelines, trying to find the one detail everyone else had missed. She never got to finish. On April 21, 2016, Michelle McNamara died in her sleep. She was 46 years old. Her death was attributed to a combination of drugs in her system and an undiagnosed heart condition. She left behind her husband and an unfinished manuscript, along with more than 3,500 computer files of research. Patton Oswalt made a decision. He enlisted two people, the investigative journalist Billy Jensen and Paul Haynes, a researcher on the book, to comb through her notes and files and help finish it. In 2016, the same year Michelle died, the case also received a new public push from law enforcement. Investigators formally tied the Maggiore murders to the East Area Rapist. The FBI and local agencies publicized the case again, along with a reward of fifty thousand dollars for information that would identify the killer. The FBI even published the photograph of Brian and Katie Maggiore, a young couple smiling into a camera, so that a new generation would see the faces of the people this man had taken. Think about the families in that year. Elizabeth, who had been a teenager in 1975, was now a grown woman with children of her own, and she was still the only living witness to her father's murder. Michelle Cruz had now lived thirty years without her sister, far longer than she had lived with her. Debbi Domingo had become older than her mother had ever been allowed to become. And every one of them knew that the man responsible might be alive, might be living a few streets away from someone they loved, might even be reading the same newspaper stories about himself. The ledger by then was almost unbearable. Thirteen murders. Dozens of rapes. More than a hundred burglaries attributed to him in some counts. Crimes in at least five counties where people had died, and in others where people had been attacked. Forty-one years since Claude Snelling had died in his backyard in Visalia. Thirty years since Janelle Cruz had died in Irvine. Thousands of tips. Not one arrest. On February 27, 2018, Michelle McNamara's book was published. It was called I'll Be Gone in the Dark. It went to the top of the New York Times nonfiction bestseller list. Readers all over the country suddenly knew the names Brian and Katie Maggiore, Lyman and Charlene Smith, Keith and Patrice Harrington, Janelle Cruz. The book ended without an answer, because in February 2018 there was no answer. Or at least, that is what the public believed. Because by the time the book reached the bestseller list, a small group of investigators had already started down a path that no one had ever used on a case this big. They were not using a police database. They were using a website built for hobbyists. And within two months of the book's publication, that path would lead them to a name.

To understand how this case was finally solved, you have to understand what DNA can do, and what it could not do for forty years. Every human being carries a genetic code inherited half from the mother and half from the father. Crime labs use a small set of markers in that code to build what is called a forensic profile, a kind of genetic fingerprint. When a lab finds a profile at a crime scene, it uploads it to the national law enforcement database known as CODIS. Think of CODIS as a giant filing cabinet of known people, mostly people who have been convicted of crimes or, in states like California after Proposition 69, arrested for felonies. If the person who left the DNA is already in the cabinet, there is a match. If he is not, the cabinet stays silent. For the Golden State Killer, the cabinet stayed silent for decades. That told investigators something important. Whoever he was, he had apparently never been placed into the database. He had stayed outside the system that Bruce Harrington had fought so hard to expand. But in the 2010s, something new happened outside the world of police work. Millions of ordinary Americans began buying home DNA kits. They spit in a tube, mailed it off, and received a report about their ancestry. Many of them then wanted more. They wanted to find unknown cousins, trace family lines, solve their own family mysteries. Some uploaded their genetic data to a free public website called GEDmatch, where anyone could compare their DNA with everyone else who had uploaded. The crucial difference is this. CODIS can only find you. Genealogy databases can find your relatives. You do not need to have taken a test yourself. If your third cousin has uploaded, and your second cousin's daughter has uploaded, a skilled genealogist can begin to see the outline of your family, and then, branch by branch, of you. Here is how it works in plain words. Investigators take a crime scene sample and convert it into the kind of DNA data that a genealogy site can read. They upload it. The site returns a list of people who share stretches of DNA with the unknown person. These are genetic cousins, most of them distant, people who share a great-great-grandparent or an ancestor even further back. Each shared stretch of DNA is a clue to how closely related they are. Then the genealogist goes to work with traditional tools. Birth records, marriage records, obituaries, census pages, newspaper archives, other people's online family trees. The goal is to find the common ancestors that link the matching cousins to the unknown person. Once those ancestors are found, the genealogist builds the family tree forward, filling in every descendant, generation by generation, until the tree reaches the present day. Then the tree is filtered. Which men were the right age? Which ones lived in the right places at the right times? Which ones match what the physical evidence suggests? From hundreds of possible relatives, the list narrows to a few names. And then, finally, investigators need proof. Genealogy is a lead, not evidence. To confirm it, they need a fresh sample of the suspect's DNA, collected legally, and they need a standard forensic profile from that sample to match the crime scene. Today, this technique is known as investigative genetic genealogy, and it has since been used in many cold cases across the United States. But in early 2018, it had never been used to crack a case of this size and fame. The Golden State Killer investigation would become the landmark case that showed the whole country it could be done. And it also opened a debate that continues to this day. The people who uploaded their DNA to GEDmatch had done so to find family, not to help police. Was it fair to search their genetic information to find a relative they had never met? After this case, GEDmatch itself was eventually bought by a forensic genetics firm. The argument about privacy and justice has not ended. There is one more thing to understand about why this method could work here at all. It depended on something that had nothing to do with modern technology. It depended on decisions made in the 1970s and 1980s by people whose names most of us will never know. Evidence technicians, criminalists, property clerks. People who collected samples at terrible scenes, labeled them correctly, sealed them and stored them, and people who, year after year, did not throw them away when shelves got crowded and cases went cold. If the swabs from the Smith house in Ventura had been lost, or contaminated, or destroyed to make room for newer cases, no amount of genealogy in 2018 could have helped. The genealogists could only follow a thread that someone had kept intact for almost forty years. There is also a difference between the two kinds of DNA tests in this story, and it is simple enough to explain. The standard forensic profile that crime labs use, called an STR profile, looks at a small number of places in the genetic code. It is perfect for comparing one sample against another, the way you would compare two fingerprints. But it says very little about distant relatives. Genealogy sites look at a much larger set of markers, hundreds of thousands of them, which is what allows them to detect a third or fourth cousin. So the investigators needed both. The large set to find the family. The standard profile to confirm the man. One more term matters too. Discarded DNA. Every person leaves traces of their DNA on the things they touch and the things they throw away. A cup. A door handle. A tissue. Once a person abandons an object in a public place or in the trash, investigators can collect it. That was how a suspect identified by genealogy could be confirmed without his knowledge, and without anyone knocking on his door. But in 2018, a team of investigators in California made a choice. One of them was Paul Holes. He was a forensic investigator who had worked on the Golden State Killer case for decades, the kind of investigator who could not let a case go even when it had passed through generations of colleagues. He had studied the old files, the maps, the patterns. He had gone through suspect after suspect, and eliminated them. He was nearing the end of his career, and this case was the one that had never let him rest. Another was a woman named Barbara Rae-Venter. She would later describe herself, in the subtitle of her own book, as an amateur DNA sleuth. She was not a police officer and had never worked inside a crime lab. Holes reached out to her for help. Her job would be to take the crime scene DNA and turn a list of distant cousins into a single name. But first, she needed to know what kind of man she was looking for. She would be working with information that strangers had shared for an entirely different purpose, and every step had to be checked twice. And for that, she turned to the oldest source in the case, the newspapers of the 1970s.

Barbara Rae-Venter began where Michelle McNamara had begun, with the old newspaper coverage of the crimes. From those articles, she drafted a profile of the kind of man investigators should be looking for. The details in those old reports suggested that the offender might have had experience in law enforcement or in the military. It was a hypothesis, not a conclusion, but it would help her filter the family tree when the time came. The crime scene DNA profile was uploaded to GEDmatch. The search returned more than one thousand genetic cousins. A thousand people, scattered across the country, who shared some fragment of DNA with the man who had killed thirteen people. None of them knew it. None of them had done anything wrong. They had only wanted to learn where their families came from. One of those uploads had come from a relative of the killer. Rae-Venter and the team began to build. They worked backward from the matching cousins, searching for the ancestors they had in common, then forward again, descendant by descendant, generation by generation, across family lines spread over many states. It was slow, painstaking work, the kind of work that consists of thousands of small decisions, each one a fork in a road. Was this cousin related through the father's line or the mother's line? Did this marriage record belong to the same family or a different one with the same name? Little by little, the trees grew. And little by little, the possible candidates narrowed. According to an academic review of the case published later that year, the field was narrowed to a small group of men, and a tool that predicts eye color from DNA helped narrow it further. And then the tree pointed at one man, a man who fit the age, fit the places, and fit the profile drawn from the old newspapers. His name was Joseph James DeAngelo. He was 72 years old. He was born on November 8, 1945, in Bath, New York. He served in the Navy during the Vietnam War. In August 1973, a small newspaper called the Exeter Sun announced that he had joined the police department of Exeter, California, describing him as a Vietnam veteran from Bath, New York. Exeter. The little town twelve miles from Visalia. He served as a police officer there from 1973 until the middle of the decade, during the very years the Visalia Ransacker was breaking into homes and the year Claude Snelling was shot. In 1976, he joined the police department in Auburn, near Sacramento. He served there until 1979, through the years that the East Area Rapist terrorized the Sacramento suburbs. And in August 1979, he was the Auburn officer fired after he was caught shoplifting a hammer and a can of dog repellent. That small story, filed away thirty-nine years earlier, had been about him. After he left police work, he eventually became a mechanic, and he later retired. At the time investigators found him, he was living in a house in Citrus Heights, a suburb of Sacramento, in the same region where the East Area Rapist had struck. He had been there, it seemed, all along. A former police officer. A retired mechanic. An aging man in a Sacramento suburb. Jennifer Carole would later describe him in a way that captures the shock of it. From the outside, she said, he looks particularly ordinary, and yet we know on the inside there's a monster there. Set the ages side by side, and the story takes on a different shape. He was 29 years old when Claude Snelling was shot in Visalia in 1975, and he was a police officer twelve miles away. He was 32 years old when Brian and Katie Maggiore were chased down on their evening walk in 1978, and he was a police officer in Auburn. He was 40 years old when Janelle Cruz was killed in Irvine in 1986. And he was 72 years old when the family tree finally reached him. For thirty-two years after Janelle's death, he had lived the ordinary life of an ordinary man, while Michelle Cruz grew from a 17-year-old girl into a woman in her late forties, still asking why. The investigators knew they had to be careful. A family tree is not proof. If they moved too fast, if word got out, they could lose everything. And it was here that Paul Holes faced one of the strangest moments of his career. On the day before he retired, he drove to DeAngelo's house in Citrus Heights. He sat in his car outside, alone. He thought about walking up to the door and simply asking the man for a DNA sample. And then he started to have second thoughts. He was by himself. He had no radio contact with dispatch. He did not want to accidentally ruin the case his colleagues were building. He had already eliminated other suspects that he had considered better candidates than this one. But he looked at the evidence and decided there was enough that this man could very well be the Golden State Killer, and he would not risk it. Years later, he called the idea of knocking on that door foolish from an officer safety standpoint. Fortunately, he said, I didn't go up to the door. He drove away and retired. The case passed to the team that would finish it. The team placed DeAngelo under surveillance. They needed his DNA, and they needed it without alerting him. On April 18, 2018, he drove to a Hobby Lobby store in Roseville, California. While he was inside, investigators swabbed the handle of the driver's side door of his car. It was the simplest thing in the world. A man touches his own car door every day without thinking. The sample went to the lab. According to the arrest warrant later made public, it matched DNA recovered at some of the Golden State Killer's crime scenes. But the investigators wanted to be certain. Five days later, on April 23, 2018, they collected items from the trash can outside his home in Citrus Heights. Among them was a used tissue. The crime lab developed a DNA profile from it. And it matched the DNA from the evidence collected after the 1980 murder of Charlene Smith in Ventura. The warrant called the tissue the conclusive link. Think about the path of that sample. It had been collected in a house in Ventura in March 1980, the house where a 12-year-old boy found his father and his father's wife. It had been packaged, labeled, and stored. It had waited through the 1980s, the 1990s, the 2000s and the 2010s. And in April 2018, thirty-eight years later, it answered the question the Smith family had been asking all that time. The evidence remembered. It always had. For the Smith family, for the Maggiore family, for the Harringtons and the Cruzes and every other family in this story, the waiting was almost over, though none of them knew it yet. Only a small circle of investigators knew the name. The families did not know yet. The public did not know at all. They had a match, a name and an address, and one more thing to do. The next day, investigators went to his door.

On April 24, 2018, Joseph James DeAngelo was arrested at his home in Citrus Heights. He was 72 years old. The warrant was for two counts of murder. The names on it were Brian Maggiore and Katie Maggiore. Forty years and two months after a young couple went out to walk their dog in Rancho Cordova, the man accused of chasing them down was taken into custody a short drive away. The next day, April 25, the Sacramento County District Attorney, Anne Marie Schubert, stood before reporters to announce the arrest. She noted that it was National DNA Day. And she said the words that would be replayed on news programs across the country. We knew we were looking for a needle in a haystack, she said, but we also knew that needle was there. And then: We found the needle in the haystack, and it was right here in Sacramento. She also said something that summed up forty years of frustration. Despite an outpouring of thousands of tips over the years, his name had not been on investigators' radar until the week before. For survivors and families who had waited decades, the news was almost impossible to take in. Michelle Cruz would later say that when she learned of the arrest, she cried and cried for hours. Bruce Harrington said the arrest would bring closure to the anguish that we all suffered for the last 40-odd years. And in Los Angeles, Patton Oswalt recorded a short video addressed to his late wife. You did it, Michelle, he said. Speaking of the way she had pursued the case, he said simply, she kept coming at him. What happened inside the police station after the arrest would not be made public for more than two years. When it was, it came from a prosecutor in open court, and it is the only account we have. According to Sacramento County prosecutor Thien Ho, when DeAngelo was left alone in an interview room, he began talking to himself. He spoke about a figure he called Jerry, someone he said he had pushed out so that he could have a happy life. He said he had destroyed all their lives. He said he now had to pay the price. And he said three words. I did all that. The prosecutor did not present this as a sincere confession of inner torment. Ho told the court that DeAngelo had feigned feeble incoherence when detectives questioned him, even though under surveillance the day before his arrest he had appeared sharp. Ho also reminded the court of 1979, when DeAngelo had faked a heart attack after being caught shoplifting and later admitted he had just pretended to act crazy. In the weeks after the arrest, the charges grew. Prosecutors in Ventura, Orange and Santa Barbara counties filed murder charges for the killings in the south. By June 2018, he was charged with twelve murders in four counties. In June 2018, search warrants were unsealed, and the public learned for the first time about the car door handle at the Hobby Lobby in Roseville and the tissue from the trash can in Citrus Heights. Then, in August 2018, the Tulare County District Attorney, Tim Ward, charged DeAngelo with a thirteenth murder, the killing of Claude Snelling in Visalia in 1975. Ward said Snelling was believed to be the first person this man had killed. The Visalia Ransacker, the East Area Rapist and the Original Night Stalker now had a single legal name. The case then moved slowly, as enormous cases do. Thirteen murders in five counties, and decades of evidence. Then, in 2020, the world shut down. A pandemic closed courthouses across California. Victims and witnesses who had waited forty years were growing older. Prosecutors would later point to the advancing age of the victims and the witnesses. In the end, the case was resolved with a plea agreement. On June 29, 2020, the hearing was held not in a courtroom, but in a ballroom at Sacramento State University, a room large enough to seat survivors, family members, lawyers and reporters at a safe distance from one another. DeAngelo, now 74, sat in a wheelchair. Prosecutors from the counties where he had committed his crimes took turns describing the cases, one after another. And DeAngelo, who had barely said a word in court since his arrest, answered each count in a hushed, raspy voice. Guilty. I admit. Guilty. I admit. He pleaded guilty to thirteen counts of first-degree murder and thirteen counts of kidnapping for robbery. He also admitted dozens of other crimes, including rapes, that could no longer be charged because the statute of limitations had run out. In all, he admitted crimes against eighty-seven individual victims at fifty-three separate crime scenes. He also admitted the attempted kidnapping of Elizabeth Snelling, the teenage girl her father had died trying to protect. In exchange, the death penalty was taken off the table. Prosecutors cited California's moratorium on executions, the pandemic, and the advancing age of the defendant, the victims and the witnesses. That day, Patton Oswalt wrote that the most important people at the hearing were the survivors. Seven weeks later, from August 18 to August 20, 2020, the survivors spoke. For three days, in the same university ballroom, survivors and family members stood one by one and addressed the man who had changed their lives. Some spoke with fury. Some told him he could go straight to hell. Some spoke quietly. One survivor said, after 42 years, I still look over my shoulder. On August 20, the families of the murder victims spoke. Elizabeth Hupp stood up to talk about her father. She told the court about the two times he had caught a man peering through her window, and the night he died trying to save her. She talked about the guilt she had carried for years, and how becoming a parent herself had finally shown her that nothing she could have said would have stopped her father from trying to save her. He would have been a wonderful grandpa, she said. And she said what sickened and angered her most. What sickens and angers me the most, she said, is that DeAngelo was able to live a normal life with his family for all those years. She said she was grateful that he would spend the rest of his life in prison. Katie Maggiore's family spoke. Her aunt, Karen Smith, described Brian and Katie as two people who loved life and whom everybody loved in the 18 months they were married. The only thing I'm happy for, she said, is that I got to live long enough to see this happen. Katie's brother, Ken Smith, addressed DeAngelo directly. You are not worth any more of my family's time, he told him. You can't hurt anybody again. Brian's mother, Loretta Maggiore, did not come in person. Her statement was read aloud by the prosecutor. It was one sentence. She had nothing good to say about Joseph DeAngelo, so she would say nothing at all to him. Michelle Cruz spoke for Janelle. She told the court she had lost her identity when her sister was killed. She said she would no longer have to look over her shoulder, no longer have to fear that he would find her and kill her too. Now, she said, my time will no longer be concentrated on who killed my sister, but rather on finding happiness and enjoyment, which I had lost because of Joseph DeAngelo 34 years ago. Even the woman who had once been married to him submitted a statement. A headline summarized it in her words. I will never be the same person. Then came August 21, 2020. Before the judge pronounced the sentence, DeAngelo was given a chance to speak. He rose from his wheelchair. He took off his mask. And he said: I've listened to all your statements. Each one of them. And I'm truly sorry to everyone I've hurt. Sacramento County Superior Court Judge Michael Bowman spoke next. He explained that the court was not saying the defendant did not deserve the death penalty, but that given the age of the defendant and of the victims, a life sentence made more sense. The survivors, he said, had spoken clearly. The defendant deserves no mercy. The room broke into applause. And then the judge said something that honored everyone in this story who had refused to let go. Without the persistence of the survivors, the families, the citizen detectives and the investigators, he said, DeAngelo may have escaped earthly justice altogether. Judge Bowman imposed eleven consecutive terms of life in prison without the possibility of parole, plus another life term, plus eight more years. It was the maximum. Joseph James DeAngelo will die in prison. Forty-five years after a father was shot in his backyard in Visalia, the man who killed him had finally been sentenced. But a sentence is not an ending. And for the families who walked out of that ballroom, the hardest question was still waiting for them at home. What now?

For some, the answer was rest. In Florida, Janelle Cruz's mother, Diane Stein, spoke to a local television station after the sentence. Her words became the headline. She can rest in peace now. For Michelle Cruz, the answer was breath. When the arrest was announced in 2018, she had said she could finally breathe again. She had spent more than three decades with her life organized around a question. Now she could begin to organize it around something else. But she still carried one question no court could answer. Why did he stop after Jeanelle? He never said. Perhaps he never will. For Jennifer Carole, the answer was more complicated. She did not believe the plea deal delivered real justice, and she wrote publicly that in the case of Joseph DeAngelo, justice had not been served. She criticized what she described as the friction-free existence he had enjoyed since his arrest. And yet she also spoke about what she called silver linings, the survivors and families who had grown close to one another since his capture, people who had lived inside the same nightmare for decades without knowing each other's names and who now stood together. For Ken Smith, Katie's brother, the word closure was not quite right. Closure means there is an end, he said, but nothing changes. You can't bring Katie and Brian back. For Patton Oswalt, the day of the sentencing was not about the man in the wheelchair. He posted photographs of his late wife, Michelle McNamara, and wrote, the insect gets none of my headspace today. He said he was thinking instead about the victims, the survivors, the witnesses, the crusaders and the investigators. Michelle McNamara did not live to see the arrest. She did not identify the killer. But she gave him the name the world now knows him by, she gathered the victims' stories into a single book, and she put the case back in front of millions of readers in the very months when investigators were closing in. Her book, I'll Be Gone in the Dark, was published two months before the arrest. Her work outlived her. The investigators moved on too. Paul Holes, who had sat in his car outside DeAngelo's house on the day before he retired and decided not to knock, wrote a memoir published in 2022 called Unmasked. Barbara Rae-Venter, the self-described amateur DNA sleuth, published her own account in 2023, called I Know Who You Are. The technique they used, investigative genetic genealogy, has since been used in many cold cases across the country, and the questions it raised about privacy are still being argued. GEDmatch, the hobbyist website that made the breakthrough possible, was later bought by a forensic genetics firm. And Bruce Harrington's law, Proposition 69, stands as a monument to a brother who would not let go. And Joseph James DeAngelo? As of 2026, he is 80 years old. According to a 2026 report, after his sentencing he was transferred to the Protective Housing Unit at California State Prison, Corcoran, in the San Joaquin Valley, the same long valley where his crimes began in Visalia half a century ago. The unit is described as a place of isolation, with a chapel and a laundry. No new charges, no successful appeals and no change to his sentence have been reported. The plea agreement spared him the death penalty. He will spend whatever time he has left in a cell. Set the then and the now side by side one last time. In 1975, Elizabeth was a teenager lying on the ground in Visalia, thinking this is it. In 2020, she was a mother standing in a ballroom, telling a judge, and the man in the wheelchair, about the father who died trying to save her. In 1980, Jennifer Carole was 18, and her little brother was 12. By the sentencing, she was in her late fifties. In 1981, Debbi Domingo was 15. By the sentencing, she was in her fifties. In 1986, Michelle Cruz was 17. By the time she spoke in court, she was 51, and she had waited 34 years. And the man who did all of this was 29 when he killed Claude Snelling, and 80 years old today, in a prison cell in the valley where it began. The families aged in the open, in front of cameras and microphones, telling their stories again and again so that no one would forget. He aged in hiding. Only one of those two ways of growing old ended in freedom. There are things this story cannot tell you, because no one knows them. We do not know why he stopped after 1986. We do not know what he was thinking during the thirty-two years he lived quietly in a Sacramento suburb. We have his words in an interview room, as recounted by a prosecutor, and we have the few sentences he spoke in a university ballroom in 2020. Beyond that, he has given the families nothing, and this story will not invent anything for him. What we do know is what the people around him did. A daughter who survived and testified. A brother who changed a law. A sister who would not stop saying her sister's name. A writer who gave everything she had to a case and died before it was solved. An investigator who knew when not to knock on a door. A genealogist who turned a thousand strangers into a family tree. Evidence technicians whose names will never appear in a headline, who sealed a sample in Ventura in 1980 and made sure it survived. And more than a thousand distant relatives who had uploaded their DNA to learn about their ancestors, never imagining it would help end a forty-year hunt. Now go back to where this story began. Rancho Cordova, California. The evening of February 2, 1978. A young Air Force sergeant and his wife step out of their home to walk their dog. Brian is 21. Katie is 20. They have been married about a year and a half. A leash, a dog, a few blocks of sidewalk. They never made it home. But today, their names are not forgotten. Their names were the first written on the warrant that ended the hunt. Brian Maggiore, an airman. Katie Maggiore, a young wife. Claude Snelling, a teacher who ran toward danger to save his daughter. Robert Offerman, a surgeon. Debra Manning, a psychologist. Lyman Smith, a former prosecutor. Charlene Smith, whose case held the evidence that confirmed the match. Keith Harrington, a medical student about to become a doctor. Patrice Harrington, a nurse, married three months. Manuela Witthuhn, who drove home from a hospital visit and took her husband's call. Cheri Domingo, a mother. Greg Sanchez, a young man of 27. Janelle Cruz, 18 years old, the last. Thirteen people, and dozens of survivors, and the families who carried them for more than forty years. If there is a lesson here, it is this. Time is not on the side of the killer. Every year he hides, the science gets better, the databases get larger, and the people who refuse to forget grow more determined. You might think a case this old is beyond saving. It never is, as long as someone keeps the evidence, and someone keeps asking. If this story stays with you, subscribe and share it.
