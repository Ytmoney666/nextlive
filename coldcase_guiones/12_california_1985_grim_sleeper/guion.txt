In 1998, the FBI's national DNA index began operating, allowing a profile from one crime scene to be searched against profiles from offenders around the country. California built its own database too, and over the years the state widened the list of people whose DNA had to be collected. In 2004, California voters approved a ballot measure that expanded DNA collection to everyone convicted of a felony, and, in a later phase, to adults arrested for felonies. Every one of those changes added names to the database. And every name was another possible key to an old lock. But a key only works if you try it in the lock. For most of the 1990s, the swabs from the 1980s killings in South Los Angeles waited. During those years, no new murders were publicly connected to the man in the orange Pinto. Detectives who had worked the original cases moved on. The families aged. Parents who had buried daughters in their twenties were now in their fifties and sixties. Children who had lost their mothers grew into adults. Enietra Washington carried what had happened to her, and later said she had lived in fear from the night of the attack. Then the silence broke. In March 2002, a fifteen-year-old girl named Princess Berthomieux was found in an alley in Inglewood. She had been strangled. She was a child in foster care, and she was the youngest of all the victims in this story. Her death did not make national news. It did not set off public alarms about a serial killer. It was investigated, like so many others, as an individual homicide. In July 2003, Valerie McCorvey, thirty-five years old and a mother of one, was strangled. Again, there was no public link to a series. Again, a family was left with an unsolved case. Then came New Year's Day, 2007. On January 1, in an alley in South Los Angeles, Janecia Peters, twenty-five, was found inside a sealed plastic garbage bag in a trash bin. She had been shot in the back. It was the same alley where Bernita Sparks had been found twenty years earlier. Janecia was twenty-five, which means she had been a small child when the first women in this story were killed. The man who took her life had been killing, it would turn out, for longer than she had been alive to remember. This time, the science was ready. Investigators recovered DNA connected to Janecia's killer, and when that profile was run against evidence from older cases, it matched. In 2007, according to later accounts, DNA linked Janecia Peters's murder to killings stretching back to the 1980s. The man in the orange Pinto had not disappeared. He had been killing again. And eventually, prosecutors would argue with DNA and firearms evidence that the man who had shot Enietra Washington in 1988 was the same man who had killed Janecia in 2007. The LAPD responded by forming a dedicated task force. It was led by a veteran homicide detective named Dennis Kilcoyne, under the authority of the department's chief at the time, William Bratton. The detectives reviewed old files, re-examined evidence, and tried to rebuild a series of crimes that stretched across more than two decades. But there was something the task force did not do. It did not announce itself. According to later reporting and the public record, the mayor and the police chief issued no press release and no community warning. The people of South Los Angeles were not told that a serial killer who had been active since the 1980s had apparently killed again on the first day of 2007. And, according to the reporter who would eventually break the story, many families were not told that their relatives' deaths had been linked. In Janecia's case, detectives visited her mother, but did not tell her that her daughter's murder was connected to a serial killer. Think about that ledger, written out in numbers. Seven women killed between 1985 and 1988. One woman shot and left for dead in 1988. A gap of more than thirteen years in which, as far as the public knew, nothing happened. Then a fifteen-year-old in 2002, a mother of one in 2003, a twenty-five-year-old in 2007. Ten murders, one attempted murder, twenty-two years. A DNA link in 2007. A task force that worked without telling the public. And for the families, still no name. The task force had a profile. It had a DNA signature that tied crime after crime together. What it did not have was a match in any offender database. The killer's DNA was not on file anywhere. Whatever the reason, he had never been entered into the database, and so the database could not tell them who he was. It is a strange kind of knowledge. Imagine holding a perfect fingerprint and being able to say, with total certainty, that the same hand touched crime scene after crime scene across two decades, and still having no idea whose hand it is. That is what the task force had in 2007. A single genetic signature, written again and again across the city's unsolved files, belonging to a man who, as far as the system was concerned, did not exist. Year after year, more profiles were added to California's database, from new convictions and new arrests. The killer's profile could be searched against them. But as long as his own DNA was not among them, the answer to the ordinary question, who is this man, would always come back the same. No match. All they could do was wait for him to make a mistake, or for someone in the community to come forward with information. And the community, of course, could not come forward with information about a killer it had not been told existed. That is the paradox at the center of these years. Whatever the reasons were inside the department, outside it the result was that the women most at risk had no idea they were at risk. Years later, investigators would come to believe that the long silence between 1988 and 2002 was never really silence. They suspected that the man had never stopped killing, that there were victims in those years whose deaths had simply never been connected to him. The nickname that was about to be given to him, the Sleeper, rested on an assumption that may have been wrong from the start. In the summer of 2008, more than a year after the task force was formed, the secret ended. It was not ended by a press conference. It was ended by a reporter.

Her name was Christine Pelisek, and she was a crime reporter for the LA Weekly, an alternative newspaper known for long investigative stories. In August 2008, she published one that changed the course of this case. She revealed that the LAPD had a secret task force hunting a serial killer in South Los Angeles, a killer whose DNA linked murders from the 1980s to murders in the 2000s. She reported that the families of the victims had not been told that their relatives were part of a series. And she gave the killer a name. Because of the long apparent gap between the killings of the 1980s and the killing of 2002, she called him the Grim Sleeper. The name was memorable, and it was meant to be. It captured the eerie idea of a man who killed, went dormant for more than a decade, and woke up to kill again. And almost overnight, a case that had been ignored for twenty-three years became front-page news. The public reaction was immediate, and much of it was furious. How, people asked, could the police know for more than a year that a serial killer was active in South Los Angeles and not tell the people who lived there? Pelisek herself, in her reporting, criticized city politicians and the police department for their lack of urgency, of resources, and of communication with the community. Relatives of the victims were upset that no one had told them their loved ones' killings had been linked. Margaret Prescod and the coalition she had founded twenty-two years earlier saw the story as a bitter confirmation of everything they had been saying since 1986. Prescod would say that the murders had not been a priority for the police chief, the mayor, or most of the media. The city moved quickly after that. Within days of the story, the Los Angeles City Council pledged a reward of five hundred thousand dollars for information leading to the killer. Remember the twenty-five thousand dollars that activists had to protest for in 1986. Now the figure was twenty times higher. The LAPD reached out to the victims' families. Officers attended vigils. They met with church and community leaders. In May 2009, the police covered the city with billboards announcing the reward. The department's leadership began to speak about the case publicly. It was, in many ways, exactly the kind of response the community had asked for in the 1980s. It had simply come more than two decades late. And still, the billboards and the reward did not produce the name. The killer did not come forward. No one called with the right tip. The task force, led by Detective Dennis Kilcoyne, needed something else, something that did not depend on a witness or a confession. It found it in a new and controversial idea from the world of DNA. To understand it, think about how a normal DNA search works. A lab has a profile from a crime scene. It searches a database of known offenders, asking a simple question. Is this person in here? If the answer is no, the search ends. Now imagine asking a different question. Not, is this person in the database, but, is anyone in the database closely related to this person? Your DNA is a mix of what you inherited from your mother and your father. Your children inherit half of yours. Your brothers and sisters share a large part of it. That means that a parent, a child or a sibling of a criminal will carry a profile that looks noticeably similar to the criminal's own. Not identical. But close enough that a careful laboratory, using special statistical tools, can say that two profiles probably belong to members of the same family. That is called a familial search. It does not name the killer. It names a relative, and gives detectives a family to look at. From there, the work becomes old-fashioned again. Detectives have to build the family out, branch by branch, using birth records, addresses, ages and criminal histories. They have to ask which relative was the right age at the time of the crimes, which one lived in the right place, which one could have been driving through those alleys in 1985 and again in 2007. A familial hit is not proof of anything. It is a direction. And it only becomes evidence when detectives obtain a direct sample from the person they suspect and the laboratory confirms that it matches the crime scene. That last step, as you will see, is where this case became almost cinematic. Britain had already used familial searching to solve serious crimes in the early 2000s. In the United States, it was far more controversial. Critics worried about privacy, about treating innocent family members as suspects simply because a relative had been convicted, and about the fact that, because of how the criminal justice system works, some communities, including Black communities, are heavily overrepresented in offender databases. Those debates were serious, and they continue today. In April 2008, California became the first state to adopt a formal policy allowing familial searches of its offender DNA database, under strict rules. The man who adopted it was the state's attorney general at the time, Jerry Brown, who had been governor decades earlier and would be governor again. When the program finally produced results in this case, Brown said, in the face of a multitude of objections, we've crafted a balanced policy to respect the rights of citizens and at the same time deploy the most powerful DNA search technology available. One of the very first cases the new program was asked to help with was the Grim Sleeper. In 2008, the California Department of Justice ran a familial search using the killer's DNA profile. Detectives waited. The answer came back. Nothing. No close relative of the killer was in the database. It was a crushing disappointment, and Detective Kilcoyne would later describe it plainly at a news conference: the department, together with the state attorney general's office, had run a familial search for the first time in 2008, and it had been unsuccessful. But a familial search is not a one-time event. A database is a living thing. Every month, new people are convicted. Every month, new profiles are added. A search that finds nothing one year can find something the next, if the right person enters the system in the meantime. The task force understood that. And so did the scientists at the state lab. They did not give up on the Grim Sleeper. They kept the profile, and they waited for the database to grow. Somewhere in Los Angeles, a young man was about to make a mistake that had nothing to do with any murder. He was going to be arrested and convicted on a felony weapons charge. And because of California's DNA law, his genetic profile was going to be added to the state database. He did not know it, but he was about to become the most important link in the entire case. He had nothing to do with the murders. He had not seen anything. He had not heard anything. What mattered was not what he knew. It was who his father was.

In 2009, a young man named Christopher Franklin was convicted on a felony weapons charge in California. It was the kind of case that rarely makes the news. But under California law, his conviction meant that a sample of his DNA was taken and his genetic profile was entered into the state's offender database. Christopher Franklin had no connection to any of the murders in this story. He was not a suspect. He was not a witness. He was simply a man whose profile now sat in a computer in a state laboratory, alongside hundreds of thousands of others. And somewhere in that same system, the profile of the Grim Sleeper was still waiting to be searched again. On April 28, 2010, the California Department of Justice ran a second familial search in the Grim Sleeper case. This time, the computer flagged a candidate. A profile in the database was similar enough to the killer's to suggest a close family relationship. The profile belonged to Christopher Franklin. Detective Kilcoyne would later describe that moment at a news conference. Through the second search, he said, the department had learned of a man that, as we know now, turned out to be a direct relative of our suspect. It is worth slowing down here, because this is where the science ends and the detective work begins. A familial hit does not say which relative. It does not say father, brother or uncle. It says, look at this family. And so the task force began to build that family out. Christopher Franklin was a young man. The killer had been active since at least 1985. That ruled him out entirely. But his father had been an adult in 1985. His father had lived in South Los Angeles. His father, detectives found, lived in a house on 81st Street. His name was Lonnie David Franklin Junior. In the summer of 2010, he was fifty-seven years old. In August 1985, when Debra Jackson was found, he had been thirty-two. When Princess Berthomieux was killed in 2002, he was nearly fifty, and she was fifteen, a girl born after the first killings in this story had already begun. On the surface, there was little about him that seemed sinister. He had worked for the City of Los Angeles as a garbage collector in the sanitation department. And, in a detail that would stun the city once it became public, he had once worked as a garage attendant for the Los Angeles Police Department itself. To his neighbors on 81st Street, he was a friendly man who liked to work on cars in the driveway of his mint-green house and who often stopped to chat. He was a father and a familiar face. He was the kind of man people waved to. Remember that driveway. Remember the mint-green house. For twenty-five years, it had been the most ordinary sight on the street. And look at the contrast that now came into focus. Women left in alleys and in trash bins. A suspect who had worked as a city garbage collector. A series of murders the police department had failed to solve for decades. A suspect who had once worked in a garage for that same police department. None of those facts proved anything by themselves. The proof would have to come from the evidence. But it is hard to read them side by side without feeling how close the answer had been, all along, to the institutions that were supposed to find it. But a familial lead is only a lead. To arrest him, and to prove the case in court, detectives needed his DNA, directly from him, and they needed it without alerting him. So the task force put him under surveillance. They followed him, waiting for him to leave behind something that carried his genetic material. A cigarette, a cup, a fork, anything he touched with his mouth and threw away. In American law, items a person abandons in public, like trash left on a table, can generally be collected by police. Then the moment came. Lonnie Franklin went to a pizza restaurant, where he was attending a birthday party. An undercover police officer was there too, posing as a busboy. The officer's job was simple and patient. Wait, watch, and collect whatever Franklin left behind when he was done eating. A pizza crust. A napkin. A drinking glass. The undercover officer collected them, sealed them as evidence, and they went to the laboratory. Think about how small that moment was. A birthday party. A pizza crust left on a plate. A busboy clearing plates in a crowded restaurant. Nobody at that party could have imagined that a dinner table was about to close a case that had been open for a quarter of a century. In the lab, scientists extracted DNA from the items Franklin had discarded and compared it to the genetic profile from the crime scenes. It matched. The DNA on the pizza crust and the other items from that restaurant table matched the DNA linked to the murders of the women of South Los Angeles. On July 7, 2010, officers from the Los Angeles Police Department arrested Lonnie David Franklin Junior at his home on 81st Street. The man the newspapers had called the Grim Sleeper was in custody. Debra Jackson had been found almost exactly twenty-five years earlier. Enietra Washington had been waiting for nearly twenty-two years. Janecia Peters had been dead for three and a half years. The arrest was announced the next day, at a news conference that put the state's attorney general, the police chief, and the task force in front of the cameras. Jerry Brown's office described the breakthrough as an unprecedented use of familial DNA. The new police chief, Charlie Beck, who had succeeded William Bratton, said he expected that Franklin would be connected to other murders. For the families, the news arrived like a shock they had stopped expecting. A name. A face. A mugshot of a man in his late fifties. A man who had been living, all those years, in the same part of the city where their daughters had died. And for one woman in particular, the arrest meant something no one else could understand. According to the testimony she would later give, Enietra Washington had seen that face before, in the front seat of an orange Ford Pinto, on a Sunday night in November 1988. She had told him that if she died, she would haunt him. She had not died. And now the man she had warned was behind bars. When police searched Franklin's home, the garage and his cars, they found something that made the arrest feel less like an ending and more like a beginning. Photographs. A great many of them. Photographs of women. Some of them, investigators feared, might be photographs of victims no one had ever connected to him. Remember what the man in the Pinto had done to Enietra. He had taken her picture. Now detectives were looking at a collection of pictures of women, many of them unidentified, and they had no idea how many of those women were still alive. The technology had found the man. Now the old-fashioned work of finding the women was about to begin, and it would involve asking the whole city to look at the faces.

Lonnie David Franklin Junior was charged with ten counts of murder and one count of attempted murder, the attempted murder of Enietra Washington. He pleaded not guilty. And then, for the families, the waiting began again. Before the case could even reach a courtroom, the police faced the question of the photographs. On December 16, 2010, the LAPD did something unusual. It released around one hundred and eighty photographs found in Franklin's possession, showing roughly one hundred and sixty women, some of them pictured more than once. Detectives asked the public to look at the faces and tell them who these women were. The pictures had been found throughout his house, in his garage and in his cars. The fear behind the release was simple. Some of those women might be alive and safe. Some might be victims no one had ever linked to him. Some might be missing, with families who had never known where to look. In the weeks that followed, people came forward. Dozens of women in the pictures were identified, and police said many of them were ruled out as victims. According to police at the time, the release also led to new missing-person cases being opened. The photographs turned the arrest from a single answer into a long list of new questions, and many of those questions were never answered in public. Meanwhile, the criminal case moved slowly. Very slowly. Months turned into years. Pretrial hearings came and went. The families who had waited decades for an arrest now waited again for a trial. By the spring of 2016, NPR was reporting that, six years after the arrest, the families of the victims were still waiting for closure. The trial finally began with opening statements on Tuesday, February 16, 2016, in a courtroom in downtown Los Angeles, nearly six years after the arrest and more than thirty years after the first murder. The prosecutor was Deputy District Attorney Beth Silverman. She told the jury that the murders followed a pattern. She said the defendant had taken advantage of the crack cocaine epidemic in South Los Angeles, choosing women who were vulnerable because of drug dependency. And she laid out the evidence that tied him to the crimes, DNA and firearms evidence, connecting him to ten murders and the attempt on Enietra Washington's life. The defense attorney, Seymour Amster, chose to reserve his opening statement until the prosecution had finished. When the defense case began, he repeatedly challenged the DNA and ballistics evidence and suggested that other attackers might have been involved. On Thursday, February 25, 2016, Enietra Washington walked into that courtroom. She was fifty-seven years old. She had been about thirty on the night of the attack. She took the stand and told the jury, step by step, what had happened to her in November 1988. She described the orange Ford Pinto, the ride, the realization that she had been shot without having seen or heard the gun. She described fading in and out of consciousness. She described being assaulted, being photographed, being pushed out of the car and left to die. And she told the jury what she had said to her attacker in that car. If she died, she had told him, she was going to haunt him. When prosecutors showed her a photograph of Franklin from 1989, Enietra Washington said she was one hundred percent certain that he was the man who had attacked her. The defense pushed back. Amster questioned her memory, pointing to things she had told detectives in 1988, including that her attacker was taller and that his face had pock-marks, details the defense said did not fit his client. It was the hardest part of being the only survivor. Every word she said was examined, and nearly three decades of memory were put on trial along with the man she accused. On Thursday, May 5, 2016, after about two months of trial, the jury returned its verdict. Guilty. Guilty on all ten counts of murder. Guilty of the attempted murder of Enietra Washington. Lonnie Franklin was sixty-three years old. In the courtroom, families who had waited thirty years shed tears and prayed. Porter Alexander Junior, the father of Alicia Monique Alexander, called the verdict a great relief. He took my baby, he said. Then came the penalty phase, when the jury had to decide between life in prison without parole and death. Prosecutors used it to show the jury more of who Franklin was. A woman from Germany, identified in court only by her first name and last initial, Ingrid W., testified about something that had happened in 1974, when Franklin was a soldier stationed in Germany with the United States Army. She was seventeen at the time. She testified that she had been taken from a street near Stuttgart and that Franklin and two other men had sexually assaulted her. A German court had convicted all three men. Prosecutors also presented evidence about the deaths of three more women for which Franklin had never been charged. Georgia Mae Thomas, forty-three. Sharon Dismuke, twenty-one. Inez Warren, twenty-eight. And they told the jury about two women who were still missing, eighteen-year-old Ayellah Marshall and twenty-nine-year-old Rolenia Morris, whose identification cards had been found in Franklin's garage. Silverman explained that she had chosen not to add charges for those cases because it would have delayed a trial that had already taken almost six years to begin. On Monday, June 6, 2016, the jury recommended that Lonnie Franklin be sentenced to death. Outside the courtroom, Porter Alexander said, we got what we came to get, a just verdict. And then he added, I'm glad I lived to see it. It's a long time coming. His wife, Mary Alexander, put it in words that every family in this story might have used. It didn't come when I wanted, she said, but it came right on time. The formal sentencing took place on Wednesday, August 10, 2016, before Los Angeles Superior Court Judge Kathleen Kennedy. The defense asked for a new trial and argued that the death verdict should be set aside, in part because the jury had heard evidence about killings Franklin was never charged with. Amster also accused the prosecutor of misconduct, saying she had rolled her eyes in a way that mocked the defense. Silverman called the allegations unsubstantiated. The judge observed that the two lawyers seemed to have a vendetta against each other. She denied the motion. Then the families spoke. One woman, the sister of a victim, told Franklin that he had not taken just anyone off the street, but someone who was really loved. And Enietra Washington stood and faced him. She told him she had lived in fear since the attack in 1988. She said he did not deserve forgiveness, because he had shown no remorse. And she told him, to his face, you are truly a piece of evil. Judge Kennedy told Franklin that she could not think of anyone she had encountered in all her years in the criminal justice system who had committed crimes as monstrous as his. And then she said something that cut against the word everyone expected. This is not a sentence of vengeance, she said. She sentenced Lonnie David Franklin Junior to death. He was sent to death row at San Quentin State Prison. In the eyes of the law, the case was over. But for the families, and for the community that had spent thirty years asking why no one had listened, the story still had one more chapter.

Lonnie Franklin never faced execution. In California, death sentences are followed by long automatic appeals, and for years before his trial the state had carried out no executions at all. In March 2019, Governor Gavin Newsom went further and ordered a moratorium, halting executions in California while he remained in office. Franklin stayed on death row at San Quentin State Prison, on the shore of San Francisco Bay, far from the alleys of South Los Angeles. On the evening of Saturday, March 28, 2020, corrections officers found him unresponsive in his single cell at about twenty past seven. He was pronounced dead at 7:43 p.m. The state corrections department said there were no signs of trauma, and that the cause of death would be determined by an autopsy. He was sixty-seven years old. He died without ever publicly explaining what he had done, and without telling anyone how many women he had really killed. For some families, his death brought a kind of quiet. For others, it brought frustration, because it closed the last door through which an answer might have come. Margaret Prescod, who had founded the Black Coalition Fighting Back Serial Murders thirty-four years earlier, put that frustration into words. We are saddened, she said, that Lonnie Franklin died without providing information on the scores of women he killed. She and the coalition called for a task force dedicated to the missing women of South Los Angeles. Her words point to the questions that remain open today, as of October 2026. Investigators had come to believe that the Grim Sleeper never really slept, that the long gap between 1988 and 2002 was not a gap at all. Prosecutors presented evidence that he was responsible for three more deaths, those of Georgia Mae Thomas, Sharon Dismuke and Inez Warren, but he was never charged with those killings and was never convicted of them. The identification cards of two women, Ayellah Marshall and Rolenia Morris, were found in his garage, and prosecutors said they had not been able to locate either woman. Around one hundred and sixty women appeared in the photographs he kept, and while many were identified, the public has never been told the full story behind all of them. Now, step back and look at the whole case, from the first summer to the last day. The criticism of the police response comes in three layers, and each one is documented. The first is the 1980s. In September 1985, the LAPD was criticized for failing to alert South Los Angeles to a serial killer earlier. Activists had to protest week after week to push for a task force and to raise a reward from ten thousand to twenty-five thousand dollars. Margaret Prescod told the city's police commissioners that the department's refusal to publicize the slayings had further endangered the lives of poor Black women living on the Southside. The second layer is 2007 and 2008. After DNA linked Janecia Peters's murder to the older cases, the department formed a task force but did not warn the public, and families were not told their relatives' cases had been linked. The public found out from a newspaper, more than a year later. Only after that story did the city offer a reward of half a million dollars and put up billboards. The third layer is time itself. Twenty-five years from the first murder to the arrest. Nearly six more years from arrest to trial. More than thirty years in total before the families heard a jury say the word guilty. The LAPD has always denied that it was indifferent to these women. Investigators pointed to real obstacles, including the presence of more than one serial killer in the same area during the same years, and the simple fact that, for most of this story, the science that eventually solved the case did not exist. Both things can be true at once. The difficulties were real. And the women most at risk were not warned. If you want one image that holds this whole case, it is that alley in South Los Angeles. In April 1987, Bernita Sparks was found there, in a trash bin. In January 2007, Janecia Peters was found there, in a trash bin. Twenty years apart. The same alley. The same killer, according to the evidence a jury accepted. In those twenty years, the world changed completely. Cell phones arrived, the internet arrived, DNA databases arrived. What did not change, for far too long, was the sense in that neighborhood that the women being left in its alleys were not anyone's urgent priority. There is also a lesson here about the tool that finally worked. Familial DNA searching is still debated. Critics worry about privacy, and about the way offender databases overrepresent some communities, including the very community that lost these women. In this case, that tool identified the man who had escaped every other method for a quarter of a century. The debate is not finished, and it should not be. But the families in this story know exactly what they waited for, and exactly how long. Let's go back now to where this story began. It is November 20, 1988. A Sunday. A woman named Enietra Washington is walking through South Los Angeles to visit a friend. An orange Ford Pinto pulls up beside her, and a man offers her a ride. She has no idea who he is. She has no idea that seven women in her own part of the city are already dead at his hands. No public warning has reached her. She gets in. She is shot. She is assaulted. She is photographed. She is pushed out of the car and left to die. And she lives. She lives to describe him. She lives to wait twenty-two years for his arrest and more than twenty-seven years for her day in court. She lives to look him in the face and tell him exactly what he is. She told him, in that car, that if she died she would haunt him. She did not die. Instead, she testified. And her testimony helped to send him to death row. Remember them. Debra Jackson, twenty-nine. Henrietta Wright, in her thirties. Barbara Ware, twenty-three. Bernita Sparks, twenty-six. Mary Lowe, twenty-six. Lachrica Jefferson, twenty-two. Alicia Monique Alexander, eighteen. Princess Berthomieux, fifteen. Valerie McCorvey, thirty-five, a mother. Janecia Peters, twenty-five. And Enietra Washington, who survived. They were daughters. They were sisters, nieces, mothers and friends. They were women who were, in the words of one grieving sister, really loved. They were not labels. They were not statistics in a bad decade. They were people, and the world around them took far too long to say so. If there is one lesson in this story, it is that a community is safest when everyone in it is treated as worth protecting. Not only the women in wealthy neighborhoods. Not only the victims who make good headlines. Everyone. The DNA had been sitting in those envelopes since the 1980s. What it needed, all along, was for someone to decide that these women were worth the urgency. If this story stays with you, subscribe and share it.
