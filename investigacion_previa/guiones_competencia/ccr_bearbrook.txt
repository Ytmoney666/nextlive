To understand why this technique mattered, one must understand why traditional DNA had failed at Bearbrook. Conventional DNA testing works on the principle of comparison. Police collect a DNA sample from a crime scene or a victim and compare it to criminal databases such as Cotus. If there is a match in the system, there is a result. If not, deadlock. In the Bearbrook case, the DNA of the four victims had sat in Cotus for years with no matches because their families. The people who could have provided reference DNA did not know their relatives were dead and therefore never submitted samples. Genetic genealogy solved that problem by taking a detour. Instead of direct comparison, the technique takes the DNA of a victim or suspect and uploads it to public genealogy databases such as Jed Match where millions of ordinary people have voluntarily submitted their DNA to research their ancestry or connect with distant relatives. Algorithms search for matching DNA segments, not perfect matches, but partial matches that indicate two people share common ancestors. From those results, a genetic genealogologist builds a family tree backward, starting from distant relatives, and narrowing until the identity of the person sought is determined. [music] The process requires thousands of hours of work combining DNA analysis, public record research, obituaries, birth certificates, marriage certificates, and sometimes pure patience.
