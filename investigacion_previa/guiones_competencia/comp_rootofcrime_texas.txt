She never made it. Two days later, two children walking to school came across something several feet from the road in a place passing drivers could easily have missed. It was the young woman who had disappeared. She had been murdered. Investigators collected evidence from her body that they believed might one day help identify the person responsible, but it wouldn't happen that year or the next. In fact, more than four decades would pass before police finally arrested someone in connection with her murder. And when they did, it wasn't a man who had spent all those years at the center of the investigation. So who did this 23-year-old woman encounter after leaving Johnny's Inn that morning? How did investigators eventually find a man who had apparently remained outside their investigation for decades? Yolanda was 23 years old in the spring of 1981, living in Universal City with her grandmother, just northeast of San Antonio. Her family was close-knit, and her grandmother played a large role in raising her. Yolanda's cousins would later remember her as tough, a tomboy, and someone with a big heart. The public record still tells us little about her day-to-day life or where she worked, but the people who loved her remembered a young woman who'd grown up feeling safe walking around that same Universal City neighborhood, with no way of knowing what would eventually happen to her there. But we do know that on Friday night, April 10th, Yolanda went to Johnny's Inn, a bar in the Universal City area. By the time she left, it was already early Saturday morning. At around 2:30 a.m., Yolanda was seen walking away from the bar, and investigators believe she was heading home. At the time, no one knew it would become the last confirmed sighting of her alive. But somewhere after Yolanda left Johnny's Inn, no one could say what happened to her next. She did not make it home, and for the next 2 days, there was no public answer for where she had gone. On the Monday morning of April 13th, 1981, as two children made their way to school near East Aviation Drive, they noticed something several feet away from the road, in a greenbelt area that was not easily visible to passing drivers. What they found would turn Yolanda's disappearance into a murder investigation. When they went closer, they discovered the body of a young woman. Police were called to the area, and investigators soon identified her as Yolanda Herrera, the 23-year-old who had walked away from Johnny's Inn two mornings earlier, had been found dead. An autopsy determined that Yolanda's death was a homicide caused by multiple blunt force injuries. Investigators also found evidence indicating that she had been sexually assaulted. The autopsy also gave investigators something that would become crucial decades later, physical evidence including fingernail scrapings and a semen sample. In 1981, however, investigators could not use that evidence the way detectives can today since DNA profiling was not yet available to identify an unknown offender. So, police had physical evidence, but no scientific way to turn it into a name. That left investigators trying to reconstruct the gap between Yolanda leaving Johnny's Inn and being found two days later. There was no publicly identified person who could explain who she encountered after leaving the bar, how she reached the place where she was eventually found, or why she never made it home. Without a name attached to the evidence they had preserved, detectives relied on traditional police work following leads and trying to identify anyone who might have had contact with Yolanda after she left Johnny's Inn. Texas DPS now says all known suspects were eventually eliminated and the case went cold. As time passed, people became harder to locate, memories faded, and the murder remained unresolved. But, the physical evidence collected from Yolanda in 1981 remained preserved even as forensic science changed around it. Years became decades, and eventually investigators would be able to get answers from that evidence that were impossible in 1981. Even when science finally produced the DNA profile of an unknown man, investigators still had to work out who that man was. A new opportunity began in late 2020 when the case was identified as eligible for the Texas Department of Public Safety's sexual assault kit initiative. Under that program, the Texas Rangers and Universal City Police submitted evidence from Yolanda's original autopsy for advanced DNA testing and genealogy research. Then in 2021, 40 years after Yolanda's murder, investigators developed a usable male DNA profile that could finally point toward the man police had been trying to identify for decades. Investigators searched law enforcement databases hoping the profile would match DNA already on file. It didn't. After 40 years, they finally had the genetic profile of an unknown man, but still no name to attach to it. They conducted additional interviews and collected reference samples from several people who could be compared against the evidence, and one by one, those comparisons came back negative. Investigators then turned to Bode Technology. In 2021, Bode conducted a surname search that produced a lead for investigators, but the laboratory was able to extract only a small amount of DNA from the critical evidence, and it was too little for genealogy testing at the time. It was progress, but not enough to identify one person, and the amount of DNA left was extremely small. Continuing to test it could consume the sample, and if that happened without producing a name, there might be nothing left to try again. That brought investigators to one of the central questions in the case. Why stop testing when they finally seemed this close? Bode Technology recommended waiting, since with so little DNA left, another unsuccessful test could destroy Yolanda's best chance of an answer. So, they preserved what remained and they waited. The case was not abandoned. Investigators continued looking for ways to move it forward, but the technology had to improve before the risk became worth taking, and for several more years that tiny amount of evidence remained untouched. By 2025, Yolanda's murder was still being treated as an active cold case, and it was among the priority cases being worked with the Texas Rangers. More than four decades had passed since those two children found her off East Aviation Drive, and the science was finally beginning to catch up. In 2025, a laboratory specializing in difficult forensic DNA cases offered another possibility. Its name was Astrea. Investigators decided the time had finally come to risk what remained and sent the sample there for advanced DNA testing. It was the same limited evidence investigators had protected years earlier, only now the technology had changed. Despite the limited amount available, Astrea was able to develop a DNA profile suitable for forensic genetic genealogy. That allowed investigators to work through genetic relatives and family branches connected to the unknown man, and this time the search began to narrow. Genealogy could not prove who killed Yolanda or explain what happened after she left Johnny's Inn, but it could give detectives something they had never had before, a specific man to investigate. His name was Kelly Lee Brower Sr. Brower was the right generation, and investigators found that he had been living in the Universal City area around the time Yolanda was killed. Yet, his name had apparently never been at the center of the original investigation, and police later said nothing in his known criminal history had given detectives a reason to connect him to Yolanda's murder. He later moved away from Texas and eventually settled in Cottonwood, Arizona. Evidence collected during Yolanda's autopsy had finally led investigators to him, but this was still only a lead. Genealogy could point detectives toward Kelly Brower. It could not establish that the DNA preserved from Yolanda actually belonged to him. To find out whether the DNA was actually his, investigators traced Brower to Cottonwood, Arizona and began working with authorities there. They conducted surveillance around Brower's residence and confirmed that he was the adult male living there. From there, investigators were able to obtain a reference DNA sample that could finally be compared directly with the male profile developed from the evidence collected after Yolanda's murder. In October 2025, Brower's sample was submitted to Bode Technology for comparison. Then came another wait. Would Brower's DNA actually match the evidence preserved since 1981? The answer came in 2026. According to investigators, Brower's DNA matched the male profile developed from the biological evidence in Yolanda's case. Genealogy had pointed investigators toward Brower, and the direct comparison, they said, tied his DNA to the evidence. 45 years after Yolanda was killed, investigators believed they finally knew whose DNA they had. One major question remained. How did a man who had remained outside the investigation for decades fit into what happened after Yolanda walked away from Johnny's Inn? Unlike many cold cases in which the suspected offender has already died, Kelly Brower was still alive, and detectives from Texas could travel to Arizona and question him directly. When Texas investigators sat down with Brower in Arizona, they already had what they believed was the forensic link. What the DNA could not tell them was what happened after Yolanda left Johnny's Inn. So, they questioned him. According to Universal City Police, Brower confessed to killing Yolanda Herrera during the Arizona interviews. Police have not publicly released every detail of that interview, so exactly what Brower said remains unclear. The interview also helped explain why Brower's name had never surfaced before. For 45 years, there had been no publicly known connection between Brower and Yolanda's murder. And according to police, they found no indication the two knew one another before that night. That takes the story back to Johnny's Inn and to the early Saturday morning when Yolanda walked away from the bar toward home. Police say Brower had been at Johnny's Inn that night, but they found no indication that he and Yolanda knew each other beforehand, which helps explain why his name had been so difficult to find. If there was no prior relationship, there may have been no obvious connection for investigators in 1981 to follow. Yolanda left Johnny's Inn at around 2:30 that morning and was believed to be walking home. According to police, Brower happened to be at the bar and saw her. Police have not publicly provided enough detail to reconstruct what happened next or exactly how Yolanda got from that area to the place off East Aviation Drive where she was found two days later, but police have described what they believe happened as a crime of opportunity. And without modern DNA technology, the preserved biological evidence could not lead detectives to a stranger whose name they did not know. That finally answered the first question from the beginning, at least according to investigators. Who did Yolanda encounter after leaving Johnny's Inn? Police say it was Kelly Brower, a man they found no evidence she had known beforehand. The second answer had unfolded across the investigation. How did investigators find someone who had remained outside the case for decades? Through evidence preserved in 1981, a DNA profile developed 40 years later, the decision to protect the tiny amount that remained, and genetic genealogy that eventually pointed them toward Arizona. With a name, what police described as a DNA match, and an alleged confession, investigators moved to arrest him. On August 19th, 2026, 45 years after Yolanda Herrera was killed, authorities arrested Kelly Lee Brower Sr. in Cottonwood, Arizona. He was 68 years old. Brower was taken into custody on a capital murder charge connected to Yolanda's 1981 death. The arrest was the result of decades of preserved evidence, failed comparisons, advancing technology, and renewed detective work. But an arrest is not a conviction. Brower has been charged with capital murder, but he has not been convicted. The DNA match and confession described in this story are claims made publicly by investigators, and the case remains subject to the court process. Six days later, on August 25th, Universal City police publicly explained how an investigation that began in 1981 had reached this point. The case had not been solved by one sudden discovery. It had survived because investigators kept returning to it. The biological evidence had been preserved, and at one crucial moment, they knew when not to use up what remained. Before that public announcement, there was another conversation that mattered more. Investigators had to tell Yolonda's family what they believed they had finally uncovered.
